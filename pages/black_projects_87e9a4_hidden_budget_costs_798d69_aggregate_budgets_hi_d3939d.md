## Introduction

Aggregate classified budgets answer one question while leaving another unresolved. They can show how much a government spends on secret activities overall, yet they cannot reveal which individual programme is consuming more money than planned, falling behind schedule or requiring repeated emergency funding. That distinction is central to understanding cost-control risks in [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}). [Public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}) of a single classified spending total provides a measure of transparency about the scale of secret expenditure, but it also bundles successful and troubled programmes together, making overruns difficult for anyone outside the authorised oversight system to identify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4078-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceDNI Releases FY 2026 Budget Request Figure for the National Intelligence Program &#124; Office of the Directo...</span></span></span>

The result is not that overruns become invisible to everyone. Rather, they become visible only to a relatively small group of cleared officials with access to programme-level information. Everyone else—including most legislators, journalists, researchers, competitors and taxpayers—sees only the combined total, not the individual programmes whose costs may be rising sharply.

## What aggregate totals show and conceal

An aggregate classified budget, sometimes called a "topline" figure, communicates the overall scale of spending without identifying where the money goes. In the United States, for example, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence publicly releases the annual National Intelligence Program budget request as a single figure while withholding the detailed allocation among individual classified programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4078-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceDNI Releases FY 2026 Budget Request Figure for the National Intelligence Program &#124; Office of the Directo...</span></span></span>

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether total classified spending is increasing or decreasing.
* Broad changes in national priorities over time.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

## How programme overruns disappear inside broad budget lines

The mechanism is straightforward but powerful. Individual programme performance becomes statistically absorbed into a much larger financial total.

Instead of a public budget containing identifiable procurement lines, classified appropriations are frequently embedded within broader accounts or protected through classified annexes. Cost growth therefore changes an internal classified ledger rather than an openly identifiable programme line. GAO reviews of US special access acquisition programmes found that the Department of Defense generally used the same budgeting processes as conventional programmes but concealed programme identities and funding within unclassified budget documents for security reasons.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

Several consequences follow.

**Large portfolios dilute individual failures.** If dozens of classified programmes are financed through related appropriations, a substantial overrun in one project may represent only a modest percentage increase in the combined account.

**Internal transfers become difficult to observe publicly.** Programme managers may shift resources between classified efforts through mechanisms authorised inside classified budget processes. Public observers can detect higher overall spending but cannot determine whether money has been redirected to compensate for an underperforming programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

**Historical comparisons become less informative.** A stable aggregate budget does not necessarily indicate stable programme costs. Rising costs in one project may be offset by reductions elsewhere, masking significant management problems within individual programmes.

**Contractor performance becomes harder to evaluate.** Public acquisition programmes often permit analysts to compare estimated and actual costs by contractor and platform. Comparable comparisons become much more difficult when contract identities remain classified.

The effect resembles viewing only the total balance of an investment fund while being unable to inspect the individual holdings. The total may appear stable even though one component has lost substantial value and another has gained.

## Why aggregate figures are a weak indicator of programme health

Budget totals are often interpreted as indicators of government priorities, but they are poor indicators of programme execution.

A classified budget can increase because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* new missions have been authorised;
* production has expanded successfully;
* inflation has raised procurement costs;
* technology has proved more complex than expected;
* schedules have slipped;
* programmes have required redesign;
* previously underestimated costs have been recognised.

</div>

From the outside, these very different explanations can produce nearly identical topline figures.

Likewise, an apparently flat classified budget may conceal extensive internal restructuring. A cancelled project may free resources that are immediately redirected to another programme experiencing major cost growth, leaving the aggregate unchanged despite significant management issues.

This ambiguity makes it difficult for external researchers to distinguish strategic investment from programme distress using aggregate financial data alone.

## What cleared reviewers need to see

Aggregate secrecy does not eliminate oversight; it changes who can perform it.

US law requires reporting on special access programmes to designated congressional oversight committees, including programme descriptions, milestone information and historical costs for authorised reviewers. These classified reporting requirements exist precisely because aggregate public figures are insufficient for meaningful oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title50-section3348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

Effective cost control therefore depends on cleared reviewers having access to information that the public cannot see, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* original cost baselines;
* revised cost estimates;
* schedule performance against milestones;
* explanations for requirement changes;
* earned-value or equivalent performance metrics;
* contractor performance assessments;
* justification for additional appropriations.

</div>

Without programme-level data, reviewers cannot distinguish between justified cost growth—such as responding to new operational requirements—and avoidable overruns caused by unrealistic planning or technical failure.

GAO's reviews of special access acquisition programmes concluded that oversight had strengthened through greater centralisation and increased attention from inspectors general and congressional oversight, reflecting recognition that secrecy requires stronger internal review rather than weaker review. The same studies also found no evidence that classified acquisition programmes were inherently more prone to cost or schedule problems than comparable non-special-access programmes, underscoring that the principal issue is visibility rather than inevitable mismanagement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

## The central accountability trade-off

Aggregate classified budgets represent a compromise between operational security and public [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}).

Publishing programme-level expenditures could expose development priorities, production rates or strategic capabilities to foreign intelligence services. At the same time, publishing only an aggregate figure limits the ability of outside observers to identify cost growth before it becomes substantial.

The accountability challenge is therefore not solved by releasing larger topline numbers. What matters is whether enough independent, security-cleared reviewers have timely access to the underlying programme data, sufficient authority to question revised estimates and the institutional independence to challenge optimistic assumptions before overruns become embedded within the broader classified budget.
