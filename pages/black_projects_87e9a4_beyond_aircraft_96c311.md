## Introduction

[Black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) are not just aircraft. Governments have used the same concealed budgets, [cover stories]({{ 'cover-stories/' | relative_url }}), restricted test sites and compartmented access to develop nuclear weapons, experimental missile propulsion, naval stealth, space-based reconnaissance, seabed listening networks and extraordinary intelligence machinery. In these programmes, secrecy may protect not only the existence of a device but the physics, sensor sensitivity, operating frequency, materials, software or collection method that gives it strategic value.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_beyond_aircraft_96c311-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Beyond Aircraft illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_beyond_aircraft_96c311-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_beyond_aircraft_96c311-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

The historical record also shows that secret research produces mixed results. The Manhattan Project created operational weapons through vast hidden industrial mobilisation. CORONA and early electronic-intelligence satellites transformed surveillance. Sea Shadow generated useful naval-stealth knowledge without becoming a warship. [Project Pluto]({{ 'project-pluto/' | relative_url }}) proved that a compact nuclear ramjet could operate, but never produced a deployable missile. Project AZORIAN built unprecedented deep-ocean recovery machinery yet recovered only part of its target. These cases make black projects easier to understand: they are portfolios of protected experiments, systems and supporting technologies, not a warehouse of uniformly successful “superweapons”.

## Weapons work behind the curtain

The Manhattan Project remains the largest and most consequential example of secret weapons research beyond aviation. It combined scientific laboratories with enormous industrial plants for uranium enrichment at Oak Ridge, plutonium production at Hanford and weapon design at Los Alamos. Its achievement depended not on hiding a single prototype in one secure building, but on concealing the purpose and interdependence of a dispersed national enterprise.

Compartmentalisation made that possible. Personnel were generally given the information needed for their immediate assignments rather than a complete picture of the programme. Department of Energy histories describe compartmentalisation as a central security principle, restricting exchanges between sites and technical groups even when closer communication might have improved efficiency. The result was a system in which thousands of workers could operate specialised machinery, manufacture components or study materials without necessarily knowing how their task contributed to an atomic bomb.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2013/04/f0/Gosling%20and%20Fehner%20-%20Closing%20the%20Circle%20%28complete%29.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.govClosing the Circle:Decisions made during the Manhattan Project determined the initial size and shape...</span></span></span>

This approach established a pattern followed by later black projects. Propulsion, guidance, sensors, warheads, materials and testing can be divided among different laboratories and contractors, while only a small number of programme leaders understand the complete design and intended mission. Such separation reduces the damage that one disclosure or espionage penetration can cause. It can also create technical costs: [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) may duplicate work, overlook interactions between components or struggle to challenge assumptions controlled by another compartment.

Project Pluto shows how this model was applied to a radically different weapon concept. Begun in the mid-1950s as a joint effort involving the US Atomic Energy Commission and Air Force, Pluto sought to develop a nuclear ramjet for a low-flying, supersonic cruise missile. Rather than burning ordinary fuel, the engine would direct incoming air through a compact reactor, heating it before expelling it to produce thrust. The proposed missile was intended to travel enormous distances at high speed and low altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.llnl.gov/purpose/history/1960s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: llnl.gov">[Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">llnl.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory Our History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The first model, the Tory II-A...Read more...</span></span></span>

Livermore researchers built a series of Tory reactor prototypes at a remote Nevada test installation. Tory II-A demonstrated the basic principle, while the more advanced Tory II-C was successfully operated in 1964 at power levels relevant to a flight engine. The programme therefore moved well beyond paper studies: it produced a functioning nuclear ramjet and the specialised facilities needed to test it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.llnl.gov/sites/www/files/1955.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: llnl.gov">[Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">llnl.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lawrence Livermore National LaboratoryFlying and Terrestrial Nuclear ReactorsAn awesome undertaking, Project Pluto entailed the design an...</span></span></span>

Yet technical achievement did not make Pluto a useful operational system. A reactor-powered missile presented severe problems involving flight testing, radioactive contamination, control, materials and political acceptability. The rise of intercontinental ballistic missiles also weakened the strategic argument for an extraordinarily complex cruise missile. Pluto was cancelled in 1964, illustrating a central feature of black-project research: secrecy can create space for high-risk experimentation, but it cannot eliminate strategic obsolescence or engineering trade-offs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.llnl.gov/purpose/history/1960s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: llnl.gov">[Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">llnl.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory Our History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The first model, the Tory II-A...Read more...</span></span></span>

Directed-energy weapons provide a modern comparison. High-energy lasers and high-power microwave systems use concentrated electromagnetic energy to damage, disable or disrupt targets. Their proposed roles include defeating drones, missiles, sensors and electronic systems. Some demonstrators have successfully engaged unmanned aircraft in live-fire trials, but the US Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office has found persistent difficulty moving such prototypes into dependable acquisition programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105868" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 23 105868</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directed Energy Weapons: DOD Should Focus...17 Apr 2023 — To support transition, the Army developed a detailed plan describing sch...</span></span></span>

The public existence of these technologies does not mean that all important work is open. Operationally decisive information—effective range, beam quality, atmospheric limitations, power supply, cooling demand, target vulnerability, engagement time and resistance to countermeasures—may remain classified. A weapon family can therefore be acknowledged while its most valuable experimental results sit inside restricted compartments. This is one reason the boundary between an openly discussed research programme and a black project is often a matter of degree rather than a simple yes-or-no distinction.

The directed-energy record also warns against judging secret weapons by dramatic demonstrations alone. GAO found that transition problems include immature technology, uncertain requirements, funding discontinuities and weak planning between research organisations and future acquisition managers. A prototype may destroy a drone under controlled conditions without yet being suitable for sustained operations aboard a ship or vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105868" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 23 105868</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Directed Energy Weapons: DOD Should Focus...17 Apr 2023 — To support transition, the Army developed a detailed plan describing sch...</span></span></span>

## Surveillance systems that changed strategy

Some of the most important black projects were designed to collect information rather than deliver explosives. Surveillance systems could alter the strategic balance by revealing missile fields, radar networks, submarine movements and weapons tests. Their secrecy was essential because a target that understood the collection method could camouflage facilities, alter communications, reduce detectable emissions or schedule activity around known surveillance windows.

CORONA was the first successful American photographic-reconnaissance satellite programme. President Dwight Eisenhower approved it in 1958, while the public was told that associated launches belonged to the Air Force’s Discoverer research programme. Its true purpose was to photograph denied areas, especially in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, and return exposed film to Earth in recoverable capsules.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/corona-declassified/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">corona declassified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">corona declassified</span></span></span>

After repeated early failures, CORONA achieved its first successful recovery in August 1960. It eventually collected more than 800,000 images, with individual photographs covering large strips of territory. The imagery gave American analysts broad views of missile sites, airfields, industrial complexes and military infrastructure that could not safely be obtained through routine overflight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/corona-declassified/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">corona declassified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">corona declassified</span></span></span>

Its strategic value went beyond finding individual installations. Satellite photography helped analysts estimate the scale of Soviet weapons deployments and reduced reliance on speculation about supposed missile gaps. When CORONA and related imagery were [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) in 1995, US officials argued that such coverage had increased confidence in negotiating and verifying arms-control agreements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/corona-declassified/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">corona declassified</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">corona declassified</span></span></span>

The programme’s secrecy protected an entire chain of capability: camera performance, orbit selection, film capacity, capsule recovery, launch schedules, interpretation methods and target priorities. Revealing any one element could have helped the Soviet Union predict coverage or disguise sensitive activity. CORONA therefore demonstrates why a surveillance black project is rarely just a hidden machine. It is a system linking spacecraft, ground stations, recovery forces, processing facilities, analysts and restricted tasking procedures.

Electronic-intelligence satellites extended this principle from images to emissions. GRAB, first launched in 1960, collected signals from Soviet air-defence radars while carrying a public scientific mission. POPPY followed from 1962 and expanded collection to radar emissions associated with land-based systems and Soviet naval vessels. The programmes helped US analysts locate emitters and understand the characteristics and geographical structure of opposing radar networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Portals/135/documents/history/csnr/programs/docs/prog-hist-03.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">prog hist 03</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">prog hist 03</span></span></span>

Later National Reconnaissance Office declassification has revealed that signals-intelligence sensors also flew on vehicles associated with photographic reconnaissance. These payloads collected information about Soviet satellite-tracking radars and radar coverage across denied territory. The surviving official histories emphasise that reconnaissance missions were developed as connected portfolios rather than isolated satellites, although substantial details of higher-orbit and later systems remain redacted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/foia-home/foia-declassified-historically-significant-documents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nro.gov.</span></span></span>

The recent declassification of JUMPSEAT adds another layer to this history. The National Reconnaissance Office disclosed in January 2026 that JUMPSEAT was developed to place signals-collection equipment in highly elliptical orbit, supplementing lower-orbit systems such as GRAB and POPPY. Its purpose included monitoring the development of adversary offensive and defensive weapon systems from an orbital position that offered different persistence and viewing geometry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/news-media-featured-stories/news-media-archive/News-Article/Article/4392223/declassifying-jumpseat-an-american-pioneer-in-space/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[National Reconnaissance Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">declassifying jumpseat an american pioneer in space</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">declassifying jumpseat an american pioneer in space</span></span></span>

Underwater surveillance followed similar logic. The Sound Surveillance System, or SOSUS, used arrays of passive hydrophones on the seabed, connected by undersea cables to shore stations. By exploiting the ocean’s deep sound channel, in which low-frequency noise can travel long distances, the system could detect and help track submarines far from the sensors themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dosits.org/galleries/technology-gallery/locating-objects-by-listening-to-their-sounds/sound-surveillance-system-sosus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dosits.org">[Discovery of Sound in the Sea]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dosits.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sound surveillance system sosus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sound surveillance system sosus</span></span></span>

SOSUS was concealed behind the unclassified installation name Project Caesar and cover descriptions of shore facilities as oceanographic stations. Its effectiveness depended on protecting array locations, sensitivity, processing methods and the acoustic signatures used to distinguish submarines from ordinary ocean noise. The system proved especially valuable against comparatively noisy Cold War submarines and later acquired legitimate scientific uses, including the study of underwater volcanic activity and whale calls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dosits.org/people-and-sound/history-of-underwater-acoustics/the-cold-war-history-of-the-sound-surveillance-system-sosus/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dosits.org">[dosits.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dosits.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the cold war history of the sound surveillance system sosus</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the cold war history of the sound surveillance system sosus</span></span></span>

Together, CORONA, GRAB, POPPY, JUMPSEAT and SOSUS show that secret surveillance research can matter as much as weapon development. Better knowledge of an adversary’s forces can reduce the risk of [surprise]({{ 'surprise/' | relative_url }}), improve targeting and expose whether political claims match observable deployments. In some circumstances, a hidden sensor network may influence deterrence more profoundly than a new missile.

## Naval stealth beyond the prototype

Sea Shadow, designated IX-529, offers one of the clearest examples of stealth technology moving beyond aircraft. Developed by DARPA, the US Navy and Lockheed, the experimental vessel tested whether shaping and materials could substantially reduce a surface ship’s [radar signature]({{ 'radar-signature/' | relative_url }}). It also explored automation, reduced crewing and a small-waterplane-area twin-hull configuration intended to improve stability in rough seas.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.darpa.mil/about/innovation-timeline/sea-shadow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: darpa.mil">[darpa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">darpa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sea shadow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sea shadow</span></span></span>

Physical concealment was part of the project. Sea Shadow was constructed and housed inside the Hughes Mining Barge, a covered floating structure originally associated with Project AZORIAN. This arrangement allowed work to occur away from ordinary observation. Completed in 1984, the vessel underwent night trials in 1985 and 1986 and was not publicly revealed until 1993.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.darpa.mil/about/innovation-timeline/sea-shadow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: darpa.mil">[darpa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">darpa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sea shadow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sea shadow</span></span></span>

The programme also demonstrates that secret testing can expose mundane mistakes as well as advanced discoveries. DARPA recounts that Sea Shadow initially produced an unexpectedly large wake because its propellers had been installed backwards. The error was corrected, but the vessel never proceeded into operational service.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.darpa.mil/about/innovation-timeline/sea-shadow" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: darpa.mil">[darpa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">darpa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">sea shadow</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">sea shadow</span></span></span>

That outcome did not make the project pointless. Sea Shadow provided empirical data about radar-cross-section reduction at sea, the interaction between stealth shaping and ship design, automation and the handling qualities of its unusual hull. DARPA states that lessons from the project were reflected in later naval systems, including aspects of the Zumwalt-class destroyer. It also notes that low-observable techniques have since been applied across missiles, helicopters, ships and ground vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.darpa.mil/news/features/stealth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: darpa.mil">[darpa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">darpa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on darpa.mil.</span></span></span>

Sea Shadow therefore represents a recurring black-project model: the demonstrator as a question-answering machine. Its purpose was not necessarily to become the next warship. It existed to determine whether specific technical ideas worked at useful scale, discover unforeseen problems and transfer successful elements into later designs.

## Special-access engineering and covert machinery

Project AZORIAN illustrates how a black project can create an extraordinary technological system without being a conventional weapon or surveillance platform. After the Soviet ballistic-missile submarine K-129 sank in the Pacific in 1968, the CIA developed a plan to recover part of the wreck from a depth of more than three miles. The public cover story held that the Hughes Glomar Explorer was a commercial vessel designed to mine manganese nodules from the seabed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0005301269.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DOC 0005301269</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOC 0005301269</span></span></span>

In reality, the ship incorporated a large central docking well, heavy-lift equipment, long strings of supporting pipe and a massive capture vehicle intended to seize and raise the submarine. The recovery mechanism was constructed under cover and loaded from below using a submerged barge so that overhead reconnaissance could not easily observe it. This was special-access technology in a particularly literal form: an entire ship and industrial cover enterprise were designed around concealing one unprecedented engineering task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

The recovery attempt began in July 1974. During the lift, much of the submarine section broke away and fell back to the seabed, although the remaining portion was brought into the ship’s enclosed well. Six Soviet sailors’ bodies were recovered and buried at sea with military honours. The CIA later characterised the operation as a major intelligence and engineering achievement despite its failure to retrieve the complete target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

AZORIAN is significant because it joined cover, private industry, marine engineering, intelligence requirements and information control into one compartmented system. It also produced a lasting legal and administrative legacy. When journalists sought records, the US government adopted the formula that it could “neither confirm nor deny” the requested information—the response now commonly called a Glomar response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/The-Glomar-Explorer.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Glomar Explorer in Film and Print</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Glomar Explorer in Film and Print</span></span></span>

The project’s exposure further shows that operational secrecy is vulnerable to events far from the central technology. Stolen documents from a Hughes office helped attract investigative attention, and press reporting in 1975 made a second recovery mission impracticable. A black project can therefore fail because its cover structure breaks, even when the engineering system itself remains functional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/museum/exhibit/project-azorian/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cia.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why secrecy helps—and what it hides

Across these cases, secrecy served several distinct purposes. It prevented adversaries from developing countermeasures; concealed intelligence targets and collection methods; allowed dangerous or politically sensitive experiments to proceed; protected industrial suppliers; and preserved ambiguity about what a system could actually do.

But the same controls can obscure failure. Project Pluto’s successful reactor tests did not overcome its operational liabilities. Sea Shadow’s technological lessons did not justify an operational ship. AZORIAN’s remarkable recovery machinery retrieved only part of the submarine. Directed-energy demonstrators continue to face the difficult transition from controlled trials to maintainable field systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.llnl.gov/purpose/history/1960s" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: llnl.gov">[llnl.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">llnl.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory Our History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The first model, the Tory II-A...Read more...</span></span></span>

Compartmentalisation can also narrow scrutiny. When very few officials, engineers or legislators can see the complete programme, independent cost estimates and technical criticism become harder to organise. Problems that cross compartment boundaries may be missed, while classified performance claims are difficult for outsiders to assess. Secrecy may be necessary to protect a capability, but it also makes it harder to distinguish a genuine breakthrough from an expensive prototype whose limitations are hidden alongside its strengths.

The historical comparison is therefore more useful than speculation about undisclosed wonder weapons. Confirmed black projects reveal a recognisable portfolio: a few operational transformations, several enabling surveillance systems, many specialised prototypes and some ambitious dead ends. Their most important products are not always the visible machines. They may be manufacturing methods, sensor-processing techniques, stealth measurements, recovery equipment, experimental data or organisational practices transferred quietly into later systems.

Black projects beyond aircraft are best understood as protected ecosystems of research. The weapon, ship or satellite is only the most conspicuous element. Around it sit laboratories, contractors, test ranges, cover organisations, data-processing centres and access controls designed to prevent any adversary—or often most of the sponsoring government—from seeing the whole picture.

    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
</script>
