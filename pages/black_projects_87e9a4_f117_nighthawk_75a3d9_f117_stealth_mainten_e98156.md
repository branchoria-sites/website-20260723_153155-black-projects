## Introduction

The [F-117]({{ 'f-117/' | relative_url }}) Nighthawk is often remembered for its faceted shape and secret development, but its low observability depended just as much on painstaking maintenance after every flight. The aircraft's stealth was not a permanent property built into the airframe; it was something that had to be preserved continuously through inspection, repair and strict quality control. Small imperfections that would be unremarkable on a conventional aircraft could undermine the carefully managed [radar signature]({{ 'radar-signature/' | relative_url }}) of the Nighthawk. For that reason, the technicians responsible for its outer skin became an essential part of the weapon system, and many aspects of their work remained classified long after the aircraft itself became public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afmc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/154860/airmen-industry-say-goodbye-to-stealth-fighter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afmc.af.mil">[af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afmc.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2008</span></span></span>

## Why small surface flaws could matter


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Stealth aircraft are designed to control how radar energy interacts with their surfaces. On the F-117, this depended on an exact combination of geometric shaping, radar-absorbent materials (RAM), sealed joints and carefully aligned panel edges. If that carefully controlled surface was disturbed, radar reflections could increase in ways that mattered operationally.

</div>

The aircraft's designers therefore treated apparently minor details as critical engineering features. Raised fastener heads, damaged sealant, chipped coatings, gaps around removable panels or poorly repaired edges could all create additional radar reflections. Rather than thinking of the aircraft's skin as ordinary metal covered by paint, maintainers treated it as a precision electromagnetic surface whose geometry had to remain within tight tolerances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/the-f-117-and-the-future-of-stealth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[airandspaceforces.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on airandspaceforces.com.</span></span></span>

Unlike conventional maintenance, where cosmetic blemishes might wait for scheduled servicing, low-observable maintenance demanded immediate attention whenever a defect could affect signature performance. This meant inspections were driven not only by structural integrity or flight safety but also by preserving the aircraft's ability to remain difficult to detect.

## How coatings and seams became mission-critical

The F-117's radar-absorbent material was only one element of its stealth system, but it required exceptional care. Much of the aircraft's exterior was covered by specialised coatings and sealants that absorbed or reduced reflected radar energy while blending smoothly into the faceted airframe. These materials required controlled application, careful curing and repeated inspection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afmc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/154860/airmen-industry-say-goodbye-to-stealth-fighter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afmc.af.mil">[afmc.af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afmc.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2008</span></span></span>

Early in the programme, maintaining these coatings proved labour-intensive. Former programme personnel described technicians painstakingly filling joints, smoothing sealant across panel lines and covering fastener heads using specialised compounds before allowing the materials to cure. The informal nickname "buttering" reflected how the work resembled carefully spreading a smooth layer across every interruption in the aircraft's surface.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0601stealth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine Two Decades of Stealth &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineTwo Decades of Stealth &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineJune 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2001</span></span></span>

Engineers also tried to reduce future maintenance through design. According to Lockheed accounts published after the programme became public, many service access panels were concentrated inside the landing gear bays and [weapons bays]({{ 'weapons-bays/' | relative_url }}) so that routine maintenance could occur without repeatedly disturbing the aircraft's outer stealth surfaces. The underside panel geometry was also deliberately standardised so that unavoidable panel lines aligned with the aircraft's broader radar-management strategy rather than creating random reflections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/1192stealth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on airandspaceforces.com.</span></span></span>

These design choices illustrate an important point: maintainability became part of stealth engineering. Designers understood that every time technicians opened a panel, removed a coating or replaced a fastener, they risked degrading the aircraft's radar performance unless the repair process restored the surface precisely.

## The maintainers became part of the weapon system

The Air Force has repeatedly acknowledged that the F-117's operational success depended on the people who maintained its low-observable characteristics. During the aircraft's retirement, commanders noted that the Nighthawk carried no onboard radar and few conventional defensive systems, relying instead on its reduced detectability. As one senior commander put it, "We depend exclusively on the stealth provided by our maintainers."<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afmc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/154860/airmen-industry-say-goodbye-to-stealth-fighter" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[afmc.af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airmen, industry say goodbye to stealth fighter &gt; Air Force Materiel Command &gt; Article Display</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2008</span></span></span>

This responsibility created highly specialised maintenance careers. Personnel responsible for repairing radar-absorbent materials were known as Material Application and Repair Specialists (MARS), a classified speciality that eventually earned the nickname "Martians" among those who served with the programme. Their expertise combined structural repair, materials science, precision finishing and classified procedures unlike those found elsewhere in the Air Force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/News/Features/Article/1499939/remembering-the-f-117-nighthawk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Air Force Remembering the F-117 Nighthawk &gt; Air Force &gt; Features</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Air Force Remembering the F-117 Nighthawk &gt; Air Force &gt; Features</span></span></span>

In practical terms, these technicians influenced combat readiness as directly as pilots. A mission could not proceed until maintenance [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) were satisfied that the aircraft's low-observable characteristics had been restored after inspection and repair.

## Why maintenance practices stayed secret too

The secrecy surrounding the F-117 extended far beyond its existence or mission planning. Exactly how its coatings were formulated, how they were repaired, what tolerances were acceptable and how inspectors evaluated repairs all represented valuable technical knowledge.

Knowing the composition of radar-absorbent materials alone would not have been enough for a foreign intelligence service to duplicate the aircraft, but understanding maintenance procedures could reveal the design's vulnerabilities, expected service life and operational limitations. Observing which areas required frequent repair might also reveal which parts of the aircraft contributed most heavily to signature control.

For that reason, many details of low-observable maintenance remained classified even after the F-117 was publicly acknowledged in 1988. Decades later, museum aircraft have still been delivered without their original radar-absorbent coatings or with sensitive materials removed, demonstrating that aspects of the low-observable system continue to be protected even after retirement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://airandspace.si.edu/air-and-space-quarterly/issue-19/military-aircraft-camouflage" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspace.si.edu">[National Air and Space Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspace.si.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Air and Space MuseumThe Art of Concealing an Airplane From Your Enemies &#124; National Air and Space MuseumJune 16, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 16, 2026</span></span></span>

## Lessons learned changed later stealth aircraft

Experience operating the F-117 showed that first-generation stealth could impose a heavy maintenance burden. Although some early concerns proved less severe than initially feared, engineers spent years improving both materials and maintenance procedures.

By the early 2000s, programme officials described significant advances. Radar-absorbent coatings increasingly benefited from automated application techniques adapted from automotive manufacturing, while redesigned quick-access strips reduced the need for repeated sealing and lengthy curing around frequently opened panels. These changes shortened maintenance times without abandoning the underlying principle that the aircraft's outer surface had to remain exceptionally smooth.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0601stealth/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine Two Decades of Stealth &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineTwo Decades of Stealth &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces MagazineJune 1, 2001...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2001</span></span></span>

The Air Force has also stated that lessons learned from operating and maintaining the F-117 directly influenced subsequent stealth aircraft. Newer designs retained the importance of precise surface control but incorporated improved materials, more durable coatings and maintenance-friendly engineering from the outset, reducing some of the labour that had characterised the pioneering Nighthawk fleet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afmc.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/154860/airmen-industry-say-goodbye-to-stealth-fighter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afmc.af.mil">[afmc.af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afmc.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2008...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2008</span></span></span>

## The fragile skin as a symbol of black projects

The F-117 demonstrates that secrecy was embedded not only in classified documents or hidden test sites but also in everyday technical work. Its stealth depended upon hundreds of small maintenance actions that were invisible to the public yet indispensable to combat effectiveness.

This makes the aircraft an unusually revealing example of a Cold War black project. The engineering breakthrough was not simply inventing an aircraft with a small radar signature; it was sustaining that signature over years of operational service. The maintainers who inspected seams, restored coatings, repaired tiny defects and guarded specialised techniques ensured that the aircraft remained as difficult to detect after hundreds of sorties as it had been when it first left the factory. In that sense, the fragile skin of the F-117 was both a technological achievement and one of the most closely guarded secrets in the entire programme.
