## Introduction

One of the least understood features of the United States' [black budget]({{ 'black-budget/' | relative_url }}) is that secret spending does not sit neatly inside a single account. Classified programmes can be funded through the National Intelligence Program (NIP), the Military Intelligence Program (MIP), or broader Department of Defense appropriations, with some capabilities drawing on more than one funding stream. As a result, apparent changes in intelligence spending do not always represent genuine expansion or contraction. Sometimes they reflect a shift in which budget category carries the cost rather than a change in the underlying activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_black_budget_dea93a_intelligence_budget_ac5a94-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Budget Games illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_black_budget_dea93a_intelligence_budget_ac5a94-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_black_budget_dea93a_intelligence_budget_ac5a94-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This creates what budget analysts often call "budget games": not necessarily illegal manipulation, but accounting choices and organisational boundaries that complicate attempts to understand long-term trends. Public top-line figures can therefore give a misleading impression if readers assume every increase or decrease corresponds directly to more or fewer classified programmes.

## The NIP, MIP, and defence-account boundary

The distinction between the NIP and MIP is based on mission rather than secrecy.

The Office of the Director of National Intelligence defines the NIP as funding intelligence programmes that support national-level policymakers and intelligence-community missions. The MIP, by contrast, funds military intelligence activities supporting operational and tactical military requirements. Both programmes contain classified activities, and neither represents the entirety of government intelligence spending. Some departments also conduct intelligence-related work outside both programmes using their own appropriations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget?highlight=WyJpbiIsImlucyIsInRvIiwidG8nIiwiYSIsImEncyIsIidhIiwiaW4gdG8iLCJpbiB0byBhIiwidG8gYSJd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

This creates several overlapping categories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **National Intelligence Program (NIP):** strategic intelligence, multi-agency capabilities, and intelligence-community programmes.
* **Military Intelligence Program (MIP):** operational and tactical military intelligence supporting combat forces.
* **Broader defence appropriations:** classified weapons development, special access programmes, research, testing, procurement and operational activities that are funded through ordinary Department of Defense budget accounts rather than intelligence programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12943" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 14, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

The boundaries are functional rather than absolute. Agencies such as the National Security Agency, National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency and National Reconnaissance Office support both national decision-makers and military commanders, meaning different parts of the same organisation can be financed from different budget categories under separate authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why category shifts complicate trend reading

Because programmes evolve, their funding categories may evolve as well.

A capability originally developed for strategic intelligence may later become a routine military asset. Conversely, a tactical system may acquire broader national responsibilities. When that happens, funding responsibility can migrate between the MIP and NIP without the underlying technology disappearing or expanding.

For outside observers, several problems follow.

**Top-line comparisons become misleading.** A decline in one intelligence account may simply reflect the movement of programmes into another account rather than genuine cuts. Likewise, growth in one category may represent transferred responsibilities instead of new capabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>

**Aggregate totals hide internal reallocations.** Even when combined intelligence spending appears stable, significant changes may be occurring beneath the published figures. Congress receives classified budget justification materials that explain many of these adjustments, but the public generally sees only annual aggregate totals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>

**Defence-wide classified funding further obscures the picture.** Some highly classified programmes appear within Department of Defense procurement, research and development, or operations accounts instead of intelligence toplines. Consequently, analysts cannot assume that every black project is reflected in published intelligence-budget figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12943" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 14, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2025</span></span></span>

The result is that year-to-year comparisons often mix genuine policy choices with administrative reclassification.

## Why programmes move between buckets

Budget category changes usually occur for institutional rather than political reasons.

A programme may shift because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* its primary customer changes from national policymakers to military commanders;
* operational responsibility moves between agencies;
* acquisition transitions from research into procurement;
* organisational reforms redistribute responsibilities across the intelligence community;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>
* Congress or executive officials revise budget structures to align funding with statutory authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/IF/IF10524/11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on congress.gov.</span></span></span>

</div>

Many such changes are routine features of government budgeting rather than attempts to conceal spending. Nevertheless, from the perspective of public analysis they reduce the value of simple budget trend lines.

## Why observers sometimes describe these as "budget games"

The phrase "budget games" generally reflects criticism of how classification and accounting interact rather than allegations of fraud.

Several mechanisms contribute to this perception.

First, multiple legitimate funding paths make it difficult to determine whether apparent budget changes represent accounting adjustments or substantive policy shifts.

Second, only limited public information accompanies published intelligence toplines. The Director of National Intelligence is required to disclose the requested NIP total, but not programme-level allocations. The Department of Defense voluntarily releases MIP toplines, yet detailed classified breakdowns remain unavailable to the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget?highlight=WyJpbiIsImlucyIsInRvIiwidG8nIiwiYSIsImEncyIsIidhIiwiaW4gdG8iLCJpbiB0byBhIiwidG8gYSJd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

Third, classified defence appropriations outside the NIP and MIP mean that researchers cannot reconstruct total black-project spending simply by adding the published intelligence budgets together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12943" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 14, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2025</span></span></span>

The practical consequence is that public debate often focuses on visible aggregate numbers while much of the underlying movement remains inaccessible.

## How budget games affect public accountability

The ability to move programmes between categories does not eliminate oversight, but it changes who can perform it.

Congressional intelligence committees, armed services committees, appropriations committees and cleared executive officials generally receive substantially more detailed classified budget information than the public. They are therefore expected to distinguish genuine programme growth from accounting changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">September 26, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 26, 2024</span></span></span>

Outside government, however, [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) becomes more difficult.

Researchers attempting to measure long-term investment in classified [satellites]({{ 'satellites/' | relative_url }}), cyber capabilities, reconnaissance systems or other black programmes cannot rely solely on published toplines. Instead, they often combine budget documents, procurement records, contractor disclosures, historical appropriations, Government Accountability Office reports, Congressional Research Service analysis and occasional declassifications to estimate broader trends.

This asymmetry means that democratic oversight relies heavily on trusted institutional review rather than independent public verification. Even when headline intelligence budgets are published annually, the movement of classified activities between intelligence and defence categories can make spending appear to rise, fall or remain constant for reasons that have little to do with the actual scale of secret programmes.
