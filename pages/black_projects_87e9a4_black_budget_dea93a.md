## Introduction

The black budget is the money trail behind [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}): the part of defence and intelligence spending where public totals exist, but programme names, technical aims, contractors, locations, performance measures and many line items remain classified. The United States now publishes headline [intelligence-budget]({{ 'budget-games/' | relative_url }}) figures, so the black budget is not wholly invisible. In fiscal year 2025, the public intelligence total was $101.1 billion when the National Intelligence Program and Military Intelligence Program appropriations are combined; for fiscal year 2026, the publicly released National Intelligence Program request alone was $81.9 billion. What remains hidden is the granular map beneath those totals: which capabilities are being bought, which programmes are growing or failing, and how money is distributed among agencies and classified projects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_black_budget_dea93a-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Black Budget" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters for black projects because secrecy is not just attached to the technology after the fact. It is built into the funding system itself. A stealth aircraft, signals-intelligence platform, cyber tool or reconnaissance satellite may be protected not only by classified technical documents, but by budget language that deliberately prevents outsiders from linking a visible appropriation to a specific capability. The result is a governance bargain: elected officials and cleared oversight bodies may see far more than the public, while citizens are asked to judge national-security spending from aggregates, fragments and delayed disclosures.

## The Public Totals Are Real, but They Are Only the Surface

The clearest public window into the black budget is the Office of the Director of National Intelligence budget table. It separates the US intelligence budget into two major components: the National Intelligence Program, which covers intelligence-community programmes and projects, and the Military Intelligence Program, which supports tactical military operations through defence intelligence activity. The same ODNI table shows a decade-plus trend: total appropriated intelligence spending rose from $63.5 billion in 2007 to $106.3 billion in 2024, then $101.1 billion in 2025.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

Those figures reveal scale, not substance. The public can see that intelligence spending after 9/11 settled into a much larger range than the pre-disclosure era suggested, and that it now exceeds $100 billion in combined NIP and MIP appropriations in recent years. But the table does not show which intelligence agencies received which shares, which satellite constellations or cyber capabilities were funded, which special-access programmes absorbed cost growth, or which projects were cut after poor performance. ODNI explicitly states that, beyond the top-line NIP figure, it will not disclose further currently classified NIP budget information, except for limited unclassified appropriations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4121-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

The black budget therefore operates as a layered disclosure system. The outer layer is the public top line: large enough to show the broad national commitment to intelligence, but too aggregated to reveal operational detail. The middle layer is visible to selected lawmakers, committees, executive officials and cleared staff. The inner layer is programme-level access, usually compartmented by clearance, [need-to-know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}) rules and, for some defence programmes, Special Access Program controls.

## Aggregate Figures Show Growth, Pressure and Political Choices

Aggregate figures are useful because they puncture the myth that black budgets are completely unmeasurable. Public reporting now allows readers to compare intelligence totals across years. ODNI’s figures show that combined NIP and MIP appropriations were $67.6 billion in 2013 after sequestration, $81.7 billion in 2019, $89.8 billion in 2022, $99.6 billion in 2023, $106.3 billion in 2024 and $101.1 billion in 2025. That pattern does not identify individual black projects, but it does show the financial envelope in which they compete.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

The 2025 and 2026 figures also show how public disclosure can coexist with continuing secrecy. ODNI announced that Congress appropriated $73.3 billion for the National Intelligence Program in fiscal year 2025, including supplemental funding. It later disclosed an $81.9 billion fiscal year 2026 NIP request, while repeating that no further currently classified NIP budget details would be released. The public learns the size of the container, not the inventory inside it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4121-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

This is why black-budget evidence should be read carefully. A rising top line may reflect new technical priorities, inflation, pay and benefits, global crises, replenishment after operations, modernisation of old systems, or simply reclassification and accounting changes. A falling or flat top line does not necessarily mean black projects are being cancelled; money may shift between NIP, MIP and wider defence accounts, or from visible programmes into classified annexes. Congressional Research Service analysis has warned that the boundary between national intelligence, military intelligence and related defence activity can be porous enough to create “budget games” if programmes move in or out of intelligence categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/94-261.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[EveryCRSReport]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Every CRSReport Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRSReport Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span></span></span>

## Hidden Line Items Are the Core of the Black Budget

The phrase “hidden line item” does not mean money is necessarily spent outside law. It usually means that the public budget documents do not identify the true programme, purpose or technical content. In defence acquisition, classified funding can be embedded in procurement, research and development, operations and maintenance, or military construction accounts, while the unclassified presentation uses generic labels, code names, rolled-up totals or classified annexes.

For intelligence authorisations, Congress can enact public legislation while the actual detailed amounts sit in a classified Schedule of Authorizations. A 2025 Senate intelligence report on the fiscal year 2026 bill stated that the classified nature of US intelligence activities precluded further public disclosure of budgetary recommendations, so the committee prepared a classified annex with a classified Schedule of Authorizations. That schedule was incorporated by reference and given the legal status of public law, while being made available to appropriations committees, the President and, under Senate rules, Members of the Senate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CRPT-119srpt51/html/CRPT-119srpt51.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CRPT 119srpt51</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CRPT 119srpt51</span></span></span>

The same structure appeared in the reported fiscal year 2027 intelligence authorisation bill: public text authorised appropriations for intelligence and intelligence-related activities, but the amounts were those specified in a classified Schedule of Authorizations. The bill text also limited public disclosure of that schedule or any portion of it except where allowed by the 9/11 Commission Act disclosure requirement, where necessary to implement the budget, or where otherwise required by law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/2026/05/20/intelligence-authorization-act-for-fiscal-year-2027-as-reported-on-may-20-2026/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">intelligence authorization act for fiscal year 2027 as reported on may</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">intelligence authorization act for fiscal year 2027 as reported on may</span></span></span>

For black projects, this mechanism is the practical heart of the issue. Public law may authorise spending, but the public version does not need to display the programme’s real name, the contractor’s role, the operational target, the system architecture or the milestone problems. That is how a public appropriations process can contain secret programmes without revealing them.

## Special Access Programmes Add a Second Lock

In the defence world, Special Access Programs add extra restrictions beyond ordinary classified status. The legal framework in 10 U.S.C. §119 requires the Secretary of Defense to report annually to congressional defence committees on Department of Defense special access programmes. Those reports must include information such as programme cost estimates for the current fiscal year, the budget year and four succeeding fiscal years, and new SAP notices must include the current estimate of total programme cost and similar existing programmes or technologies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

That requirement is important because it disproves a common overstatement: SAPs are not supposed to be completely outside Congress. The defence committees are meant to receive reports, notifications and cost information. New SAPs cannot be initiated until the defence committees are notified, except where statutory procedures allow limited handling. The oversight exists, but it is classified and concentrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

The same law also shows why the public may still see very little. The Secretary of Defense may waive inclusion of certain information in SAP reports if its inclusion would adversely affect national security, though the information and justification must then be provided to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee. In other words, the system narrows access rather than eliminating it: from the public, to committees, and sometimes to just the top bipartisan leadership of those committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

That narrowing is defensible when the project concerns a fragile intelligence method or a technology whose value depends on [surprise]({{ 'surprise/' | relative_url }}). It is more controversial when secrecy shields cost overruns, duplication, weak testing, contractor dependence or policy choices that could be debated without exposing sensitive technical detail.

## The Snowden-Era Glimpse Showed What a Real Budget Map Looks Like

The most famous modern glimpse beneath the aggregate top line came in 2013, when The Washington Post reported on a classified fiscal year 2013 intelligence “black budget” summary obtained from Edward Snowden. The public already knew the government’s overall intelligence-spending level, but the leaked material mapped agency priorities, objectives, successes and failures in a way official releases did not.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/black-budget-summary-details-us-spy-networks-successes-failures-and-objectives/2013/08/29/7e57bb78-10ab-11e3-8cdd-bcdc09410972_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post‘Black budget’</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post‘Black budget’</span></span></span>

That episode illustrates the difference between a top-line figure and a usable budget map. A top line tells the public that tens of billions are being spent. A budget summary can show which agencies dominate, which mission areas are prioritised, what performance gaps officials recognise, and where money is being directed after operational failures. Wired’s contemporaneous coverage noted that the material revealed previously hidden details about priorities such as cryptologic work and the relative scale of major intelligence agencies, while also stressing that sensitive details had been withheld from publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2013/08/black-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New Snowden Leak Reports &#x27;Groundbreaking&#x27; NSA Crypto-Cracking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New Snowden Leak Reports &#x27;Groundbreaking&#x27; NSA Crypto-Cracking</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that leaks are a good substitute for oversight. It is that official aggregate disclosure leaves a large analytical gap. When programme-level material appears, even partially, it changes public understanding because it shows how the budget actually functions: as a set of choices among collection, analysis, covert action, cyber operations, satellites, human intelligence, counterterrorism, counterproliferation and military support.

## What Remains Hidden Is Not Just Names

The most obvious hidden material is programme identity: the true name, sponsor, contractor, location and mission of a black project. But the more important hidden categories are often less dramatic and more consequential for governance.

**Performance.** Public budget documents rarely show whether a classified system is meeting technical milestones, whether intelligence collection is producing useful results, or whether a platform’s promised capability has been downgraded. The 2013 black-budget reporting was striking precisely because it described objectives and gaps, not merely spending totals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/national-security/black-budget-summary-details-us-spy-networks-successes-failures-and-objectives/2013/08/29/7e57bb78-10ab-11e3-8cdd-bcdc09410972_story.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: washingtonpost.com">[The Washington Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">washingtonpost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Washington Post‘Black budget’</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Washington Post‘Black budget’</span></span></span>

**Cost growth and schedule delay.** Secrecy can make it harder for outside analysts, watchdog groups and ordinary legislators to compare classified acquisition performance with ordinary procurement. A Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office review of special access programmes in the early 1990s found that sampled programmes had cost increases, schedule delays and performance problems similar to other DoD acquisitions, while their public funding lines were purposely disguised in unclassified budget documents to enhance security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span></span></span>

**Duplication.** A hidden programme may overlap with another classified or unclassified effort. Section 119’s requirement that notices for new special access programmes identify similar existing programmes or technologies suggests that duplication is a known risk inside the system, not merely an outside suspicion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

**Policy consequences.** Some black-budget decisions are not just technical. Funding a surveillance architecture, covert-action capability, intelligence-sharing system or autonomous targeting support tool can alter civil-liberties risks, alliance relationships and escalation dynamics. Yet the debate may occur only among cleared officials because the budget line itself is classified.

## Why Details Stay Classified

The strongest argument for black-budget secrecy is that some capabilities are destroyed by disclosure. If an adversary learns the operating assumptions of a sensor, the access route of a cyber operation, the signature of a stealth platform or the collection priority of a satellite system, it can adapt. In those cases, the budget line is sensitive because it points to the existence, scale or maturity of the capability.

Budget details can also disclose timing. A sudden rise in a classified procurement account may suggest that a prototype has moved into production. A construction line can point to a facility. A contractor award can identify an industrial base, a technology path or a deployment plan. Even when individual figures seem harmless, repeated disclosures can allow adversaries to build a pattern over time.

The official disclosure language reflects this logic. ODNI does not merely decline to publish programme details as a matter of convenience; it states that beyond the disclosed top-line figure there will be no further disclosures of currently classified NIP budget information, with only limited exceptions. Senate intelligence legislation similarly uses classified schedules and annexes because public disclosure of budget recommendations is considered incompatible with the classified nature of intelligence activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4121-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

Still, the case for secrecy is strongest at the technical and operational edge, not necessarily at every level of financial governance. A government can plausibly hide a sensor’s design while disclosing stronger aggregate categories, retrospective cost bands, audit findings or [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) lessons after a programme ends. The live dispute is not whether secrecy should exist, but how much budgetary opacity is truly necessary.

## Oversight Works Differently When the Public Cannot Follow the Money

Ordinary public spending oversight relies on many eyes: journalists, inspectors general, rival [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}), academics, advocacy groups, auditors, local communities and interested citizens. Black-budget oversight relies on a much smaller circle. Classified annexes, SAP reports and committee briefings can provide legal accountability, but they reduce the number of people able to test whether the spending makes sense.

That smaller circle creates two opposite risks. The first is under-protection: too much disclosure could expose a fragile capability or reveal national priorities to adversaries. The second is under-scrutiny: too much classification can make it easier for weak programmes to survive, for cost overruns to be normalised, or for policy-heavy choices to be treated as technical secrets. CRS has framed the core public-disclosure dilemma as a long-running question of whether limited budget data can be released without revealing properly classified programmes or helping adversaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/94-261.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[EveryCRSReport]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Every CRSReport Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRSReport Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span></span></span>

This is also why aggregate disclosure is politically valuable but incomplete. It gives the public a rough sense of scale and trend. It does not allow the public to compare agency claims against outcomes, or to know whether a failed classified programme consumed billions before cancellation. For black projects, the most important oversight questions are often simple but hard to answer publicly: What is the purpose? How much has it cost? Who benefits? Is it working? What risks does it create? Who outside the programme has the power to stop it?

## The Line Between Legitimate Secrecy and Budgetary Blindness

The black budget [reveals]({{ 'reveals/' | relative_url }}) a paradox at the centre of black projects. The public can know that very large sums are being spent on classified intelligence and defence activities, and can even track the official top line year by year. Yet the public cannot see the line items that would explain the real allocation of risk, money and technological ambition. That is not an accidental feature; it is the design.

The best reading of black-budget evidence is therefore neither credulous nor conspiratorial. Public totals are meaningful. Classified annexes and SAP reporting show that legal oversight mechanisms exist. At the same time, the hidden line items prevent ordinary democratic scrutiny of programme performance, cost growth, duplication and policy tradeoffs. The black budget is not simply a pot of secret money; it is a governance architecture for funding capabilities whose value may depend on secrecy, while asking the public to accept that the most important details must remain behind a classified wall.
