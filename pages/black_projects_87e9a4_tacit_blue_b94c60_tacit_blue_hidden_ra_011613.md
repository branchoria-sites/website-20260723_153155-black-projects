## Introduction

Tacit Blue was designed to answer a difficult question that lay at the heart of battlefield stealth: could an aircraft actively search for enemy forces with radar without immediately revealing its own position? A stealth airframe alone was not enough. Conventional surveillance radars often emit powerful, predictable signals that can be detected by enemy electronic support systems long before the radar itself finds a target. For a reconnaissance aircraft intended to operate close to hostile air defences, those emissions could negate the value of its carefully engineered low [radar signature]({{ 'radar-signature/' | relative_url }}).



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_tacit_blue_b94c60_tacit_blue_hidden_ra_011613-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Hidden Emissions illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_tacit_blue_b94c60_tacit_blue_hidden_ra_011613-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_tacit_blue_b94c60_tacit_blue_hidden_ra_011613-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Northrop's solution paired a low-observable airframe with a **low probability of intercept (LPI)** side-looking radar derived from the Pave Mover technology effort. Rather than making the radar invisible—which is impossible—it sought to make the emissions difficult to recognise, classify and locate quickly enough for an opponent to react effectively. The combination became one of Tacit Blue's most influential technological achievements, shaping later surveillance and stealth aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/tacit_blue.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Security TACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global SecurityTACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX)July 7, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 7, 2011</span></span></span>

## Why ordinary radar transmissions reveal aircraft

A conventional radar is effectively a powerful radio transmitter. It sends pulses of electromagnetic energy, waits for reflections from targets, and calculates their position from the returning echoes.

The problem is that hostile electronic intelligence receivers do not need to wait for those echoes. They only need to detect the outgoing transmission itself. In many situations this is actually easier than detecting the aircraft because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the radar broadcasts energy directly towards the surrounding environment;
* interceptor receivers need detect only the transmitted signal, not a much weaker reflected echo;
* characteristic pulse timing, frequencies and scan patterns often identify the radar type and, by extension, the aircraft carrying it.

</div>

For an aircraft intended to fly near heavily defended front lines, this creates a paradox. The aircraft may be difficult for enemy search radars to see, yet its own sensor can advertise its presence if it operates in a conventional way. This was exactly the vulnerability Tacit Blue sought to reduce.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1049/iet-rsn.2016.0295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[IET Research Journal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ietresearch.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IET Research JournalUsing a moving aerial platform to detect and localise a low probability of intercept radar - Hejazi Kookamari - 2017...</span></span></span>

## What low probability of intercept means

Low probability of intercept does **not** mean that a radar cannot be detected. Instead, it means making interception sufficiently difficult that enemy receivers either fail to recognise the signal or require much longer to identify and locate it.

Although the detailed implementation of Tacit Blue's Hughes radar remains classified, open technical literature describes the principles that define LPI radar generally. These include combinations of:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* lower peak transmission power spread over longer transmission periods;
* wide bandwidth rather than concentrating energy on a narrow frequency;
* rapid frequency changes that make tracking the signal difficult;
* coded waveforms requiring sophisticated processing to distinguish from background noise;
* carefully controlled antenna sidelobes that minimise unwanted radiation away from the intended beam.

</div>

Rather than producing obvious, repetitive pulses, an LPI radar attempts to distribute its energy in ways that appear less conspicuous to hostile electronic support receivers while remaining recoverable by its own sophisticated signal processors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-probability-of-intercept_radar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Low-probability-of-intercept radar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Low-probability-of-intercept radar</span></span></span>

## How Tacit Blue combined stealth with its radar

Tacit Blue's significance was not simply that it carried an LPI radar. The aircraft itself was designed around the requirement to use that radar while surviving close to enemy forces.

DARPA has described Tacit Blue as unusual because the airframe was developed around the sensor rather than treating the radar as equipment added after the aircraft had already been designed. The large side-looking antenna determined much of the aircraft's internal arrangement and external geometry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.darpa.mil/news/features/stealth" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: darpa.mil">[darpa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">darpa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stealth aircraft &#124; DARPA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Stealth aircraft &#124; DARPA</span></span></span>

This integration worked on several levels.

First, the aircraft reduced the chance of being detected by conventional hostile radar through its carefully shaped low-observable structure.

Second, its own surveillance radar was engineered to avoid becoming an equally obvious source of electromagnetic emissions.

Third, the radar supported the aircraft's intended mission of ground moving target indication (GMTI) and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imaging, allowing it to monitor armoured formations without relying on visual observation. According to programme descriptions released after declassification, the radar combined these surveillance modes with low probability of intercept features specifically intended to improve survivability near the forward battle area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/tacit_blue.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Security TACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global SecurityTACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX)July 7, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 7, 2011</span></span></span>

## Sensors and stealth had to work together

Tacit Blue demonstrated an important systems-engineering lesson that later became central to stealth aircraft: survivability depends on **signature management across multiple domains**, not simply reducing radar reflections.

An aircraft's observable characteristics include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* radar reflections from its structure;
* infrared emissions from engines;
* radio-frequency emissions from radar and communications;
* visual and acoustic signatures.

</div>

Reducing only one of these can leave another dominant source of detection. Tacit Blue therefore combined a buried engine installation, shielded intake arrangement, [curved stealth]({{ 'curved-stealth/' | relative_url }}) shaping and an LPI radar into one integrated design rather than treating each feature separately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0896tacit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine The (Tacit) Blue Whale &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine The (Tacit) Blue Whale &#124; Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span></span></span>

This philosophy became increasingly important as military electronic surveillance systems improved. Passive receivers capable of detecting radar emissions posed a growing threat even to aircraft with very small radar cross-sections.

## What remains publicly unknown

One reason Tacit Blue continues to attract attention is that the aircraft's overall objective is well documented while the technical implementation remains largely secret.

Public sources consistently confirm that the Hughes side-looking radar incorporated LPI capabilities and supported GMTI and SAR functions. They also agree that demonstrating this combination of stealth and survivable surveillance was a principal programme objective. However, detailed information about:

* specific transmitted waveforms;
* power levels;
* coding methods;
* frequency management;
* onboard signal processing;
* electronic protection techniques

has never been officially released.

This lack of detail is unsurprising. Many of the same design principles remain relevant to modern airborne surveillance radars, making the underlying engineering sensitive decades after Tacit Blue first flew. Consequently, discussions of the aircraft's LPI radar necessarily rely on confirmed programme goals and the publicly understood principles of LPI radar rather than classified implementation details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/tacit_blue.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Security TACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global SecurityTACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX)July 7, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 7, 2011</span></span></span>

## Why the demonstration mattered

Tacit Blue proved that a surveillance aircraft did not have to choose between carrying an effective radar and remaining difficult to detect. By integrating a low-observable airframe with a radar designed to minimise its own electromagnetic signature, the programme showed that active sensing and stealth could complement rather than undermine each other.

The specific radar developed for Tacit Blue also fed into later airborne ground-surveillance programmes, while the broader concept—that emissions themselves are part of an aircraft's signature—became a lasting principle of stealth design. Later low-observable aircraft increasingly treated radar, communications and other electronic systems as features whose emissions required as much management as the aircraft's physical shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/systems/aircraft/tacit_blue.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: globalsecurity.org">[globalsecurity.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">globalsecurity.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Global Security TACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Global SecurityTACIT BLUE Battlefield Surveillance Aircraft-Experimental (BSAX)July 7, 2011...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 7, 2011</span></span></span>
