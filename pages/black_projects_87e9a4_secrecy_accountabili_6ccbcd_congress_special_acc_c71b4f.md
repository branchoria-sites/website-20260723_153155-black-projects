## Introduction

Special Access Programs (SAPs) are among the United States government's most tightly protected defence activities, with security controls that go beyond ordinary classified programmes. That extra secrecy does not remove them from congressional oversight. Instead, US law creates a specialised oversight system in which a limited group of congressional defence committees receives classified reports on programme funding, purpose and justification while the wider Congress and the public remain excluded. This arrangement is intended to balance operational secrecy with democratic [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}): protecting genuinely sensitive military capabilities without allowing secret programmes to operate entirely outside legislative scrutiny. The system is deliberately selective, but it also raises continuing questions about whether Congress receives enough information to exercise meaningful oversight.

## How does Congress receive information on Special Access Programs?

Congressional oversight of Department of Defense SAPs is governed primarily by 10 U.S.C. §119. Rather than requiring [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}), the statute obliges the Secretary of Defense to provide classified reports directly to the congressional defence committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

## Annual reporting requirements

The annual reporting process is designed to ensure that SAPs are incorporated into the normal defence budgeting cycle rather than becoming entirely hidden funding streams.

The reports provide lawmakers with information that allows them to compare:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* requested funding against previous expenditure;
* programme progress against stated milestones;
* projected future costs; and
* relationships between current and planned spending.

</div>

Because the reports accompany the President's defence budget, congressional committees can evaluate whether continued funding is appropriate before appropriations legislation is enacted. This creates a formal opportunity for questioning programme growth, delays or duplication even when technical details remain classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

## Why must new Special Access Programs be reported?

Congressional oversight is not limited to existing programmes. The law also requires advance notification when new SAPs are established.

By 1 February each year, the Secretary of Defense must submit a classified report identifying every newly designated SAP. That notification must include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* notice that the programme has received SAP status;
* the justification for granting additional protection;
* the current estimate of total programme cost; and
* identification of existing programmes or technologies with similar missions or technologies.

</div>

The comparison requirement serves an important governance purpose. It allows Congress to consider whether a proposed SAP genuinely requires exceptional protection or merely duplicates work already being carried out elsewhere within the Department of Defense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

The statute also provides that a SAP generally may not be initiated until Congress has been notified and a 30-day period has elapsed after notification, reinforcing the principle that even highly classified programmes are expected to enter the oversight system before becoming operational.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

## What happens when a programme's secrecy changes?

Congress is also notified when the protection surrounding a SAP changes.

If the Department of Defense intends to alter the classification of a SAP or publicly declassify information about one, it must normally notify the congressional defence committees at least 14 days before the change. The report must explain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the proposed change;
* the reasons for it; and
* any planned public announcement.

</div>

In exceptional circumstances, the Department may shorten this timetable, but it must explain why advance notice was not possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

This requirement gives Congress visibility not only into programme creation but also into major decisions affecting long-term secrecy.

## Why legislative visibility remains limited

Although Congress possesses statutory oversight authority, visibility is intentionally restricted.

Several features limit how much legislators can know:

* **Committee-based access.** Information is generally provided to the congressional defence committees rather than to every Member of Congress.
* **Classified handling rules.** Members and staff receiving SAP information must comply with stringent security procedures that restrict wider discussion.
* **[Need-to-know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}) principles.** Even within Congress, access is not automatically universal simply because information has been reported.
* **Executive waiver authority.** If the Secretary of Defense determines that including particular information in a report would harm national security, specific reporting requirements may be waived on a case-by-case basis. In those situations, however, the omitted information and the justification for withholding it must still be provided jointly to the chair and ranking minority member of each congressional defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

These mechanisms mean that congressional oversight exists but is concentrated among a relatively small number of cleared legislators rather than the legislature as a whole.

## The continuing accountability debate

The oversight framework reflects a deliberate compromise rather than a complete solution.

Supporters argue that limiting access protects military advantages while ensuring elected representatives can review programme costs, legality and strategic necessity without exposing sensitive capabilities. Because the defence committees receive classified budget and programme information, SAPs are not intended to exist entirely outside congressional appropriations or legislative review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

Critics, however, contend that oversight can still be weakened by the combination of extreme compartmentalisation, limited membership access and executive discretion over the information shared. The central policy question is therefore not whether Congress has statutory authority—it does—but whether the information provided is sufficient for legislators to challenge programme assumptions, detect unnecessary duplication and hold the executive branch accountable without compromising national security.

Within the broader debate over [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), this illustrates the distinction between secrecy and unchecked secrecy. Special Access Programs are legally protected beyond ordinary classification, yet federal law requires structured reporting to Congress so that the most sensitive defence activities remain subject to at least a defined form of democratic oversight.
