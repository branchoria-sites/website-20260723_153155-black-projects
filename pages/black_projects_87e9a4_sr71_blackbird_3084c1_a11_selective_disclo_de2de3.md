## Introduction

On 29 February 1964, President Lyndon B. Johnson publicly announced the existence of a new Mach 3 reconnaissance aircraft, calling it the “A-11”. The statement appeared to lift the curtain on one of the Cold War’s most advanced aviation projects, but it was in fact a carefully managed disclosure. The aircraft shown to the public was not the CIA’s highly classified A-12 OXCART in its true operational form, and the announcement concealed the organisational structure, technology and mission that lay behind the programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

The episode illustrates a recurring pattern in [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}): governments may reveal enough to demonstrate technological superiority, reassure political audiences or explain visible activity, while preserving the most sensitive information. Rather than ending secrecy, Johnson’s announcement transformed it into a more selective and sustainable form.

## What Johnson announced in February 1964

Johnson's announcement presented the aircraft as a major American advance in military aviation. He stated that the new aircraft could sustain speeds above Mach 3 and described it as an interceptor designated the "A-11". Public photographs accompanied the announcement, giving the United States an opportunity to display an extraordinary aerospace achievement without explaining the classified intelligence programme from which it emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

The timing served several purposes. By early 1964, advanced Blackbird-family aircraft had already been flying in secret for years, making complete concealment increasingly difficult. Test flights, production activity and growing awareness inside government all increased pressure for some form of public explanation. A controlled presidential announcement allowed the administration to shape the narrative instead of risking piecemeal disclosure through speculation or leaks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

Historians of the Blackbird programme have noted that the aircraft displayed publicly was associated with the interceptor derivative that later became known as the YF-12A, while Johnson deliberately referred to it using the simpler designation "A-11". This naming choice added another layer of ambiguity, separating the public story from the deeper classified programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

## Why the A-11 announcement did not tell the full story

The announcement left untouched the programme that mattered most strategically: the CIA's Project OXCART and its single-seat A-12 reconnaissance aircraft. The A-12 had been developed under extraordinary secrecy as the successor to the [U-2]({{ 'u-2/' | relative_url }}), with entirely different operational arrangements from the public impression created in 1964. It was managed by the CIA rather than the Air Force, flown from highly restricted facilities and intended for strategic intelligence collection rather than public demonstration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

Several critical facts remained concealed:

* **The operating organisation.** The announcement did not reveal the CIA's central role in designing, testing and preparing the aircraft for intelligence missions.
* **The true reconnaissance mission.** Public emphasis on performance obscured the aircraft's primary value as a strategic intelligence platform.
* **The wider Blackbird family.** The existence of multiple closely related aircraft—including reconnaissance and interceptor variants—was not explained.
* **Technical innovations.** Sensitive features involving [radar signature]({{ 'radar-signature/' | relative_url }}) reduction, specialised materials, sensors and operational methods remained classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

As a result, the public learned that the United States possessed an extraordinarily fast aircraft without learning how that capability had actually been organised or intended to operate.

## Selective disclosure as a black-project tactic

Johnson's announcement demonstrates that secrecy is not simply a choice between total concealment and complete openness. Instead, black programmes often rely on selective disclosure, releasing carefully chosen facts while protecting the information that would most benefit foreign intelligence services.

In this case, several objectives could be pursued simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Strategic signalling.** Public confirmation of a Mach 3 aircraft demonstrated American technological leadership during the Cold War.
* **Protection of intelligence capabilities.** The reconnaissance architecture, operational concepts and classified systems remained hidden.
* **Narrative control.** The government defined what the public and foreign observers would know instead of allowing rumours to dominate.
* **Long-term programme protection.** By revealing only part of the story, officials reduced pressure for broader disclosure while preserving operational security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

</div>

This approach reflects a broader governance principle within black projects: information can itself become a managed capability. Officials decide not merely whether to disclose, but precisely which facts advance national objectives and which remain compartmented.

## Why the announcement still matters

The 1964 announcement has sometimes been misunderstood as the moment the Blackbird programme became public. Later declassification showed that this interpretation was incomplete. The public had been shown only one carefully constructed version of a much larger classified enterprise, whose CIA origins and operational history remained hidden for decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>

For historians of secret aerospace programmes, the episode is valuable because it demonstrates that official acknowledgement does not necessarily end secrecy. Instead, disclosure can become another security instrument. By revealing exceptional performance while concealing organisational structure, operational use and much of the technology, Johnson's "A-11" announcement became one of the clearest examples of how black projects can be revealed sideways—public enough to signal power, but incomplete enough to protect the programme that truly mattered.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/a-12-oxcart/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A-12 Oxcart</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A-12 Oxcart - CIA...</span></span></span>
