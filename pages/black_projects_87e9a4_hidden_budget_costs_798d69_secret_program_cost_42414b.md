## Introduction

The most effective cost controls inside secret programmes are not [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}), but disciplined internal scrutiny. Classified projects can remain operationally secure while still being subject to rigorous financial management if authorised reviewers receive enough evidence to test estimates, challenge schedules and verify progress. Experience from defence oversight shows that secrecy alone does not determine whether programmes stay on budget. Rather, success depends on whether decision-makers maintain credible cost estimates, objectively measure work completed, and conduct timely independent reviews before problems become too expensive to correct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAODecember 21, 1992...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

For [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), the central implementation challenge is preserving compartmented information without allowing optimistic assumptions or weak management to escape examination. Effective controls therefore focus on giving a limited number of cleared officials reliable evidence instead of broad public visibility.

## Building credible estimates and realistic contingency

Cost control begins long before construction or production. Secret programmes often involve immature technologies, uncertain manufacturing methods and ambitious performance goals, making early estimates especially vulnerable to optimism.

Well-established government cost-estimating practice emphasises several safeguards:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Base estimates on clearly defined technical requirements rather than desired budget targets.
* Compare assumptions with historical programmes where appropriate.
* Identify major cost drivers explicitly.
* Document assumptions so they can be tested later.
* Include contingency reserves that reflect technical uncertainty rather than arbitrary percentages.
* Update estimates as engineering knowledge improves rather than preserving outdated baselines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a77186.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO-09-3SP, GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide: Best Practices for Developing and Managing Capital Program CostsMarch 2, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2009</span></span></span>

</div>

Independent cost estimates are particularly valuable. Rather than relying solely on the programme office's projections, an independent team reconstructs major cost drivers using separate assumptions and data. Differences between the two estimates expose hidden risks before key funding decisions are made. The GAO's Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide describes independent cost estimating as the strongest form of analytical review because it provides senior decision-makers with an objective comparison rather than merely validating the programme's own figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a77186.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO-09-3SP, GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide: Best Practices for Developing and Managing Capital Program CostsMarch 2, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2009</span></span></span>

For classified programmes, this review does not require wider disclosure. Independent analysts simply need the appropriate security clearances and sufficient technical access to evaluate the assumptions.

## Using schedule and progress data to challenge claims

Cost overruns rarely appear without warning. They usually emerge alongside schedule slips, changing technical requirements or slower-than-expected engineering progress.

One of the most widely used methods for identifying these trends is Earned Value Management (EVM), which integrates three measurements:

* Planned work.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Work actually completed.
* Money actually spent.

</div>

Comparing these measures allows reviewers to determine whether a programme is merely spending money or genuinely completing planned work. Persistent divergence between schedule progress and expenditure provides an early warning that future cost growth is becoming more likely. The Department of Defense continues to require ANSI/EIA-748-compliant earned value systems for many major cost-type and incentive contracts, including those supporting classified work where appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/subpart-234.2-earned-value-management-system" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: acquisition.gov">[Acquisition.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">acquisition.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Subpart 234.2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EARNED VALUE MANAGEMENT SYSTEM &#124; Acquisition.GOVMay 7, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 7, 2026</span></span></span>

The value of earned-value information in black programmes is that reviewers do not need public disclosure of sensitive capabilities. Cleared oversight officials can examine performance indices, milestone completion and forecast completion costs without revealing operational details outside authorised channels.

Reliable integrated master schedules provide a second control. Linking engineering activities, testing milestones and production tasks into a single schedule makes it easier to identify whether apparent progress depends upon unrealistic assumptions or whether delays in one subsystem threaten the entire programme. When schedule data and cost data are analysed together, optimistic reporting becomes more difficult.

## Designing timely review without public disclosure

The timing of oversight often matters more than its visibility.

Reviews conducted only after major spending decisions have already been made rarely prevent overruns. More effective systems introduce independent scrutiny before major programme milestones, allowing leaders to reconsider scope, technology maturity or affordability while meaningful alternatives still exist.

Effective classified oversight generally includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Independent milestone reviews before large funding commitments.
* Cleared technical experts outside the programme office.
* Financial specialists able to examine contractor performance.
* Inspectors general and audit organisations with authorised access.
* Congressional committees operating through secure classified procedures where required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAODecember 21, 1992...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

</div>

The objective is not to maximise the number of reviewers but to ensure that reviewers possess sufficient authority, technical expertise and independence to challenge management assumptions.

GAO's reviews of special access acquisition programmes found that oversight within the Department of Defense became progressively more centralised and structured during the late 1980s and early 1990s in response to congressional concerns. Increased Inspector General involvement, stronger reporting requirements and improved access for congressional defence committees were intended to strengthen [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) while preserving programme secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAODecember 21, 1992...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

## Why independence matters more than visibility

A recurring lesson from major acquisition programmes is that programme managers naturally become invested in their own plans. Even experienced managers may underestimate technical difficulty or delay reporting emerging problems.

Independent reviewers help counter several predictable biases:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Optimism bias**, where future performance is assumed to improve despite contrary evidence.
* **Commitment escalation**, where additional funding is justified primarily because substantial resources have already been invested.
* **Institutional pressure**, where reporting bad news may threaten programme continuation.

</div>

Outside reviewers can compare assumptions with previous classified and unclassified programmes, challenge productivity forecasts, question contingency levels and recommend re-baselining when circumstances have changed. These functions require independence far more than public transparency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a77186.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO-09-3SP, GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide: Best Practices for Developing and Managing Capital Program CostsMarch 2, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2009</span></span></span>

## Evidence from special access programmes

Public evidence about individual black programmes remains limited, but available oversight reports provide several useful observations.

GAO's review of Department of Defense special access acquisition programmes found no systematic evidence that these highly classified projects experienced fundamentally different patterns of cost growth, schedule delay or performance shortfalls compared with comparable conventional acquisition programmes. Some secret programmes performed well; others suffered the same types of overruns seen elsewhere in defence procurement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ACQUISITION Oversight of Special Access...</span></span></span>

That finding is significant because it suggests that secrecy itself is not the primary driver of affordability. Instead, familiar project-management disciplines remain decisive:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Realistic baseline estimates.
* Continuous schedule monitoring.
* Objective measurement of completed work.
* Independent financial and technical review.
* Willingness to adjust programmes when evidence changes.

## The practical balance between secrecy and affordability

Effective cost control inside black projects does not require revealing sensitive technologies to the public. It requires ensuring that authorised decision-makers can ask difficult questions early enough to influence outcomes.

The strongest implementation model combines credible cost estimating, disciplined schedule management, earned-value performance measurement, independent cost analysis and recurring milestone reviews by cleared experts. Together these mechanisms preserve operational secrecy while maintaining the analytical discipline needed to detect unrealistic assumptions, emerging overruns and declining programme performance before they become irreversible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a77186.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO-09-3SP, GAO Cost Estimating and Assessment Guide: Best Practices for Developing and Managing Capital Program CostsMarch 2, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2, 2009</span></span></span>
