## Introduction

Secrecy is intended to keep sensitive capabilities away from adversaries, but excessive secrecy can also prevent authorised personnel from accessing information they are already entitled to use. Within [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), this problem is amplified by Special Access Programs, compartmented systems and strict “need-to-know” rules. While these controls reduce the risk of unauthorised disclosure, they can also fragment knowledge across organisations, making it harder for cleared officials to compare data, detect duplication, coordinate operations or identify emerging safety risks. The result is a paradox: systems designed to improve security may reduce the effectiveness of the people responsible for protecting national security when legitimate information sharing becomes unnecessarily difficult. The challenge is therefore not eliminating [compartmentation]({{ 'compartmentation/' | relative_url }}), but ensuring that it remains proportionate and does not obstruct authorised collaboration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesInformation Security Oversight Office (ISOO) &#124; National ArchivesJuly 9, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 2026</span></span></span>

## How restricted systems create signal dilution

In highly classified environments, access decisions are often based on both security clearance and a demonstrated [need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}). Possessing the highest clearance does not automatically grant access to every programme or document. This principle protects exceptionally sensitive information, but when applied too broadly it can scatter related evidence across separate [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) that few individuals can view together.

For black programmes, this means engineers, operators, intelligence analysts and acquisition officials may each possess only a narrow fragment of the overall picture. Each group may make reasonable decisions within its own compartment while remaining unaware that another office already solved the same technical problem, identified a conflicting requirement or discovered an important limitation.

Signal dilution occurs because genuinely significant information becomes buried among numerous isolated repositories. Rather than lacking data, organisations possess too much separated data. Analysts spend time determining whether they are permitted to search particular systems before they can determine whether relevant information exists at all. This increases delays during technical investigations, operational planning and programme oversight. The 9/11 Commission later described a broader intelligence version of this problem, arguing that an institutional [culture]({{ 'culture/' | relative_url }}) of "need to know" had become an obstacle and should evolve toward a "need to share" where appropriate for authorised personnel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://911commission.gov/report/911Report_Exec.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 911commission.gov">[911 Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">911commission.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">911 Commission National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">911 Commission National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States</span></span></span>

The issue is not simply technological. Modern classified networks can store enormous quantities of information, but organisational barriers often determine whether users know where to search or whether they are permitted to retrieve related material from another compartment. As a result, information may technically exist within government while remaining practically unavailable to the officials who need it.

## Why compartmentation hides duplication and safety risks

Compartmentation intentionally limits the number of people exposed to exceptionally sensitive information. However, if boundaries become too rigid, they can conceal unnecessary duplication and delay recognition of hazards.

Several risks emerge:

* **Repeated work:** Independent [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) may unknowingly conduct similar research because they cannot discover earlier classified efforts.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Conflicting engineering decisions:** Separate programmes may develop incompatible standards or assumptions without cross-comparison.
* **Missed technical warnings:** Safety observations recorded inside one compartment may never reach engineers responsible for related systems elsewhere.
* **Reduced oversight:** Auditors and inspectors with broad responsibilities may require multiple compartment approvals before assembling a complete picture of programme performance.

</div>

Large defence and intelligence organisations have repeatedly recognised this tension. Classification policy seeks to protect information whose disclosure could damage national security, but oversight bodies have also warned against excessive classification because it complicates management, increases administrative costs and reduces the effectiveness of authorised information exchange.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesInformation Security Oversight Office (ISOO) &#124; National ArchivesJuly 9, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 2026</span></span></span>

In black projects, the problem is particularly acute because programmes are deliberately structured so that few individuals understand the entire system. That design can be essential against espionage, yet it also increases dependence on carefully managed mechanisms for authorised cross-compartment coordination.

## Why cumbersome controls encourage informal workarounds

When official procedures become excessively slow or fragmented, personnel often seek practical alternatives that allow work to continue. These responses are usually intended to overcome administrative barriers rather than bypass security altogether, but they can create new vulnerabilities.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* verbally passing information that is difficult to retrieve formally;
* rewriting sensitive material into less detailed summaries;
* relying on personal networks instead of documented information channels;
* repeatedly requesting temporary access permissions, creating administrative delays during urgent work.

</div>

Such workarounds illustrate an important point: people generally bypass cumbersome processes because the operational task still has to be completed. If authorised information cannot move efficiently through approved systems, pressure grows to move it through less formal ones.

Security policy increasingly recognises that safeguarding and responsible sharing are complementary rather than competing objectives. Structural reforms introduced after major security failures have sought both to strengthen protection against unauthorised disclosure and to improve authorised sharing among cleared personnel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesInformation Security Oversight Office (ISOO) &#124; National ArchivesJuly 9, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 9, 2026</span></span></span>

## The trust problem inside classified organisations

Over-classification affects internal [trust]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}) as well as public confidence. When experienced personnel repeatedly discover that documents are inaccessible despite already holding the necessary clearance, they may begin to question whether restrictions genuinely reflect security needs or simply institutional habit.

This can produce several organisational consequences:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* confidence in classification decisions declines;
* officials become uncertain about what can legitimately be shared;
* collaboration slows because participants fear accidental violations;
* programme managers spend increasing time resolving access disputes rather than technical problems.

</div>

The classification system functions best when restrictions appear understandable and proportionate. If too much information receives exceptional protection, genuinely critical secrets become harder to distinguish from routine material requiring only ordinary safeguards.

## Balancing compartmentation with authorised collaboration

The solution is not unrestricted access. Black projects often involve [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}), military capabilities or advanced technologies whose compromise would create genuine national security harm. Strong compartmentation therefore remains necessary for many activities.

The objective is instead to ensure that authorised personnel can share information efficiently once a legitimate operational requirement has been established. Governments have increasingly emphasised this balance through reforms encouraging responsible information sharing without weakening protection of sensitive sources and methods. Following the intelligence failures highlighted by the 9/11 Commission, policy increasingly shifted from treating secrecy as an end in itself toward ensuring that classified information reaches the right cleared people at the right time while remaining protected from unauthorised disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://911commission.gov/report/911Report_Exec.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: 911commission.gov">[911commission.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">911commission.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">911 Commission National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">911 Commission National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States</span></span></span>

Within black programmes, effective security therefore depends not only on preventing leaks but also on preventing unnecessary internal isolation. A compartment that blocks adversaries is valuable; a compartment that also blocks the engineers, operators or oversight officials already authorised to act on the information may ultimately weaken the programme it was intended to protect.

      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
