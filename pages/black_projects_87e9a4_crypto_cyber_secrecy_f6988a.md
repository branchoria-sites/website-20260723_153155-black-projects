## Introduction

Cryptologic and cyber capabilities are among the [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) most dependent on secrecy because their effectiveness often rests on an adversary not knowing that a weakness has been discovered. A code-breaking programme may quietly read encrypted traffic only while the target trusts its cipher, follows exploitable procedures or reuses flawed key material. A cyber operation may retain access only while the target remains unaware of a software vulnerability, compromised account, implanted tool or detectable collection method.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_crypto_cyber_secrecy_f6988a-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Crypto Cyber" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This creates an unusually fragile kind of advantage. A secret aircraft can remain useful after its existence becomes known; a secret digital access route may disappear as soon as the target changes a password, patches a server, replaces an encryption system or begins feeding false information through the compromised channel. Secrecy therefore protects more than technical specifications. It protects the target’s ignorance—and, with it, the continuing stream of intelligence or operational access.

## Cryptologic value

Cryptology covers both the protection of communications and the effort to defeat that protection. In intelligence work, the most valuable breakthrough is not always the mathematical recovery of a cipher key. It may be the discovery of poor operating procedures, predictable message formats, reused key material, insecure equipment, identifiable transmission patterns or a mistake repeated by human operators.

This distinction matters because cryptologic value is usually **channel-specific and time-sensitive**. Intelligence services may be able to read one military network but not another, or understand some periods of traffic while suffering gaps after technical or procedural changes. The German Navy’s more secure versions and operating practices made its communications harder to exploit than many army or air-force systems. Changes in equipment could interrupt access until cryptanalysts found a new route back in.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/cryptologic-spectrum/Ultra.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ultra</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSAUltra and the Battle of the Atlantic: The British View26 Jul 2010 — the German Enigma machine, when it was introduced, was an undoubte...</span></span></span>

The intelligence gained can extend far beyond the literal text of messages. Even when content cannot be decrypted, analysts may learn from metadata and [traffic analysis]({{ 'traffic-analysis/' | relative_url }}): who communicates with whom, when a network becomes unusually active, where transmitters are located, which headquarters controls a unit, or whether a formation is preparing to move. Direction finding and communications patterns can therefore remain valuable alongside code-breaking, although their usefulness also declines when targets alter frequencies, routes, schedules or transmission discipline. The US National Security Agency’s historical account highlights both cryptanalysis and sophisticated direction finding as major parts of the cryptologic record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/History/Cryptologic-History/Center-Cryptologic-History/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center Cryptologic HistoryFrom the outstanding cryptanalysis of the VENONA project, which identified numerous Soviet agents in the Uni...</span></span></span>

[VENONA]({{ 'venona/' | relative_url }}) provides a different example. The programme examined encrypted Soviet diplomatic and intelligence communications intercepted during and after the Second World War. Its partial success arose because some one-time-pad key material had been reused—an implementation failure in a system that is secure when used correctly. The resulting decrypts helped identify Soviet intelligence activity, but the source had to be protected because revealing the underlying weakness would warn the target and could expose investigations, intercept holdings and analytical methods.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/Helpful-Links/NSA-FOIA/Declassification-Transparency-Initiatives/FOIA-Reports-and-Releases/FOIA-Reports-and-Releases-List/igphoto/2003550784/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[NSA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">United States Cryptologic History Series VI Volume 5 1952-...7 MAY 52 WITH A VENONA C-N RELEASE DATE 03-13-2025. The central mechanism is therefore cumulative. A cryptologic capability may depend on several concealed facts at once:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* that particular communications are being intercepted;
* that a supposedly secure system contains a weakness;
* that analysts recognise recurring formats, names or operating habits;
* that enough historical traffic has been stored to exploit a later breakthrough;
* and that the resulting intelligence is being used without making the source obvious.

</div>

Protecting only the algorithm or machine is not enough. A target can close the channel simply by learning which part of its communications practice has become readable.

## How targets adapt

A target that suspects compromise has several rapid defensive options. It can replace keys, equipment or software; reduce radio use; move sensitive discussions to couriers or face-to-face meetings; impose stricter message procedures; change network architecture; investigate insiders; or deliberately transmit deceptive material. In digital systems, it may patch the weakness, reimage machines, rotate credentials, revoke certificates and search historical logs for signs of intrusion.

This makes intelligence use a problem of **gain versus exposure**. Acting immediately on every intercepted message may save lives or prevent damage, but conspicuously precise action can reveal that the adversary’s communications are being read. Intelligence organisations therefore try to disguise the origin of information, corroborate it through other sources or act in ways that do not expose the full precision of the access.

The same logic explains [compartmentation]({{ 'compartmentation/' | relative_url }}) inside a black project. A policymaker may receive an assessment without learning the exact cryptologic source. An operational commander may be told that a convoy is expected without being shown the intercepted message. A partner government may receive selected intelligence while the collection method remains restricted. These controls reduce the number of people whose actions, errors or disclosures could alert the target.

Adaptation is not always complete or immediate. Replacing widely deployed equipment can be slow, and organisations frequently fail to apply available security updates. Targets may also misunderstand what has been compromised, changing one procedure while leaving another exploitable. Yet the defender often has an inherent advantage once the weakness is identified: eliminating a vulnerable service or changing credentials may take hours, whereas discovering and developing the original access may have taken months or years.

Cyber operations compress this cycle further. A previously unknown software flaw—a “zero-day” vulnerability—is valuable partly because the vendor has not yet issued a patch and defenders do not know what indicators to seek. Once disclosed, the vendor can repair the code, security companies can write detection rules and network operators can hunt for related activity. CISA’s reporting on commonly exploited vulnerabilities shows that attackers move quickly around newly revealed weaknesses, while its guidance repeatedly urges organisations to prioritise patching flaws known to be under active exploitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa24-317a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cisa.gov">[CISA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cisa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cisa.gov.</span></span></span>

A cyber capability may also depend on secrecy beyond the vulnerability itself. Exposure can reveal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the malware’s command-and-control infrastructure;
* the digital certificates or accounts used to disguise it;
* the way it persists after a restart;
* the data it searches for;
* the countries, industries or individuals being targeted;
* and the technical signatures by which defenders can identify earlier intrusions.

</div>

Once these details become public, defenders can block domains, seize servers, invalidate credentials, search archived telemetry and reconstruct the operator’s broader campaign. Disclosure can therefore destroy current access and illuminate past operations at the same time.

## When a cyber secret escapes

The 2017 disclosure of the exploit commonly known as EternalBlue demonstrates the difference between a secret capability and a conventional weapon. EternalBlue exploited weaknesses in Microsoft’s Server Message Block protocol. Microsoft issued its MS17-010 security bulletin and patches in March 2017; the Shadow Brokers released a collection of alleged US intelligence tools the following month. In May, WannaCry used the exposed technique to spread rapidly through vulnerable Windows systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security-updates/securitybulletins/2017/ms17-010" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: learn.microsoft.com">[microsoft.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">learn.microsoft.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Learn Microsoft Security Bulletin MS17-010</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Learn Microsoft Security Bulletin MS17-010</span></span></span>

CERT-EU reported that the exploit had become available through the Shadow Brokers release and that WannaCry affected more than 200,000 computers worldwide, including systems associated with British hospitals. The episode showed both sides of the disclosure mechanism. For patched systems, the capability’s value sharply declined because the vulnerable route had been closed. For unpatched systems, however, publication transformed a restricted intelligence technique into a reusable tool for criminals and hostile actors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cert.europa.eu/static/SecurityAdvisories/2017/CERT-EU-SA2017-012.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cert.europa.eu">[cert.europa.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cert.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on europa.eu.</span></span></span>

This is an important qualification to the idea that digital secrets simply “vanish overnight”. Disclosure does not make an exploit disappear everywhere. It changes the environment around it. The original operator loses exclusivity; vendors and defenders gain the information needed to respond; other attackers gain a tested method; and vulnerable legacy systems may remain exposed for years. The capability becomes noisier, easier to attribute and less suitable for discreet intelligence collection, even while becoming more dangerous as a widely copied weapon.

The case also illustrates why cyber arsenals create risks that aerospace black projects do not. A stolen aircraft design does not instantly place a working aircraft in thousands of criminal hands. Leaked exploit code can be copied almost without cost, modified and deployed across borders. The intelligence service is therefore protecting not only its own access, but custody of a capability that may endanger friendly governments, companies and public infrastructure if it escapes.

## Disclosure risk

Governments face a genuine conflict when they discover an unknown vulnerability in widely used technology. Reporting it to the vendor allows a patch to protect everyone using the product, including domestic government and critical-infrastructure systems. Keeping it secret may permit intelligence collection, military access, counterintelligence or a law-enforcement operation against a high-value target.

The United States formalised this trade-off through the Vulnerabilities Equities Process. Its published 2017 charter states that the default public interest lies in disclosing newly discovered vulnerabilities, unless an overriding intelligence, law-enforcement or national-security reason justifies temporary restriction. It also recognises intermediate choices, such as sharing mitigations with selected organisations, imposing limits on government use or informing allies at a classified level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trump White House Archives Vulnerabilities Equities Policy and Process</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House Archives Vulnerabilities Equities Policy and Process</span></span></span>

The decision cannot be reduced to “offence versus defence”. Officials must consider how common the affected technology is, whether another party is likely to discover the flaw, how easily the vulnerability can be exploited, what damage disclosure or retention might cause, whether a vendor can produce an effective patch, and how important the prospective intelligence access is. A weakness in a specialised foreign military device presents a different risk from one in a networking component used by hospitals, banks and government departments worldwide.

Secrecy also complicates [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}). Public oversight bodies can examine legal authority, safeguards and broad categories of interception or equipment interference, but detailed debate about individual tools may itself expose the capability. British law, for example, provides statutory frameworks for interception and equipment interference alongside oversight by judicial and investigatory authorities, while restricting disclosures that could prejudice national security or the continued work of intelligence services.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/interception-of-communications-code-of-practice-2022/interception-of-communications-code-of-practice-accessible" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Interception of communications code of practice (accessible</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interception of communications code of practice (accessible</span></span></span>

That tension is unavoidable rather than fully solvable. Excessive disclosure can terminate productive access and teach targets how to evade surveillance. Excessive secrecy can conceal weak risk management, prevent affected vendors from protecting users and magnify the damage if a tool is stolen or independently discovered. The appropriate question is therefore not whether secrecy is inherently justified, but what exactly it is preserving, how long the advantage is likely to last and who bears the risk while it remains hidden.

## Why these secrets are unusually perishable

Cryptologic and cyber black projects protect advantages that frequently exist only in relation to a target’s mistaken belief. The target believes its cipher is sound, its administrators are trustworthy, its server is patched, its network is clean or its communications pattern is unobserved. The secret programme converts that false confidence into intelligence.

Once the false belief is corrected, the mechanism changes. The cipher is replaced. The vulnerable service is disabled. The compromised device is rebuilt. Communications move to another channel. Detection signatures spread among defenders. Even when access survives, it may become less reliable because the target now expects surveillance and can practise deception.

This is why digital secrecy is not merely a shield around an expensive development programme. It is an active component of the capability. The useful asset is often not the code-breaking equation, exploit or implant by itself, but the period during which the adversary does not know that it is vulnerable. In that setting, one disclosure can erase years of patient work—and, in the cyber realm, can simultaneously hand the underlying technique to everyone else.
