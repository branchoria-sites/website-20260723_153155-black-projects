## Introduction

One of the most persistent features of government secrecy is that information often remains classified long after the original reason for protecting it has disappeared. Within the world of [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), this creates a paradox: technologies become obsolete, programmes are cancelled, adversaries change and engineering methods are superseded, yet many related documents remain hidden for decades. The result is not always a deliberate effort to conceal history. More often, it reflects administrative incentives, fragmented ownership of records and declassification systems that struggle to keep pace with the volume and complexity of classified archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

This matters because prolonged secrecy affects more than historians. It complicates democratic oversight, fuels speculation about hidden programmes and weakens confidence that governments distinguish between information that genuinely requires protection and information that simply has not been reviewed. Understanding why obsolete secrets survive helps explain one important source of over-classification and the wider [trust]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}) problem surrounding black projects.

## How classification outlives the original threat

Classification systems are designed around the principle that information should remain secret only while disclosure would damage national security. In the United States, Executive Order 13526 explicitly requires information to be [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) "as soon as it no longer meets the standards for classification", and most permanently valuable classified records are scheduled for automatic declassification after 25 years unless they qualify for specific exemptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

In practice, however, several mechanisms allow secrecy to persist far beyond the original operational need.

First, technical information rarely ages uniformly. A weapons programme from the 1970s may contain engineering details that are obsolete, alongside [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}), cryptographic methods, foreign liaison relationships or testing procedures that agencies still believe could reveal enduring capabilities or vulnerabilities. Rather than separating the sensitive portions, reviewers may keep the entire document classified because disentangling the information requires time and specialist knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

Second, classification decisions tend to be conservative. The consequences of releasing genuinely sensitive information can be severe, while the institutional cost of leaving harmless material classified is often much lower. Faced with uncertainty, reviewers frequently err on the side of continued protection rather than disclosure.

Third, many records belong to multiple agencies. A single black project file might include contributions from military organisations, intelligence agencies, [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) and allied governments. Before release, each organisation with an interest in the document may need to review it. These referral processes can take years, especially when agencies disagree about what remains sensitive. Executive Order 13526 specifically provides procedures for handling such referrals because they represent a recurring obstacle in declassification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

## Why programme offices delay file review

The largest obstacle to declassification is often organisational rather than technical.

Programme offices are created to develop, operate and support active capabilities, not to prepare historical records for public release. Once a black project ends, responsibility for its archives may pass through several reorganisations, mergers or office closures. Decades later, identifying who has authority to approve declassification can itself become difficult.

Resource allocation creates another problem. Agencies generally receive stronger incentives to protect classified information than to release it. Reviewing decades of archived documents demands experienced personnel who understand both the original technology and current security risks. Those experts are usually assigned to active missions instead.

As a result, declassification competes poorly against operational priorities. Backlogs accumulate even when agencies formally support greater transparency.

The process is further slowed because reviewers must examine documents individually or in carefully defined series rather than assuming that age alone removes all sensitivity. Complex technical records often require page-by-page assessment to determine whether specific passages still reveal intelligence sources, military capabilities or protected design concepts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

This creates an institutional asymmetry:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Classifying a new document is usually quick.
* Declassifying an old document often requires multiple specialists, legal review and coordination across agencies.
* Few organisations are rewarded for accelerating release, while mistakes that expose genuinely sensitive information receive intense scrutiny.

</div>

The predictable outcome is a growing stockpile of records awaiting review.

## Why automatic declassification is less automatic than it sounds

Automatic declassification is frequently misunderstood as meaning that records simply become public after a fixed number of years.

The reality is more complicated.

Executive Order 13526 establishes automatic declassification after 25 years for permanently valuable records, but it also permits exemptions where disclosure could still reasonably harm national security. These include protecting confidential intelligence sources, ongoing intelligence methods, certain weapons-related information and information restricted by treaty or statute. Agencies may also request file-series exemptions for categories of records that almost invariably contain exempt information, delaying their release beyond the standard timetable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

Consequently, the existence of very old classified records does not necessarily indicate [improper secrecy]({{ 'improper-secrecy/' | relative_url }}). Some categories legitimately retain sensitivity for many decades.

The difficulty lies elsewhere: broad exemptions and administrative delays can result in large collections remaining closed even when substantial portions no longer present meaningful security risks. Historical files often contain a mixture of enduring secrets and information whose operational relevance disappeared long ago.

## What risk-based declassification would change

Many scholars, archivists and transparency advocates argue that declassification should focus less on document age alone and more on realistic contemporary risk.

A genuinely risk-based approach would ask whether disclosure today would provide an actual advantage to a modern adversary, rather than whether the information was sensitive when originally produced.

In practical terms, this could include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* separating obsolete technical information from still-sensitive intelligence sources or methods;
* updating classification guidance more frequently to reflect technological change;
* prioritising review of historically significant black-project records whose operational value has clearly expired;
* expanding digital review tools that help identify genuinely sensitive passages instead of treating entire files as uniformly classified.

</div>

Executive Order 13526 already requires agencies to conduct periodic reviews of their classification guidance to ensure it reflects current circumstances and identifies information that no longer requires protection. The challenge has been implementing these principles consistently across vast archives containing millions of pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National ArchivesDecember 29, 2009...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

## Why obsolete secrecy matters for trust in black projects

For black projects, excessive secrecy creates costs that extend beyond the historical record.

When governments continue withholding information whose original military value has plainly diminished, outsiders often struggle to distinguish between necessary protection and institutional habit. Every delayed release reinforces the perception that secrecy exists primarily to avoid scrutiny rather than to prevent genuine harm.

Later declassifications frequently show that many once-secret programmes were technically impressive but historically understandable. Yet because records remained hidden for decades, public debate often filled the gap with speculation. That dynamic can make future official claims about necessary secrecy less persuasive, even when current programmes genuinely require protection.

The challenge is therefore not eliminating secrecy but keeping it aligned with changing reality. Information whose disclosure would still damage national security deserves continued protection. Information that survives classification only because review systems are slow, fragmented or poorly incentivised contributes little to security while imposing growing costs on transparency, [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) and public confidence.
