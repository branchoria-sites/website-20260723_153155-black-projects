## Introduction

[Black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) create a genuine democratic dilemma: secrecy can be essential to preserve a military or intelligence advantage, but secrecy that shields a programme from meaningful scrutiny can also conceal waste, illegality, strategic error or institutional self-interest. The answer is not to make every sensitive capability public. It is to ensure that secrecy limits what adversaries can learn without preventing authorised representatives, auditors, inspectors and courts from asking whether the programme is lawful, affordable, necessary and competently managed.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_secrecy_accountabili_6ccbcd-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Accountability" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters because [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}) and democratic accountability are not the same thing. A stealth system’s technical characteristics may properly remain classified, while its broad purpose, total cost, legal authority and oversight arrangements can still be tested. The strongest model is therefore not “maximum transparency”, but controlled accountability: narrow classification, cleared scrutiny, independent access to evidence, recorded decisions and eventual declassification when the danger has passed.

## Why secrecy can be legitimate

Some black-project information cannot be released without damaging the capability it describes. Details of radar signatures, sensor ranges, communications methods, vulnerabilities, deployment assumptions or [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}) may enable an adversary to counter a system before it becomes operational. In such cases, secrecy is not merely administrative caution; it is part of the capability itself.

The legitimate case is strongest when disclosure would produce a specific and foreseeable security harm. A reconnaissance platform may become less effective once its operating altitude or sensor limits are known. A cyber or cryptographic capability may become useless when a target understands how access was obtained. Even an apparently harmless budget detail can reveal the scale, maturity or strategic priority of a hidden programme when combined with contract awards, test activity and specialist recruitment.

Democratic governments therefore permit specially protected programmes. In the United States, Department of Defense Special Access Programs impose restrictions beyond ordinary classification. Federal law requires the Secretary of Defense to report annually to the congressional defence committees on these programmes, including requested funding and programme-level information, and to provide separate notice and justification for newly designated programmes. The law also requires notification when their classification status is to change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

This statutory structure reflects an important principle: secrecy from the public does not automatically mean secrecy from the legislature. A programme may be hidden from foreign intelligence services, commercial competitors and general political debate while remaining visible to selected representatives with appropriate clearances.

Aggregate disclosure can also preserve accountability without exposing operational detail. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence publishes topline figures for the National Intelligence Program and Military Intelligence Program while withholding the detailed allocations beneath them. The National Intelligence Program’s requested total was first publicly disclosed under a congressional requirement, showing that useful fiscal transparency can be introduced without publishing individual secret programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[odni.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ic budget</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National Intelligence Program budget request was first publicly...Read more...</span></span></span>

The key test is necessity. Classification is defensible when it protects a capability, source, method or vulnerability. It becomes harder to justify when it mainly protects an institution from criticism.

## When secrecy defeats accountability

The danger begins when the same officials who design, fund and operate a programme also control nearly all information about its performance. Outsiders cannot challenge assumptions they are not permitted to see, and insiders may have incentives to preserve budgets, careers or institutional prestige.

US classification rules formally recognise this risk. Executive Order 13526 states that information must not be classified to conceal violations of law, inefficiency or administrative error, prevent embarrassment, restrain competition, or delay the release of information that does not require national-security protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">order to: (1) conceal violations of law, inefficiency, or administrative error; (2) prevent embarrassment to a person, organization, or...</span></span></span> The prohibition is clear in principle; the difficulty is enforcing it inside highly compartmented programmes where few people can inspect the underlying evidence.

### The information monopoly

Oversight is weakest when a watchdog depends on the organisation being examined to decide what the watchdog may know. A parliamentary committee may possess formal jurisdiction yet remain ineffective if programme officials can delay briefings, narrow access, exclude specialist staff or provide only selected evidence.

The US Government Accountability Office, Congress’s principal audit institution, has described comparable limits in intelligence oversight. Although GAO possesses broad evaluative authority, it has reported legal and practical constraints on reviewing CIA activities, including lack of access to some accounts and an inability to compel access to certain foreign-intelligence information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-01-975t" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Observations on GAO Access to Information on CIA...GAO faces both legal and practical limitations on its ability to review these prog...</span></span></span> A right to ask questions is therefore not enough; an oversight body must also have an enforceable route to records, personnel and financial data.

Recent GAO work has shown that even established oversight offices can lose effectiveness when inspections stop or recommendations are not tracked. In a 2024 review of Department of Defense intelligence oversight, GAO warned that ending routine component inspections and failing to monitor earlier recommendations could prevent senior leaders from obtaining an accurate picture of intelligence activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-24-106190.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao 24 106190</span></span></span>

The problem is structural rather than merely personal. Most officials inside a black project may act conscientiously, yet the system still produces an imbalance: programme managers know far more than the people authorising funds or assessing legality. Without independent access, elected representatives may be presented with a controlled narrative rather than a complete basis for judgement.

### Cost and performance can disappear behind classification

Secrecy can be especially damaging in acquisition programmes because technical uncertainty, optimistic forecasting and contractor dependence are already difficult to manage. Classification reduces the number of people who can compare estimates, challenge milestones or identify duplication.

Congressional concern about this problem is longstanding. A 1992 GAO review noted dissatisfaction with both the expanding number of Department of Defense Special Access Programs and the use of special-access controls for large, expensive acquisition programmes. It found that oversight had increased, but the concern itself was revealing: additional protection was being applied not only to small experimental efforts but to programmes involving substantial public expenditure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span></span></span>

Ordinary major defence programmes are subject to cost, schedule and performance reporting intended to support congressional scrutiny. GAO has described such information as a central element of acquisition oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-22-104687.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span> When comparable information is restricted inside a black programme, scrutiny must be reproduced within classified channels rather than abandoned. Otherwise secrecy changes from a security measure into a management exemption.

Historical failures show the stakes. The classified Future Imagery Architecture satellite programme was cancelled after serious technical and cost problems, with public reporting placing the loss in the billions of dollars. Contemporary analysis argued that restrictions on access meant classified acquisition programmes generally received less scrutiny than unclassified ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/2009/08/pentagon-secret-budget-tops-35-billion" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pentagon Secret Budget Tops $35 Billion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Pentagon Secret Budget Tops $35 Billion</span></span></span> The lesson is not that secret technology inevitably fails. It is that technical secrecy does not eliminate the need for independent cost testing, milestone review and the possibility of cancellation.

### Past abuses explain why trust is insufficient

Modern intelligence oversight in the United States was shaped by evidence that secrecy and executive assurances alone were inadequate. The Senate’s [Church Committee]({{ 'church-committee/' | relative_url }}) uncovered abuses involving several federal agencies, including the CIA, FBI and National Security Agency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/investigations/church-committee.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: senate.gov">[U.S. Senate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on senate.gov.</span></span></span> Its work led to recommendations intended to place intelligence activity within a constitutional system of accountability and helped drive the creation of permanent congressional intelligence committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.senate.gov/about/resources/pdf/church-committee-full-citations.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: senate.gov">[U.S. Senate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Senate The Church Committee</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Senate The Church Committee</span></span></span>

That history is directly relevant to black projects. Democratic control cannot rest on the assumption that national-security bodies will always recognise and correct their own excesses. Secrecy removes many of the normal corrective mechanisms—public criticism, competitive analysis, investigative reporting and open litigation—so stronger internal and legislative safeguards are needed, not weaker ones.

## What the public is entitled to know

Meaningful accountability does not require publishing engineering drawings or operational plans. It does require enough information for citizens to understand the broad commitments being made in their name.

At minimum, the public should normally be able to know:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* the legal authority under which secret programmes are established;
* the aggregate scale of classified expenditure;
* which institutions are responsible for authorisation and review;
* whether independent auditors and inspectors have full access;
* the general rules governing classification and declassification;
* whether serious failures, unlawful conduct or major cost overruns have occurred, once disclosure can be made safely.

</div>

This information allows political debate about priorities without revealing the details needed to defeat a capability. Voters may reasonably be denied the operating frequency of a sensor, but they should not be denied all knowledge that billions of pounds or dollars are being committed to a particular strategic mission.

The distinction can be expressed as **capability secrecy versus governance transparency**. Capability secrecy protects how a system works, where it is vulnerable and how it will be used. Governance transparency explains who authorised it, under what law, at what broad cost and with what safeguards. The former may need to remain narrow and durable; the latter should be withheld only when even high-level disclosure would cause identifiable harm.

[Overclassification]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}) weakens this distinction. The US Public Interest Declassification Board has warned that the classification system keeps too many secrets for too long and can obstruct both information sharing within government and communication with the public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://transforming-classification.blogs.archives.gov/2012/11/30/322/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: transforming-classification.blogs.archives.gov">[Transforming Classification]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">transforming-classification.blogs.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span> Excessive classification can also dilute protection by spreading security resources across material of unequal importance. When almost everything is treated as exceptionally sensitive, reviewers struggle to identify what truly requires exceptional protection.

## Oversight compromises that preserve security

The practical challenge is to create scrutiny rigorous enough to expose error while keeping genuinely sensitive information out of public circulation. Several compromises are more credible than a simple choice between total disclosure and executive secrecy.

### Cleared legislative committees

A small cross-party group of legislators can receive full classified briefings, inspect budgets and question programme leaders. US law requires reporting on defence Special Access Programs to designated congressional committees, while covert-action law requires presidential findings and reporting to the congressional intelligence committees, subject in exceptional circumstances to more restricted notification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

This arrangement works only when committee members and cleared staff have sufficient time, expertise and access. Restricted membership can protect secrets, but excessive restriction can also concentrate knowledge in so few people that scrutiny becomes shallow. Members must be able to use technical, legal and financial specialists who are independent of the programme under review.

The United Kingdom uses a related model through Parliament’s Intelligence and Security Committee. Its remit includes scrutiny of intelligence policy, administration, expenditure and certain operations. Its reports are submitted to the Prime Minister, who may seek the exclusion of material on national-security grounds before publication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isc.independent.gov.uk/reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isc.independent.gov.uk">[Intelligence and Security Committee]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isc.independent.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on independent.gov.uk.</span></span></span> This permits classified examination followed by a public account, although the executive’s role in resourcing the committee and reviewing its publications creates continuing questions about institutional independence.

### Independent inspectors and auditors

Inspectors general, national audit institutions and specialist acquisition reviewers can examine records without publishing sensitive contents. Their public reports may omit operational details while still identifying weak controls, unreliable data, conflicts of interest or unimplemented recommendations.

The safeguard is strongest when access is guaranteed by law rather than negotiated case by case. An auditor that can see only what programme managers choose to provide cannot reliably certify legality or value for money. Oversight bodies also need authority to report obstruction directly to the legislature.

The distinction between access and publication is crucial. A watchdog may need unrestricted access to classified evidence even though its eventual public report contains only findings, broad explanations and redacted recommendations. Security is preserved because the evidence remains controlled; accountability is preserved because programme officials do not possess a veto over independent examination.

### Layered budget transparency

A practical budget compromise uses several levels of disclosure:

1. **Public toplines** show the overall scale of classified spending.
2. **Classified programme accounts** give authorised committees the detailed allocation.
3. **Independent audits** test whether expenditure is lawful and accurately recorded.
4. **Retrospective disclosure** [reveals]({{ 'reveals/' | relative_url }}) historical costs and outcomes after operational sensitivity declines.

The US publication of aggregate intelligence-budget figures demonstrates the first layer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[odni.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ic budget</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National Intelligence Program budget request was first publicly...Read more...</span></span></span> The Special Access Program reporting statute provides part of the second.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span> The harder task is ensuring that auditors possess the same programme visibility as the executive agencies they examine.

Layered transparency is preferable to a binary system in which a budget line is either completely public or almost wholly invisible. It gives citizens a sense of scale, gives legislators programme detail and allows auditors to test the underlying accounts.

### Scheduled review and declassification

Secrecy should not renew itself automatically. Programmes need periodic review to determine whether the original harm from disclosure still exists. Protection may remain justified for particular components—such as sensor design or intelligence sources—while the project’s existence, cost, purpose or historical outcome can be released.

Declassification has democratic value beyond satisfying curiosity. It allows historians, engineers and policymakers to compare original claims with actual results. It can reveal whether oversight worked, whether officials understated risk and whether lessons were carried into later programmes. Without retrospective visibility, each generation of black projects is evaluated largely through internal institutional memory, which may itself be selective.

A credible system therefore requires decision records: who approved classification, what damage was anticipated, when the decision must be reviewed and which elements can be separated for earlier release. The burden should gradually shift. At the beginning of a genuinely sensitive programme, officials may reasonably presume secrecy for critical technical details. As time passes, continued withholding should require a renewed explanation rather than bureaucratic inertia.

## The limits of courts and public challenges

Courts can provide another form of independent control, but national-security litigation presents an acute problem: hearing the evidence may itself expose the information being protected. Governments may invoke doctrines such as the state-secrets privilege to prevent disclosure of material said to threaten national security. The US Department of Justice’s policy requires high-level review before the privilege is asserted and states that it should be used only when disclosure would risk significant harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/speech/statement-attorney-general-eric-holder-assertion-state-secrets-privilege-shubert-v-obama" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Department of Justice Statement of Attorney General Eric Holder on Assertion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Justice Statement of Attorney General Eric Holder on Assertion</span></span></span>

The privilege may be legitimate where no protective procedure can prevent dangerous disclosure. Yet it can also block claimants from testing government conduct. If a case is dismissed before evidence is examined, the judiciary may never determine whether an alleged abuse occurred.

The better compromise is procedural rather than absolute: cleared judges, closed hearings, security-vetted counsel where permitted, redacted evidence and unclassified summaries. These devices cannot solve every case, but they reduce the risk that the executive’s classification claim becomes conclusive merely because it has been asserted.

Judicial restraint should also be matched by stronger legislative and inspector-general access. Where public litigation cannot proceed safely, another independent institution must be able to examine the same conduct. Otherwise secrecy creates an accountability gap rather than a transfer of scrutiny.

## A workable democratic test

A black project can be considered legitimately secret only when the secrecy surrounding it meets more than a national-security label. The governing system should be able to answer five questions.

**Is the harm specific?** Officials should identify what disclosure would enable an adversary to learn or do. Vague references to sensitivity should not be sufficient.

**Is the secret narrowly drawn?** Technical details may require protection even when the programme’s broad purpose, legal basis or aggregate cost does not.

**Can an independent body see the whole picture?** At least one institution outside the operational chain should have enforceable access to programme records, personnel and expenditure.

**Can mistakes reach elected decision-makers?** Serious cost growth, unlawful conduct, safety failures and strategic changes should trigger mandatory reporting rather than discretionary briefing.

**Will secrecy expire or be reviewed?** Continued classification should require periodic justification, with separable information released when protection is no longer necessary.

These tests do not remove the tension between security and democracy. They make it governable. Black projects may need to operate outside public view, but they should never operate outside law, accounting or review. The democratic objective is not to eliminate secrecy; it is to prevent secrecy from becoming a self-authorising form of power.
