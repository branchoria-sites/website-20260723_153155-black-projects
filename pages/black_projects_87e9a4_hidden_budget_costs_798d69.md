## Introduction

Classified funding can protect a black project from adversaries, but it can also weaken the ordinary pressures that keep ambitious programmes affordable. When budget lines, technical assumptions and contractor performance are visible only to a small circle, fewer people can challenge optimistic estimates, compare alternatives or identify a failing programme before large sums have been committed.

Secrecy does not automatically produce waste. Classified programmes remain subject to internal budgeting, audits, milestone reviews and congressional scrutiny. Historical reviews have also found that their cost and schedule problems are not necessarily worse than those of comparable open programmes. The central risk is subtler: secrecy narrows the market for criticism. It reduces the number of independent officials, competitors and legislators able to test the figures, while making it harder for the public to distinguish a disciplined programme from one being sustained by institutional momentum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — GAO reviewed the Department of Defense&#x27;s (DOD) management and oversight of its special access acquisition programs...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

## What budget opacity changes

A [black budget]({{ 'black-budget/' | relative_url }}) is not usually a pot of money wholly invisible to government. In the United States, classified expenditure is considered through restricted budget documents and classified annexes, while aggregate intelligence totals are publicly released. For example, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence disclosed an $81.9 billion request for the National Intelligence Program for the 2026 financial year, but programme-level allocations remained classified. That distinction matters for cost control. An aggregate total can show whether overall classified spending is rising, but it cannot reveal whether one project has doubled in estimated cost, whether another has missed successive milestones, or whether money is being shifted from a troubled programme to keep it alive. Those details may be available to authorised officials, but not to the wider community of analysts, journalists, competing suppliers and legislators who scrutinise ordinary public programmes.

Classified appropriations may also be concealed within broader or deliberately uninformative budget lines. A 1992 Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office review found that the US Department of Defense used the same basic resource-justification and execution processes for special-access acquisitions as for ordinary programmes, but disguised their funding in unclassified budget material for security reasons. The concealment protected programme identities, yet it also meant that public documents could not support meaningful programme-by-programme evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — GAO reviewed the Department of Defense&#x27;s (DOD) management and oversight of its special access acquisition programs...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

The result is an accountability asymmetry. Programme managers and contractors know far more than outsiders, while many potential challengers cannot inspect the assumptions behind a cost estimate. Even within government, access may arrive late or be restricted by [compartmentation]({{ 'compartmentation/' | relative_url }}). Earlier GAO examinations found that some oversight organisations had difficulty obtaining timely access to special-access programmes and that incomplete reporting by military departments hampered central supervision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gao.justia.com/department-of-defense/1989/5/special-access-programs-nsiad-89-133/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.justia.com">[gao.justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia GAO Reports - NSIAD-89-133 - Special Access Programs - Department of DefenseMay 4, 1989 — GAO found that: (1) DOD has increased th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 4, 1989</span></span></span>

This does not mean that disclosure of every line item would be sensible. Publishing the budget profile of a stealth demonstrator, reconnaissance satellite or cryptographic programme could reveal development stage, production tempo or operational priorities. The practical cost-control question is therefore not whether secrecy should disappear, but whether enough cleared reviewers receive sufficiently detailed and timely information to challenge the programme before its problems become irreversible.

## Why secrecy can delay bad news

Large defence programmes often begin with uncertain technology, incomplete designs and demanding performance targets. Reliable control depends on recognising that uncertainty early, placing realistic contingency in the estimate and updating the baseline when evidence changes. GAO guidance describes credible cost estimates, integrated schedules and earned-value data—measurements comparing completed work with planned cost and progress—as core tools for controlling complex federal programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2024-08/gao-20-195g.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

A secret programme can use all of those tools. Its vulnerability is that the evidence may circulate within a very small reporting chain. Compartmentation can separate technical [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}), financial reviewers and operational sponsors who would normally challenge one another. It can also make comparisons with related programmes harder, because data from one compartment may not be available to analysts in another.

The Navy’s A-12 Avenger II illustrates the danger. The classified carrier-based stealth aircraft was developed under a $4.8 billion fixed-price incentive contract, a structure intended to place much of the overrun risk on the contractor team. By 1990, however, the contractors reported that the programme would exceed the contract ceiling by an amount they could not absorb, that the first flight would slip and that some performance requirements could not be met. The programme was cancelled in January 1991 before a prototype flew.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-91-98.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NSIAD-91-98 Navy A-12: Cost and Requirements</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-91-98 Navy A-12: Cost and Requirements</span></span></span>

The A-12’s failure was not caused by secrecy alone. It combined immature technology, weight and design difficulties, an aggressive schedule, unrealistic contractual assumptions and poor communication about the programme’s condition. Yet secrecy made the management environment less forgiving. Information was tightly compartmented, external technical challenge was limited, and senior decision-makers received the full scale of the trouble only after cost and schedule pressures had become severe. The case shows why transferring financial risk to a contractor is not a substitute for independent insight into whether the underlying plan is technically achievable.

The [B-2]({{ 'b-2/' | relative_url }}) bomber provides a related but less catastrophic example. GAO reported that major cost increases during its development arose primarily because manufacturing began with an incomplete design, material costs were underestimated and the production schedule was extended. A $2.6 billion increase between January and June 1989 was attributed chiefly to a one-year schedule extension. These are familiar acquisition failures rather than uniquely secret ones, but the B-2’s classification limited contemporary public understanding of the assumptions and trade-offs driving its escalating price.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF] BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF] BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span></span></span>

The lesson from both cases is that hidden budgets do not create a separate economics of procurement. They magnify ordinary risks by reducing the number of people able to detect them. An incomplete design remains an incomplete design; a weak estimate remains weak. Secrecy merely makes those weaknesses harder to contest outside the authorised chain.

## Contractor incentives inside a closed market

[Black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) frequently rely on a small group of cleared firms with secure facilities, specialised staff and experience handling restricted technology. These requirements are often unavoidable, but they shrink the pool of credible bidders. Once a programme has selected a contractor and embedded highly classified knowledge in its workforce and systems, replacing that contractor may become exceptionally expensive or operationally disruptive.

This can weaken the government’s bargaining position. A company may possess unique knowledge of the design, test data, manufacturing process and classified supply chain. Even when another supplier is technically capable, transferring the work could require new clearances, secure infrastructure and extensive disclosure of compartmented information. The threat of competition—the customer’s strongest protection against excessive price or poor performance—therefore becomes less credible.

Secrecy can also conceal the commercial significance of a contract. Investors may know that a defence company has received classified work without knowing its precise value, risk profile or delivery obligations. Other suppliers cannot see enough of the requirement to propose cheaper alternatives, while independent cost analysts cannot readily compare the contractor’s prices with those offered on similar programmes.

Contract type does not remove this structural imbalance. A fixed-price contract appears to protect the government because the contractor is expected to bear costs above the agreed ceiling. But if failure would destroy a strategically important capability, threaten a major supplier or leave the armed forces without an alternative, the government may still face pressure to renegotiate, provide additional funding or relax requirements. The A-12 demonstrated how quickly nominally transferred risk can return to the customer when the contractor can no longer perform under the original terms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-91-98.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NSIAD-91-98 Navy A-12: Cost and Requirements</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-91-98 Navy A-12: Cost and Requirements</span></span></span>

Cost-reimbursement contracts pose the opposite hazard. They are often appropriate for highly uncertain research because no bidder can sensibly promise a fixed total before the technology is understood. However, they place more financial risk on government and can reward additional effort rather than economical delivery unless milestones, incentives and audit rights are carefully designed.

The most effective safeguard is not a universally preferred contract type. It is a combination of realistic technical baselines, independent government cost estimates, access to contractor performance data and credible decision points at which the customer can stop, restructure or compete parts of the programme. In a black project, each safeguard must exist inside the classified environment rather than being assumed to arise from public scrutiny.

## Oversight: narrower, not necessarily absent

It is misleading to say that secret programmes receive no oversight. US law requires the Secretary of Defense to report annually to congressional defence committees on Special Access Programs, including the total amount requested and information on individual programmes. New designations must be reported and justified, with the current estimate of total programme cost among the required information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

Classified committees, inspectors general, departmental acquisition officials and authorised auditors can therefore examine information unavailable to the public. GAO’s 1992 review concluded that oversight of special-access acquisitions had increased and that Congress was receiving adequate information on the programmes examined. It also found that cost increases, delays and performance problems were broadly similar to those in non-special-access programmes, a useful caution against assuming that every hidden programme is unusually wasteful.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — GAO reviewed the Department of Defense&#x27;s (DOD) management and oversight of its special access acquisition programs...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

The same review nevertheless identified weak internal controls in areas including contracting, technology, communication and record-keeping, and found that special-access programmes had not always been monitored as closely as ordinary ones. Earlier GAO work had found limited central oversight, inconsistent criteria for creating programmes and difficulties even determining how many special-access programmes existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gao.justia.com/department-of-defense/1992/12/defense-acquisition-nsiad-93-78/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.justia.com">[gao.justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO found that: (1) DOD properly established its SAAP, but often did not strictly comply with DOD acquisition regulations since the regul...</span></span></span>

There is also a legal and procedural wrinkle. GAO reported in 2015 that statutes governing major defence acquisition programmes generally did not apply to classified programmes in the same way. Exemptions can be justified where standard reporting would expose sensitive information, but they remove common triggers for formal cost, schedule and performance reporting unless equivalent classified controls are deliberately installed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-15-192.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao</span></span></span>

Oversight quality therefore depends less on the formal existence of a committee than on four practical questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Access:** Do reviewers receive the clearances and programme information they need without avoidable delay?
* **Expertise:** Can they test technical and cost assumptions rather than merely receive briefings?
* **Continuity:** Are the same reviewers able to follow the programme over several years and recognise shifting baselines?
* **Authority:** Can they freeze funds, demand an independent estimate, change contract strategy or recommend cancellation?

</div>

A small, cleared oversight group may protect secrets better than a broad public process. It may also become overdependent on programme sponsors, particularly when members lack time or specialist staff. Classified briefings can inform legislators while still making it difficult for them to discuss concerns with colleagues, explain objections publicly or build support for cancelling a politically protected programme.

## Can secrecy ever improve cost control?

Secrecy sometimes helps a programme move faster and spend less. A tightly contained team can avoid public controversy, frequent requirement changes and coordination across a large bureaucracy. The best-known black aerospace organisations used small staffs, direct access to senior decision-makers and unusually short reporting chains. Those management features can reduce overhead and protect engineers from shifting institutional demands.

Yet these benefits come from organisational discipline, not from budget opacity itself. The same closed environment that protects a skilled team can also shield an unrealistic programme. Rapid decision-making is valuable when leaders receive honest information; it is dangerous when bad news is filtered upward or when advocates treat classification as a reason to avoid external challenge.

The most defensible model is selective secrecy. Operational details, signatures, vulnerabilities and exact capabilities can remain compartmented, while cleared overseers receive full cost estimates, confidence ranges, schedule data and contractor-performance measures. Programme identity may need protection in public documents, but aggregate disclosure should be supplemented by strong classified reporting to several independent centres of authority rather than one sponsor-controlled chain.

Independent cost teams are especially important. They should be organisationally separate from the office seeking approval, able to examine contractor data and required to state uncertainty rather than produce a single politically convenient figure. Periodic “zero-based” reviews can then ask whether the programme would still be approved if it were proposed at its current estimated cost, rather than allowing prior expenditure to justify further commitment.

Competition can also be preserved below the level of the complete system. Even where only one prime contractor can integrate a black platform, the government may compete subsystems, software, test services or production lots when security permits. Maintaining government ownership of technical data and interface standards can reduce dependence on a single supplier later in the programme.

Finally, classification decisions themselves should be reviewed. GAO found in the late 1980s that sensitivity was sometimes cited without demonstrating why ordinary security controls were inadequate. Unnecessary special-access restrictions increase administrative expense, reduce the available workforce and narrow oversight without adding equivalent protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gao.justia.com/department-of-defense/1988/5/special-access-programs-nsiad-88-152/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.justia.com">[gao.justia.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO Reports</span></span></span>

## The central trade-off

The cost-control problem in black projects is not that no one knows where the money goes. It is that too few independent people may know enough, early enough, to challenge where it is going. Aggregate disclosures and classified committee reports provide a degree of accountability, but they cannot fully reproduce the competitive and political pressure surrounding an open programme.

History also resists a simple verdict. Special-access programmes have delivered important capabilities, and GAO has not found that they invariably perform worse than ordinary acquisitions. At the same time, the A-12, B-2 and decades of oversight findings show that secrecy can conceal familiar warning signs: immature designs, unrealistic schedules, weak internal records, contractor dependence and estimates that assume technical success before it has been demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF] BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF] BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span></span></span>

A secret programme controls its costs only when its secrecy is matched by stronger internal challenge. The less scrutiny available outside the compartment, the more rigorous the classified scrutiny inside it must become.
