## Introduction

One of the clearest signs that secrecy has expanded beyond a consistent assessment of national-security harm is when different reviewers produce materially different redactions of the same document. In the context of [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) and other highly classified activities, governments often argue that withheld information must remain secret because disclosure would cause identifiable damage. Yet repeated releases of the same records sometimes reveal that one office concealed text another later released without apparent consequence, or that different agencies censored entirely different passages. These inconsistencies do not automatically prove bad faith or illegitimate secrecy, but they do raise an important question: if the same information can be judged both dangerous and harmless, how reliable is the underlying classification process?



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_overclassification_t_f052cc_redaction_inconsiste_87f626-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Redaction Conflicts illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_overclassification_t_f052cc_redaction_inconsiste_87f626-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_overclassification_t_f052cc_redaction_inconsiste_87f626-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The evidence suggests that inconsistent redactions usually reflect decentralised decision-making, differing institutional risk tolerance and changing interpretations of classification rules rather than a single coherent assessment of harm. That matters because public confidence in necessary secrecy depends not only on protecting genuine secrets but also on demonstrating that decisions are principled, repeatable and defensible.

## Records released by one authority but censored by another

The strongest evidence comes from side-by-side comparisons of successive releases rather than isolated complaints about censorship.

A widely cited example is a 1994 U.S. State Department memorandum concerning the Rwandan genocide. Between 2001 and 2012, Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) reviewers released four different versions of exactly the same document. Each version concealed different words and passages, while simultaneously revealing material another reviewer had previously blacked out. Every reviewer justified the decisions on national-security grounds, yet the pattern demonstrated that the supposedly fixed boundary between harmful and harmless information shifted dramatically depending on who performed the review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveFOIA Follies: FOIA Reviewers Declassify Same Rwanda Document Four Times, Creating New Secrets Each TimeApril 3...</span></span></span>

The National Security Archive has documented numerous similar cases in which:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* material removed in one release appeared unredacted in later releases;
* agencies reviewing the same records reached incompatible conclusions;
* repeated declassification reviews steadily restored information that earlier reviewers had claimed required continued protection; and
* identical information remained hidden in one document while appearing openly elsewhere in the government's own records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/foia/2019-04-18/redactions-declassified-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

The problem extends beyond isolated historical documents. The Archive's catalogue includes examples involving Cold War records, intelligence reports, diplomatic correspondence and inspector-general investigations in which different review [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) reached visibly different conclusions about releasability. Rather than demonstrating a stable standard, the releases reveal an evolving series of individual judgements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/foia/2019-04-18/redactions-declassified-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

## Why separate reviewers hide different passages

These inconsistencies do not necessarily mean that reviewers are acting arbitrarily. Several structural features of the classification system make divergent outcomes likely.

First, declassification authority is distributed. Documents frequently contain information that originated with multiple agencies. Under U.S. practice, each originating agency often retains authority over information it supplied, meaning one office may approve release while another insists on continued protection. The National Archives notes that transferred records can remain subject to restrictions imposed by originating agencies, and appeals may proceed through agency review before reaching the Interagency Security Classification Appeals Panel (ISCAP).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/declassification.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Declassified Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesDeclassified Records &#124; National ArchivesMay 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2020</span></span></span>

Second, reviewers operate under asymmetric incentives. Incorrectly releasing genuinely sensitive information can carry serious professional consequences, whereas excessive redaction is usually much less risky. When uncertainty exists, reviewers may therefore favour withholding information. This tendency encourages conservative decisions that differ from reviewer to reviewer rather than the application of a single objective threshold.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/foia/2019-04-18/redactions-declassified-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

Third, institutional knowledge changes over time. A reviewer examining a document twenty years after its creation may understand technological developments, diplomatic history or intelligence capabilities differently from an earlier reviewer. Information once regarded as revealing may later appear routine, while some previously overlooked details may acquire new significance. Executive Order 13526 anticipates periodic reassessment through mandatory declassification review rather than assuming that initial decisions remain permanently correct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/declassification.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Declassified Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesDeclassified Records &#124; National ArchivesMay 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2020</span></span></span>

## What conflicting redactions reveal about secrecy

The existence of inconsistent redactions does not demonstrate that all classified information is unnecessary. Instead, it [reveals]({{ 'reveals/' | relative_url }}) that classification often involves judgement rather than a purely mechanical application of rules.

Three observations emerge consistently from documented cases.

**Classification boundaries are less precise than official rhetoric sometimes suggests.** If different qualified reviewers repeatedly disagree over individual passages, the distinction between genuinely damaging disclosures and harmless historical information is often less clear-cut than public explanations imply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveFOIA Follies: FOIA Reviewers Declassify Same Rwanda Document Four Times, Creating New Secrets Each TimeApril 3...</span></span></span>

**Institutional caution frequently outweighs demonstrable harm.** Many later releases show that information once withheld can eventually be published without any identifiable damage to national security. That does not prove the original decision was unreasonable at the time, but it does illustrate how precautionary judgement can become embedded in classification practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/foia/2019-04-18/redactions-declassified-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

**Repeated review can expose inconsistency rather than merely reduce secrecy.** Each additional declassification review effectively creates an audit trail of changing institutional judgement. When different versions reveal incompatible redactions, they provide tangible evidence that at least some withholding decisions reflected reviewer discretion rather than universally recognised security requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB420/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security ArchiveFOIA Follies: FOIA Reviewers Declassify Same Rwanda Document Four Times, Creating New Secrets Each TimeApril 3...</span></span></span>

## How inconsistency weakens claims of necessary secrecy

For programmes associated with black projects, credibility is especially important because outside verification is inherently limited. Legislators, courts, historians and the public must often rely on official assurances that secrecy protects identifiable capabilities rather than simply preserving institutional preference.

Conflicting redactions weaken those assurances in several ways.

They encourage retrospective scepticism. Once readers discover that material previously described as too sensitive for release later appears openly, they naturally question whether other withheld passages are equally overstated.

They complicate oversight. Congressional investigators, journalists and historians comparing multiple releases must spend substantial effort determining whether differences reflect genuine security concerns, administrative error or evolving policy rather than analysing the underlying events themselves.

They blur the distinction between legitimate secrecy and over-classification. Executive Order 13526 expressly prohibits classification to conceal inefficiency, administrative error or embarrassment, yet inconsistent releases make it difficult for outside observers to determine whether continuing secrecy genuinely reflects national-security harm or merely differing institutional preferences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/declassification.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Declassified Records &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesDeclassified Records &#124; National ArchivesMay 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 28, 2020</span></span></span>

Perhaps most importantly, inconsistent redactions create a cumulative credibility problem. Citizens generally accept that some information about advanced military capabilities, [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}) and sensitive technologies must remain secret. Confidence erodes not because every redaction is disputed, but because visibly inconsistent decisions suggest that similar information is being treated differently without an equally visible rationale.

## Why inconsistency does not automatically prove improper secrecy

It is important not to overstate what conflicting redactions demonstrate.

Some differences arise because classification guidance changes legitimately over time. Intelligence sources may die, weapons systems may retire, diplomatic relationships may evolve and foreign governments may consent to disclosure years after records were created. Separate releases may also involve different legal authorities, exemptions or contextual information unavailable during earlier reviews.

Moreover, individual passages rarely exist in isolation. A sentence that appears harmless in one document may reveal sensitive relationships when combined with surrounding material in another. Agencies sometimes evaluate contextual risk rather than isolated words.

Nevertheless, the documented pattern of incompatible releases across multiple agencies and multiple decades indicates that reviewer judgement plays a larger role than official descriptions of classification sometimes acknowledge. For debates about black projects and government secrecy, that distinction matters. The issue is not whether secrecy is ever justified, but whether the process for deciding what truly requires protection remains sufficiently consistent to sustain public confidence. When the same information is alternately hidden and released by different authorities, the burden of explaining why those differences occurred becomes part of maintaining the credibility of official secrecy itself.
