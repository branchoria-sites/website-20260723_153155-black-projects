## Introduction

[Carve-out contracts]({{ 'carve-outs-95feed/' | relative_url }}) are an exceptional security mechanism used for some of the most sensitive defence programmes, particularly within Special Access Program (SAP) structures. Instead of following the normal industrial security oversight process, responsibility for inspecting and administering a contract is removed from the standard defence security organisation and assigned directly to the sponsoring government component. This arrangement reduces the number of people who know the contract exists, making it harder for outsiders—and even many cleared officials—to identify or connect the programme to a particular capability. At the same time, concentrating authority within a small sponsoring office can weaken familiar checks and balances. Government audits have repeatedly found that the very features that protect secrecy can also create blind spots in [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) if exceptional arrangements are not carefully justified and independently monitored.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/ggd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ggd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) Contracts &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 18, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 1983</span></span></span>

## How carve-out contracts work

In a conventional classified defence contract, the government assigns industrial security oversight to a designated security organisation. Historically this role was performed by the Defense Investigative Service (DIS), with successor responsibilities now largely carried out through the Defence Counterintelligence and Security Agency (DCSA) and other cognisant security authorities. These organisations conduct routine inspections, verify compliance with security regulations and provide an independent layer of oversight between the sponsoring agency and the contractor.

A carve-out contract deliberately changes that arrangement. The sponsoring department assumes security responsibility itself, relieving the normal industrial security organisation of its inspection role. The contract remains subject to security requirements, but those requirements are administered through a much narrower chain of authority. According to Department of Defense policy, this mechanism is intended only for cases where even knowledge that a particular contract exists—or that it is associated with a specific Special Access Program—is itself classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/afmc/publication/dodm5220.22v2_afman16-1406v2_afmcsup/dodm5220.22v2_afman16-1406v2_afmcsup.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-PublishingAdministrative Changes to AFMAN 16-1406_afmcsup, National Industrial Security Program:April 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2026</span></span></span>

Modern DoD policy therefore treats carve-outs as an exception rather than the default. [Approval]({{ 'approval/' | relative_url }}) requires additional justification beyond approval of the underlying Special Access Program, reflecting the principle that bypassing ordinary oversight should occur only when normal administrative handling would itself reveal sensitive information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/ggd-83-43.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GGD-83-43 Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) ContractsFebruary 18, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 1983</span></span></span>

## Why sponsors restrict ordinary inspection

The principal objective is not simply to protect technical data but to minimise programme visibility across government and industry.

Ordinary industrial security inspections inevitably expose information to additional personnel. Inspectors may learn contractor identities, facility locations, project relationships, document inventories or operational schedules. Even if inspectors never see the programme's technical details, repeated inspections can reveal patterns that allow knowledgeable observers to infer the existence or scale of a highly classified effort.

Sponsors therefore seek to reduce exposure by:

* limiting the number of organisations aware that the contract exists;
* preventing routine inspection records from identifying sensitive programmes;
* reducing administrative links between [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) and visible government offices;
* avoiding situations where ordinary security reporting unintentionally discloses classified associations.

This reflects a broader principle within compartmented security: protecting not only classified information itself but also information about who possesses it, where work is occurring and how different activities are connected. The less administrative visibility a programme generates, the more difficult it becomes to reconstruct its overall footprint from routine government records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/doddir/dod/ai26.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource ProgramInformation Security Supplement to DoD 5200.1-R...</span></span></span>

## Why exceptional channels create oversight risks

The same characteristics that improve secrecy can weaken independent oversight.

### Fewer independent reviewers

Traditional oversight relies on institutional separation. Security inspectors, contracting officials and programme managers perform different functions, allowing one group to identify weaknesses overlooked by another.

A carve-out compresses these roles. When the sponsoring office both manages the programme and oversees security compliance, there are fewer opportunities for independent challenge. GAO concluded that this reduction in external review increased the risk that deficiencies would remain undetected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/ggd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ggd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) Contracts &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 18, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 1983</span></span></span>

### Inconsistent security standards

GAO also found that growing numbers of carve-out contracts created practical difficulties for contractor security administrators. Different sponsoring organisations sometimes imposed differing procedures beyond standard industrial security requirements, increasing complexity and making consistent compliance more difficult across facilities handling multiple sensitive programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gao.justia.com/department-of-defense/1983/2/further-improvements-needed-in-department-of-defense-oversight-of-special-access-carve-out-contracts-ggd-83-43/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.justia.com">[Justia GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAOJustia GAO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia GAOJustia GAO Reports - GGD-83-43 - Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) C...</span></span></span>

### Reduced visibility into programme growth

When contracts operate outside ordinary inspection channels, central authorities may have only incomplete information about how many carve-outs exist or whether continued exceptional treatment remains justified.

During its 1983 review, GAO reported that the precise number of carve-out contracts was unknown, estimated that there were probably several thousand, and found cases where contracts appeared to have received carve-out status for reasons unrelated to genuine security necessity. The audit also identified instances where some carve-out contracts were apparently not inspected by anyone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gao.justia.com/department-of-defense/1983/2/further-improvements-needed-in-department-of-defense-oversight-of-special-access-carve-out-contracts-ggd-83-43/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.justia.com">[Justia GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAOJustia GAO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Justia GAOJustia GAO Reports - GGD-83-43 - Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) C...</span></span></span>

These findings did not imply that all carve-outs lacked oversight, but they demonstrated how decentralised administration could reduce visibility at the departmental level.

## A concrete example of the oversight problem

The risks identified by GAO became more tangible during later investigations into document control.

In a 1986 review of a contractor's special-access programme, GAO compared document accountability in ordinary classified programmes with controls used under a special-access contract. Regular classified work received periodic inspections from the Defence Investigative Service and was judged to be well managed. The special-access programme, however, suffered significant weaknesses in document accountability.

GAO concluded that independent semi-annual inspections might have detected deterioration much earlier. Although the sponsoring component maintained an on-site security representative, that official's responsibilities were divided among numerous security duties and did not provide the same independent verification normally supplied through routine industrial security inspections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/130581" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Controls Over Classified Documents for a Special Access Program at Lockheed Corporation &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 24, 1986...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 24, 1986</span></span></span>

The case illustrates the central oversight concern: replacing an established inspection system with programme-specific supervision does not automatically provide an equivalent level of independent scrutiny.

## How policy evolved to reduce the blind spot

Government policy has gradually narrowed the circumstances in which carve-outs may be used.

Changes introduced after GAO's reviews required clearer approval authorities, written security plans and explicit justification before granting carve-out status. Regulations also prohibited carve-out contracts that did not support properly approved Special Access Programs and required designated officials to oversee their administration. These reforms recognised that exceptional security measures needed their own governance mechanisms rather than relying solely on programme discretion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/ggd-83-43.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GGD-83-43 Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) ContractsFebruary 18, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 1983</span></span></span>

Current guidance similarly states that carve-outs should be reserved for situations where revealing the existence of the contract or its association with a Special Access Program would itself compromise protected information. Even then, responsibility for security administration must be formally reassigned and coordinated through established Special Access Program oversight structures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/afmc/publication/dodm5220.22v2_afman16-1406v2_afmcsup/dodm5220.22v2_afman16-1406v2_afmcsup.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-PublishingAdministrative Changes to AFMAN 16-1406_afmcsup, National Industrial Security Program:April 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 11, 2026</span></span></span>

## The lasting trade-off

Carve-out contracts illustrate a recurring tension within [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) and other highly compartmented programmes. Restricting visibility can be operationally valuable because it limits opportunities for sensitive information to spread through routine administrative processes. However, every layer of secrecy that removes a programme from standard inspection channels also reduces the number of independent officials able to verify that security procedures, document controls and administrative practices remain effective.

The historical record does not suggest that carve-out contracts are inherently deficient. Rather, government reviews consistently argue that exceptional secrecy requires equally robust compensating oversight. Without those safeguards, the mechanisms designed to conceal a programme from adversaries can also obscure weaknesses from the institutions responsible for ensuring that classified activities remain secure, accountable and properly governed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/ggd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ggd</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Further Improvements Needed in Department of Defense Oversight of Special Access (Carve-Out) Contracts &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 18, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 1983</span></span></span>
