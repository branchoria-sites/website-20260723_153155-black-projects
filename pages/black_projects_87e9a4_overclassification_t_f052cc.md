## Introduction

[Black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) require secrecy because technical details, operational methods and [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}) can lose their value once exposed. The trust problem begins when that protection spreads beyond information whose disclosure would cause identifiable harm. Over-classification hides facts for too long, obstructs oversight, burdens legitimate research and makes official explanations difficult to test. It can also weaken security by burying genuinely vital secrets inside a much larger mass of restricted material.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_overclassification_t_f052cc-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Trust" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The result is a credibility trap. When officials routinely withhold harmless, obsolete or already public information, people become less willing to accept secrecy even when it is justified. Gaps in the record invite speculation about hidden programmes, while later disclosures can make earlier denials appear evasive or misleading. Effective secrecy therefore depends not on classifying as much as possible, but on drawing a defensible boundary: protect capabilities that still need protection, disclose what can safely be disclosed, and explain that distinction clearly enough to retain public confidence.

## Why some secrecy is legitimate

For a genuine black project, secrecy is not merely an institutional preference. It may be part of the project’s military value. Revealing the radar signature of a stealth aircraft, the limits of a reconnaissance sensor, the location of a test facility or the method used to penetrate a communications system could allow an adversary to develop countermeasures. Special Access Programs accordingly impose tighter access and safeguarding rules than ordinary classification where exceptional sensitivity warrants them.

US classification rules formally require more than embarrassment or administrative convenience. Executive Order 13526 permits classification only when unauthorised disclosure could reasonably be expected to damage national security and the information falls within specified areas such as military plans, intelligence activities, foreign relations or technological matters. The principle is sound: secrecy should follow the likely harm from disclosure. A newly developed sensor may require strict protection, while its programme name, broad historical purpose or existence might eventually be acknowledged without exposing how it works. This distinction allows governments to preserve an operational advantage while giving legislators and the public enough information to judge whether public money and authority are being used lawfully.

Official intelligence-transparency policy now recognises that protection and openness are complementary rather than opposing goals. The Office of the Director of National Intelligence says that responsible disclosure should improve public understanding of intelligence missions, legal authorities and oversight while continuing to protect sources, methods and other information whose release would harm national security. It explicitly connects appropriate transparency with earning and retaining public trust.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/index.php/how-we-work/transparency" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Transparency &#124; Office of the Director of National IntelligenceThe Principles of Intelligence Transparency for the Intelligence Commun...</span></span></span>

The difficult question, then, is not whether black projects should contain secrets. It is whether each layer of secrecy remains necessary, proportionate and reviewable as the programme changes.

## When protection becomes over-classification

Over-classification occurs when information is classified even though it does not meet the required [damage test]({{ 'damage-test/' | relative_url }}), when an entire document is restricted because a small portion is sensitive, or when material remains classified after the original risk has disappeared. The Information Security Oversight Office has distinguished between clear-cut over-classification, questionable classification and partial over-classification in which only part of an otherwise properly protected document is unnecessarily withheld.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/reports/1999-annual-report.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1999 annual report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Overclassification: (a) Clear-cut: The information in the document does not meet the standards necessary for classification; (b) Question...</span></span></span>

Black projects are especially vulnerable because compartmentation limits the number of people able to challenge a decision. An official preparing a classification guide has strong incentives to err on the side of secrecy: under-protection may carry professional or security consequences, whereas keeping too much hidden usually produces no immediate penalty. That asymmetry can turn an exceptional safeguard into a routine institutional habit.

Several mechanisms reinforce the problem:

* **Classification spreads through copying.** Derivative classifiers reproduce markings from existing documents, so an overly broad original decision can pass into reports, briefings, contracts and databases.
* **Whole documents inherit the sensitivity of one passage.** Unless information is separated and marked precisely, harmless context may remain inaccessible because it sits beside a protected technical detail.
* **Programme offices protect organisational territory.** Restricting information can preserve control over budgets, expertise and decision-making, even when wider circulation would improve analysis or [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}).
* **Declassification has weaker incentives.** Operational [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) receive recognition for delivering capabilities, not for reviewing old files line by line. Obsolete restrictions can therefore outlive the technology, threat or programme that justified them.
* **Compartmentation conceals duplication.** Separate teams may be unable to recognise that they are researching similar problems, acquiring comparable systems or withholding information already available elsewhere.

These effects are not only theoretical. The National Security Archive has documented records released in full by one authority while another continued to redact the same information on national-security grounds. It has also identified separate reviews of the same document that concealed different passages, suggesting that individual judgement and institutional caution sometimes mattered more than a consistent assessment of harm.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/foia/2019-04-18/redactions-declassified-file" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security Archive Redactions: The Declassified File &#124; National Security Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Washington D.C., April 18, 2019 – The National Security Archive has published hundreds of examples over the years of “dubious secrets” wh...</span></span></span>

Such inconsistencies are damaging because classification markings ask the public, courts and legislators to defer to official expertise. When demonstrably harmless or already published material remains censored, that request for deference becomes harder to sustain.

## Too many secrets can weaken security

Over-classification is often criticised as an openness problem, but it can also obstruct the national-security work it is supposed to support. A bloated classified environment increases storage, clearance, training, auditing and information-technology costs. More importantly, it makes information harder to locate and share with people who have a legitimate operational need for it.

Congress acknowledged this problem in the Reducing Over-Classification Act of 2010. The law found that unnecessary classification creates confusion over who may receive information and impedes sharing between federal agencies, state and local authorities and private organisations. It described over-classification as contrary to the information-sharing environment developed after the intelligence failures surrounding the attacks of 11 September 2001.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/plaw/111/public/258" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The underlying problem is signal dilution. Security personnel have limited time and attention. If large quantities of routine material are handled like exceptionally dangerous secrets, employees may struggle to distinguish information that would genuinely cause grave harm if compromised. Excessive restrictions can also encourage informal workarounds when staff find the official system too cumbersome, creating new handling risks.

The bipartisan Moynihan Commission reached a similar conclusion in 1997. Its central argument was that the most important secrets are better protected when secrecy is limited to its necessary role. Excessive secrecy, it found, can leave policymakers inadequately informed, reduce governmental accountability and prevent meaningful public debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[FAS Project on Government Secrecy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fas.org.</span></span></span>

This is particularly relevant to black programmes involving multiple military services, intelligence agencies and private contractors. Compartmentation may protect a technology from hostile intelligence, yet the same boundaries can stop authorised officials from identifying safety concerns, conflicting requirements or repeated spending. Secrecy becomes counterproductive when it blocks the oversight and technical criticism needed to make the programme succeed.

A 2024 Government Accountability Office examination of Department of Defense intelligence oversight illustrates the continuing concern. Although the relevant oversight office was formally entitled to unrestricted access even to compartmented intelligence Special Access Programs, GAO reported that without regular inspections it was unclear how genuinely independent oversight of those activities would be achieved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-24-106190.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao 24 106190</span></span></span>

Access on paper is therefore not enough. Oversight bodies require the staff, procedures and institutional standing to examine deeply compartmented work rather than merely being told that they could request access.

## How secrecy produces suspicion

The public rarely sees the operational logic behind a black project while it remains active. It sees restricted airspace, unexplained expenditure, unusual prototypes, contradictory statements or heavily redacted documents. When government provides no safe explanatory context, observers fill the gap with inference.

This does not mean every rumour arises from a real programme. It means that authentic secrecy can create an environment in which accurate fragments, mistaken observations and unsupported claims become difficult to separate. A witness may see an unfamiliar test aircraft but misjudge its performance. A former employee may know one compartment of a project but infer much more about another. A leaked code name may be genuine even when the dramatic story attached to it is not.

The relationship between classified aerospace work and unidentified-object speculation is a vivid example. In its historical review of unidentified anomalous phenomena, the US Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office concluded that secrecy surrounding genuine national-security programmes had contributed to public beliefs that the government was concealing extraterrestrial technology. According to the review, some alleged hidden programmes were misidentifications of authentic sensitive activities, while other claims referred to projects that did not exist or had been discontinued.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

That conclusion does not require the public to accept every official assessment without question. It demonstrates the mechanism: when observers encounter evidence of real concealment but cannot learn what is being concealed, they may correctly infer a secret programme yet incorrectly identify its purpose. Continued silence then appears to confirm the mistaken explanation.

Redaction itself can intensify this response. Experimental research has examined whether visibly censored official documents encourage readers to suspect a cover-up. The wider lesson is that withholding information does more than remove facts from the public record; it communicates that significant facts exist but cannot be inspected. In low-trust environments, that signal can become more influential than the limited information officials release.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/88DB61FA87834E7D0687867EA7F0A600/S2052263015000214a.pdf/classified-or-coverup-the-effect-of-redactions-on-conspiracy-theory-beliefs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cambridge.org.</span></span></span>

This produces a difficult asymmetry. A vague denial may be technically accurate because officials cannot mention the classified programme that explains an observation. To the audience, however, the denial may look deliberately evasive. If the programme is acknowledged decades later, the revelation can retrospectively validate suspicion of official secrecy even while disproving the specific rumour.

## The credibility cost of delayed disclosure

Time changes the balance between secrecy and accountability. Technical specifications that were dangerous to reveal during development may become harmless after a system is retired, adversaries acquire similar technology or the underlying science becomes widely known. Yet classification does not always decline at the same pace as sensitivity.

The Pentagon Papers remain a prominent example of what happens when official secrecy extends beyond a persuasive security case. The classified study documented US decision-making in Vietnam and was leaked in 1971, but the complete official version was not released by the National Archives until 2011. The long delay reinforced the perception that classification had protected the government from scrutiny at least as much as it had protected military operations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/pentagon-papers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Pentagon Papers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Pentagon Papers</span></span></span>

For black projects, delayed acknowledgement can have a comparable effect even where the original secrecy was entirely legitimate. Once a previously denied aircraft, site or research programme becomes public, people may reasonably ask which parts genuinely required protection and which parts were withheld merely because acknowledging them was inconvenient. Failure to answer that question leaves the entire secrecy system vulnerable to retrospective distrust.

The problem is magnified by inconsistent disclosure. Governments may publicise a successful capability to demonstrate technological strength while withholding cost overruns, accidents, abandoned alternatives or disagreements that accompanied its development. A selective history can be factually correct yet still appear manipulative because it presents secrecy as necessary for failures but optional for achievements.

Trust therefore depends partly on whether eventual disclosure looks candid. A credible historical release should explain what was protected, why secrecy was justified at the time, what went wrong, how oversight operated and why the information can now be made public. A ceremonial unveiling of a successful aircraft is less persuasive than a record that also allows independent examination of decisions and consequences.

## What proportionate secrecy looks like

The answer is not immediate disclosure of every classified project. It is a system that forces secrecy to remain specific, reviewable and temporary wherever possible.

**Classify the harmful detail, not the surrounding subject.** Programme offices should separate technical characteristics, operational vulnerabilities and intelligence sources from information that can be released safely. Acknowledging that research exists need not reveal how it works.

**Use realistic review dates.** Classification guidance should reflect how quickly a capability is likely to become obsolete or independently discoverable. Executive Order 13526 requires agencies with original classification authority to review their guidance to identify information that no longer needs protection. The Fundamental Classification Guidance Review process was created specifically to reduce unnecessary withholding and keep agency rules aligned with current circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/fcgr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

**Give oversight bodies practical access.** Parliamentary or congressional committees, inspectors general and specialised auditors need cleared staff, secure facilities and the authority to follow spending and performance across [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}). Oversight that depends entirely on summaries prepared by the programme being examined is not independent scrutiny.

**Record the reasoning.** A classification decision should identify the anticipated harm from disclosure rather than relying on broad labels. Documented reasoning makes later review more disciplined and reduces the tendency to preserve secrecy simply because it already exists.

**Create usable challenge routes.** Officials should be able to question doubtful classifications without risking their careers. Members of the public can request Mandatory Declassification Review of eligible classified records, while the Interagency Security Classification Appeals Panel can review certain agency decisions. These mechanisms provide a lawful alternative to treating leaks as the only route to disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/training/mdr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

**Release explanatory context proactively.** Once operational danger has declined, agencies should publish coherent histories rather than waiting for fragmented records to emerge through litigation, leaks and archival requests. Proactive disclosure allows legitimate secrecy to be explained before speculation becomes entrenched.

Modernisation also matters. The Information Security Oversight Office’s 2024 annual report described over-classification and fragmented infrastructure as longstanding systemic problems, while the Public Interest Declassification Board has repeatedly called for technology-assisted classification and declassification suited to the scale of digital records. A paper-era system cannot reliably review the volume of material created by contemporary intelligence and defence organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2024-annual-report-final-508.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span></span></span>

## Public confidence depends on restraint

People do not need access to every blueprint or operational plan to judge whether a secrecy system deserves trust. They need evidence that classification has limits: independent oversight, consistent rules, correction of errors, eventual disclosure and credible explanations for what remains hidden.

Over-classification undermines each of those signals. It blurs the line between national protection and institutional self-protection. It makes harmless disclosures look like major breaches, encourages cynicism among cleared personnel and leaves the public unable to distinguish necessary silence from concealment of failure. Once that distinction collapses, even accurate official statements may be treated as strategic messaging rather than reliable information.

The central paradox of black projects is therefore that durable secrecy requires visible restraint. Governments protect their most sensitive capabilities more effectively when they demonstrate that secrecy is exceptional, evidence-based and regularly reconsidered. Public confidence cannot be created by revealing everything, but neither can it survive a system that appears unwilling to reveal anything until exposure becomes unavoidable.
