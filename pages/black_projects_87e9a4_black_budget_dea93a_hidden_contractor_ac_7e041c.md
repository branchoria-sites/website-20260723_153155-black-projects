## Introduction

Secrecy can make [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) more secure, but it also makes contractors harder to evaluate from the outside. When programme names, contract values, technical milestones, and even the identity of participating companies are classified, the public cannot easily judge whether a contractor is delivering on time, within budget, or according to contract. This does not mean contractors operate without oversight. Rather, [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) shifts away from public scrutiny towards a limited circle of cleared officials, auditors, inspectors and congressional committees. The central question is therefore not whether oversight exists, but whether enough independent scrutiny survives once most information is removed from public view.

## What contractor details disappear from public view?

Classified acquisition programmes often conceal much more than the technology itself. Depending on the programme's security classification or special access controls, public records may omit:

* The contractor or subcontractor responsible for the work.
* Contract values and annual spending.
* Performance milestones and delivery schedules.

* Testing locations and production facilities.
* Numbers of aircraft, [satellites]({{ 'satellites/' | relative_url }}), sensors or other systems being acquired.
* Reasons for schedule delays or cost increases.

Public budget documents may instead show only broad appropriations or generic programme elements. Even companies involved in highly classified work sometimes refer only to "classified programmes" or "restricted contracts" in investor reports without identifying the customer or capability.

This creates an information gap. Investors, journalists, researchers and taxpayers can often see that a contractor receives substantial classified business, but cannot independently determine whether individual programmes are succeeding or failing.

## How secrecy changes cost and performance scrutiny

Ordinary government contracting relies on multiple layers of external pressure. Budget documents, congressional hearings, procurement databases, media reporting and independent analysts all help identify cost overruns or poor contractor performance.

Highly classified programmes reduce many of these checks.

### Public comparison becomes difficult

For conventional defence programmes, analysts can compare planned and actual costs, production rates and delivery schedules over many years. For deeply classified programmes, equivalent information may remain unavailable for decades, if it is ever released.

Without identifiable programme data, outsiders cannot easily determine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* whether costs have grown unexpectedly;
* whether technical requirements have changed repeatedly;
* whether contractors have met agreed milestones; or
* whether delays stem from contractor performance, government decisions or changing operational requirements.

</div>

The absence of comparable public information also makes it harder to benchmark competing contractors.

### Market discipline is weakened

Competition normally encourages accountability because future contracts depend partly on demonstrated performance.

When contracts themselves are classified, other companies may know little about a rival's actual performance. Likewise, shareholders cannot accurately assess whether strong classified revenue reflects efficient delivery or continuing government support for strategically important capabilities.

This does not eliminate contractual incentives, but it reduces the amount of reputational pressure generated through public markets and open procurement.

### Failures may remain hidden for years

Many classified programmes are eventually [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) in part, revealing technical setbacks or cancelled efforts long after procurement decisions were made.

Historical examples show that expensive classified programmes have experienced schedule delays, technical failures and restructuring before becoming publicly known. Because disclosure is delayed, lessons that might normally influence public debate or procurement reform often emerge years later.

## What oversight bodies can still examine

Secrecy narrows public visibility but does not remove formal accountability mechanisms. Instead, responsibility shifts to institutions authorised to review classified information.

### Congress

Selected congressional committees receive classified briefings and funding information for intelligence and defence programmes. Members and cleared staff can question officials, review classified budget submissions and authorise or restrict funding.

However, oversight effectiveness depends on several factors:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how much programme information executive agencies provide;
* whether members possess the technical expertise needed to evaluate complex systems;
* time available to review highly classified material; and
* statutory limits associated with particularly sensitive special access programmes.

</div>

Some especially sensitive programmes are reported through more restricted channels than ordinary classified acquisitions, concentrating knowledge within a relatively small group of cleared officials.

### Inspectors General

Inspectors General retain authority to investigate allegations of waste, fraud, abuse and misconduct inside classified programmes. Because they hold appropriate clearances, they can review documentation unavailable to the public.

Their investigations have repeatedly identified weaknesses in defence contract oversight even where classified work is involved. ## Why independent verification becomes more difficult

One of the greatest accountability challenges is not the absence of oversight but the inability to verify that oversight independently.

In open procurement, journalists, academics, competitors and watchdog organisations can compare official statements with procurement records, contractor disclosures and technical evidence.

In black projects, most of those reference points disappear.

As a result:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a113916.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GA O-06-800T, DOD Acquisitions: Contracting for Better Outcomes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">As a result, DOD paid out an estimated $8 billion in award fees on contracts in our study population, regardless of whether acquisition o...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* external experts cannot independently validate government performance claims;
* investigative reporting is constrained by classification rules;
* academic analysis relies heavily on historical releases rather than current documentation; and
* legal challenges become more difficult because many relevant records remain inaccessible.

</div>

This places greater weight on trusted institutional oversight rather than distributed public scrutiny.

## Accountability depends more on institutions than transparency

Hidden contractors are not beyond government control, but they operate within a different accountability model.

Instead of relying primarily on [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}), accountability depends on contract officers, security officials, inspectors, auditors and cleared members of Congress exercising effective oversight behind closed doors. When those institutions function well, secrecy and accountability can coexist. When oversight resources are limited, reporting channels are narrowed or documentation is incomplete, secrecy can make cost growth, performance problems and management failures substantially harder to detect and correct.

The result is a fundamental trade-off within black projects: the same secrecy that protects sensitive military and intelligence capabilities also limits the ordinary public mechanisms that help ensure contractors deliver efficiently and remain accountable for the resources entrusted to them.
