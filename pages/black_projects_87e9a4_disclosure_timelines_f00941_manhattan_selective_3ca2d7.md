## Introduction

The Manhattan Project did not move directly from absolute secrecy to full disclosure after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Instead, the United States adopted a carefully managed disclosure strategy that acknowledged the programme's existence, explained its scientific foundations, and described its unprecedented industrial scale while deliberately excluding the engineering details that would have aided another country's weapons effort. The centrepiece of this policy was the **Smyth Report**, officially titled *Atomic Energy for Military Purposes*, released on 12 August 1945. It demonstrated that a government could publish an extensive official history of a secret weapons programme without revealing its most sensitive technical knowledge. This became an influential model for later [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}): acknowledge enough to establish public legitimacy and historical credit, but retain the information that still carried military value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/public_reaction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[osti.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">public reaction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Informing the Public, August 1945...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1945</span></span></span>

## Why public explanation became urgent in August 1945

The atomic bomb was impossible to conceal once it had been used in combat. President Harry Truman announced the Hiroshima attack immediately, and the unprecedented destructive power of the weapon created intense public, scientific and international curiosity. Officials recognised that silence would encourage speculation, rumours and potentially uncontrolled disclosures by thousands of scientists, engineers and military personnel who had worked on the project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/public_reaction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">public reaction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Informing the Public, August 1945...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1945</span></span></span>

Planning for this moment had begun well before the bombings. During 1944, senior officials including James Conant, Vannevar Bush and General Leslie Groves concluded that an authorised technical history would both educate the public and reinforce the limits of continuing secrecy. Henry DeWolf Smyth, a Princeton physicist involved in the early stages of the project, was commissioned to write the report under explicit security guidance. His manuscript underwent review by Groves and leading project scientists, including J. Robert Oppenheimer and Ernest Lawrence, to ensure that it explained the programme without revealing information judged useful for weapon development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/public_reaction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">public reaction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Informing the Public, August 1945...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1945</span></span></span>

This approach reflected a broader governance objective. Democratic governments could not plausibly ask citizens to accept the political and moral consequences of atomic weapons while refusing to explain what had happened. At the same time, military leaders believed that disclosure had to be selective because the wartime alliance with the Soviet Union was already giving way to strategic rivalry.

## What the authorised report disclosed

Rather than functioning as a manual, the Smyth Report presented a broad scientific and administrative history of the Manhattan Project.

It openly described:

* The scientific discovery of nuclear fission and why it suggested the possibility of enormous energy release.
* The progression from theoretical physics to practical engineering.
* The organisational structure of the Manhattan Project.
* The enormous industrial effort devoted to uranium enrichment and plutonium production.
* The construction and purpose of major sites including [Oak Ridge]({{ 'oak-ridge/' | relative_url }}), Hanford and Los Alamos.
* The collaboration between scientists, universities, industry and the military.
* The scale of investment in people, factories and infrastructure required to produce the bomb.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/publications/smyth_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATOMI C ENERGY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ATOMI C ENERGY</span></span></span>

This emphasis served two purposes. First, it corrected the popular misconception that the atomic bomb had been a single laboratory breakthrough. The report instead showed that success depended on an immense national mobilisation involving chemistry, metallurgy, engineering, industrial management and logistics. Second, it publicly recognised the work of thousands of participants whose contributions could finally be acknowledged without exposing operational secrets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/smyth-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ahf.nuclearmuseum.org">[Nuclear Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ahf.nuclearmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Nuclear Museum Smyth Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear MuseumSmyth Report - Nuclear Museum...</span></span></span>

Smyth himself made the intended audience clear. His preface explained that the report was written for scientifically trained readers who could help explain atomic energy to the wider public. At the same time, he warned that secrecy requirements had necessarily shaped both its contents and its emphasis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/publications/smyth_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATOMI C ENERGY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ATOMI C ENERGY</span></span></span>

## How the report explained the science without revealing weapon design

The report's most significant achievement lay in the distinction it drew between explaining principles and revealing implementation.

Readers learned why uranium and plutonium could sustain nuclear chain reactions and why separating suitable isotopes posed an enormous engineering challenge. They learned that reactors could produce plutonium, that isotope separation required multiple competing industrial techniques, and that the project had demanded unprecedented manufacturing capacity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/publications/smyth_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATOMI C ENERGY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ATOMI C ENERGY</span></span></span>

What they did **not** receive were the precise design solutions that transformed these principles into an effective weapon. The report intentionally avoided detailed treatment of critical engineering problems, manufacturing tolerances, component arrangements, specialised production methods and other information that remained militarily valuable. Its purpose was to explain what had been accomplished rather than how to reproduce it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Resources/openness.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manhattan Project: Nuclear Energy and the Public&#x27;s Right to Know</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Nuclear Energy and the Public&#x27;s Right to Know</span></span></span>

This distinction reflected an important policy judgement. By 1945, physicists around the world already understood the basic science of nuclear fission through published research. What remained strategically important was not the existence of the underlying theory but the accumulated practical knowledge required to build a functioning weapon at scale. The report therefore acknowledged publicly available scientific concepts while withholding the wartime innovations that converted theory into deployable technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/publications/smyth_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ATOMI C ENERGY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ATOMI C ENERGY</span></span></span>

## How nuclear secrecy survived rapid acknowledgment

The publication of the Smyth Report did not signal the end of classification. Instead, it helped define what remained secret.

General Groves' foreword explicitly warned that the report represented the authorised limit of disclosure and that unauthorised release of additional information could still violate the Espionage Act. The message was directed not only at foreign intelligence services but also at the project's own personnel. If information appeared in the report, discussion was generally acceptable; if it did not, participants were expected to assume it remained protected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/public_reaction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">public reaction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Informing the Public, August 1945...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1945</span></span></span>

This solved an immediate governance problem. More than 100,000 people had participated in the Manhattan Project, many of whom suddenly faced questions from journalists, colleagues and family members. The Smyth Report created a common, officially approved narrative that reduced uncertainty about what could safely be discussed while discouraging unauthorised elaboration. Historians of the Department of Energy have noted that the report was intended both to encourage informed public discussion and to preserve essential military secrets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1945-present/public_reaction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">public reaction</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Informing the Public, August 1945...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1945</span></span></span>

The policy also recognised that secrecy itself could become more effective when paired with selective openness. Rather than encouraging reporters to investigate every unexplained aspect of the programme, officials released enough authoritative information to satisfy many immediate questions while clearly marking the boundary beyond which disclosure would not extend.

## A lasting model for black project disclosure

The Smyth Report established an enduring pattern for revealing highly classified programmes after their existence can no longer be denied.

Its approach combined several elements:

* **Official [acknowledgment]({{ 'acknowledgment-gap/' | relative_url }})** instead of continued denial.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Scientific explanation** sufficient for public understanding.
* **Recognition of participants** and administrative history.
* **Deliberate omission** of information that retained operational or strategic value.
* **Clear signalling** that authorised disclosure did not imply complete declassification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Resources/openness.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osti.gov">[OSTI.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osti.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manhattan Project: Nuclear Energy and the Public&#x27;s Right to Know</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manhattan Project: Nuclear Energy and the Public&#x27;s Right to Know</span></span></span>

</div>

That balance distinguished acknowledgment from transparency. The public learned that the United States had mobilised an extraordinary scientific-industrial enterprise to produce atomic weapons, but not the full technical pathway by which those weapons had been engineered. In later decades, many governments would employ comparable disclosure strategies for other sensitive military programmes, releasing enough information to explain a capability while preserving the technical core that continued to matter for national security.

      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
