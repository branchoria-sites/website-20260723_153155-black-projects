## Introduction

The public does not see the detailed US [black budget]({{ 'black-budget/' | relative_url }}), but neither does Congress as a whole. Instead, access is deliberately restricted to a relatively small group of lawmakers, cleared professional staff, and, for the most sensitive programmes, only those who have both the appropriate security clearance and a demonstrated need to know. This layered system is intended to balance democratic oversight with the protection of [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}), military capabilities and highly classified technology. In practice, it means that most Members of Congress vote on overall defence and intelligence appropriations without seeing every classified programme that those appropriations support. The closer a programme comes to the most sensitive intelligence or Special Access Program (SAP) categories, the narrower the circle of congressional visibility becomes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2022</span></span></span>

## Public, committee and compartmented access levels

[Congressional access]({{ 'congressional-access/' | relative_url }}) to the black budget is best understood as a series of increasingly restrictive layers rather than a simple division between "Congress" and "the public".

**The public** can see only limited information. Since the late 2000s, the US government has released top-line figures for the National Intelligence Program and publishes broad appropriations for many defence accounts. Those figures reveal the overall scale of intelligence spending but not the individual classified projects beneath them.

**Most Members of Congress** receive less information than many people assume. They vote on appropriations bills and may receive classified briefings relevant to their committee work, but they generally do not have routine access to detailed [intelligence budgets]({{ 'budget-games/' | relative_url }}), operational plans or compartmented programme information. The Senate Select Committee on Intelligence explicitly notes that while Senators may access classified intelligence assessments, access to intelligence programmes, sources, methods and detailed budgets is generally limited to members of the intelligence committees and certain defence appropriators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/about-the-committee/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceAbout The Committee &#124; Senate Select Committee on Intelligence...</span></span></span>

**Specialised oversight committees** form the principal congressional audience for the black budget. These include:

<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[house]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceAbout The Committee &#124; Senate Select Committee on Intelligence</span></span></span> House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/about-the-committee/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[senate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceAbout The Committee &#124; Senate Select Committee on Intelligence...</span></span></span> Senate Select Committee on Intelligence.
* The intelligence and defence appropriations subcommittees.
* The armed services committees where Department of Defense Special Access Programs fall within their jurisdiction.

These committees receive classified budget justifications, programme briefings and notifications that are unavailable to the wider Congress. Federal law requires annual reporting on many Special Access Programs to the relevant congressional oversight committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

## How cleared oversight differs from public transparency

Oversight is not the same as [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}). Members serving on the relevant committees receive access because they are carrying out a constitutional oversight function, not because the information has become public.

In practical terms, committee members may review:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Classified budget requests.
* Programme cost estimates.
* Operational justifications.
* Major milestones.
* Inspector General findings.
* Classified notifications when new highly restricted programmes are established.

</div>

By contrast, public budget documents normally reveal only aggregate funding levels or broad mission categories. Programme names, [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}), locations, technical performance, intelligence sources and operational details frequently remain classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

Committee oversight is also supported by cleared professional staff. These staff members hold appropriate security clearances, analyse classified documents, prepare questions for hearings and help members understand highly technical programmes. Without cleared staff, effective congressional oversight of complex intelligence and defence spending would be extremely difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/about-the-committee/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceAbout The Committee &#124; Senate Select Committee on Intelligence...</span></span></span>

## Where access narrows even further

Even committee membership does not guarantee access to every black programme.

Many highly sensitive activities operate within compartmented systems known as Controlled Access Programs (CAPs) in the Intelligence Community or Special Access Programs elsewhere in government. These impose restrictions beyond ordinary Top Secret classification. Access requires not only the appropriate clearance but also formal [approval]({{ 'approval/' | relative_url }}) based on a specific operational [need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2022</span></span></span>

Congress has recognised that excessive compartmentation can weaken oversight. In response, legislation now requires intelligence agencies to provide baseline and annual reports on Controlled Access Programs to the congressional intelligence and appropriations committees as well as congressional leadership. Those reports include information such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* When a programme began.
* Why it exists.
* Annual funding.
* Whether it remains operational.
* Whether new compartments have been created or existing ones modified.

</div>

These requirements are designed to ensure that the existence of extremely sensitive programmes is not hidden indefinitely from the officials responsible for legislative oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2022</span></span></span>

## Why only a small number of lawmakers routinely see the details

The limited circle of access reflects several practical and legal considerations.

First, intelligence agencies seek to minimise the number of people exposed to exceptionally sensitive operational information. Restricting access reduces the risk of accidental disclosure and protects intelligence sources and methods.

Second, Congress has organised oversight around specialist committees rather than expecting all 535 Members to review classified operational material. Committee members develop expertise in intelligence, defence acquisition and classified budgeting that would be difficult to replicate across the entire legislature. The Senate Intelligence Committee's structure is specifically intended to concentrate classified oversight while coordinating with related committees such as Appropriations and Armed Services.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/about-the-committee/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Senate Select Committee on IntelligenceAbout The Committee &#124; Senate Select Committee on Intelligence...</span></span></span>

Third, many black programmes involve technologies or operations that span multiple agencies. Oversight therefore often requires coordination between intelligence committees, defence committees and appropriations committees rather than a single congressional body. Statutory reporting requirements reflect this shared oversight structure by directing reports to multiple committees with relevant jurisdiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title50-section3348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code</span></span></span>

## The continuing debate over whether oversight is sufficient

The existence of congressional oversight does not end debate over its effectiveness.

Critics argue that heavy compartmentation, complex classification rules and reliance on "need to know" restrictions can leave even cleared legislators with only partial visibility into some programmes. Others contend that expanding access too broadly would undermine national security by increasing the risk of leaks or exposing sensitive intelligence capabilities.

Congress itself has responded to these concerns by strengthening statutory reporting requirements for Controlled Access Programs and Special Access Programs in recent years, reflecting an ongoing effort to improve legislative visibility without making the programmes public. The result is a system that deliberately distinguishes between **public transparency**, which remains limited, and **classified democratic oversight**, which is concentrated in a relatively small group of authorised lawmakers and cleared staff.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12080" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">April 20, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 20, 2022</span></span></span>
