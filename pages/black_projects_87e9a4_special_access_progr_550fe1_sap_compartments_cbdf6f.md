## Introduction

Special Access Program (SAP) compartmenting is the mechanism that allows a highly classified defence programme to be divided into smaller, tightly controlled pieces. Rather than giving everyone on a project access to every document, design or operational plan, the programme is organised into separate compartments and sub-compartments. Personnel are read into only the specific elements they need to perform their jobs, even if they already possess the required security clearance. This layered approach enables large, technically complex [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) to function while limiting the consequences of espionage, accidental disclosure or insider compromise. It is one of the central ways that sensitive programmes remain manageable without abandoning the principle of "[need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }})".<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>

## Programme compartments, sub-compartments and projects

Modern US Department of Defense policy describes SAPs as a structured architecture rather than a collection of isolated secret programmes. The architecture is organised into multiple tiers, allowing sensitive information to be grouped according to the capability or technology it protects.

At the highest level are **SAP umbrellas**, which organise related activities. Beneath them sit **SAP compartments** (Tier 2), followed by **SAP sub-compartments** (Tier 3 and lower). Individual programmes exist within this hierarchy, with each tier treated as its own SAP for management and security purposes. Rather than creating arbitrary divisions, the structure groups closely related critical programme information while keeping unrelated work isolated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>

Current DoD policy also distinguishes between two different kinds of SAPs:

* **Structural SAPs**, which exist mainly to organise the security architecture.
* **Content SAPs**, which directly protect distinct pieces of critical programme information.

The policy further requires that sensitive information be protected at the **lowest practical tier**. Instead of hiding an entire programme behind one enormous compartment, the information is broken into smaller protected units wherever feasible. This reduces unnecessary exposure while allowing different [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) to collaborate only where their responsibilities overlap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>

The Air Force's implementation guidance reflects the same philosophy. Before creating new compartments or projects, officials are instructed to review existing structures to avoid unnecessary duplication, recognising that excessive compartmenting can itself become a management burden.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/saf_aa/publication/afi16-701/afi16-701.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">afi16 701</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-PublishingAIR FORCE INSTRUCTION 16-701December 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 7, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why partial access reduces compromise risk

Compartmenting assumes that very few individuals genuinely require complete knowledge of an entire programme.

A propulsion engineer, for example, may need detailed information about engine performance but have no operational requirement to know mission planning, [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}), specialised sensors or manufacturing methods. Likewise, software developers may work on mission systems without access to stealth materials, while logistics personnel may support deployment without seeing detailed design information.

This creates several security advantages.

* **Smaller exposure after a breach.** If one compartment is compromised, the attacker gains access only to that portion rather than the complete programme.
* **Reduced insider risk.** An authorised insider can access only the information associated with their approved duties.
* **Controlled dissemination.** Information crossing compartment boundaries generally requires additional [approval]({{ 'approval/' | relative_url }}) rather than routine sharing.
* **Better auditing.** Programme security officers can identify exactly which individuals had access to a particular compartment instead of maintaining a single broad access list.

These controls reinforce Executive Order 13526's long-standing requirement that access depends not only on clearance eligibility but also on a demonstrated need to know. Possessing a Top Secret clearance alone does not automatically permit entry into every SAP or every compartment within a SAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526 &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Another practical benefit is limiting the value of intelligence collection by foreign adversaries. Modern defence systems often combine multiple disciplines—materials science, software, electronics, manufacturing, intelligence support and operational tactics. By separating these elements, no single engineer, contractor or administrator necessarily understands the complete capability, making comprehensive theft substantially more difficult.

## How compartment boundaries are maintained

Compartmenting is sustained through administrative and technical controls rather than relying solely on [trust]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}).

Personnel must be individually approved for access to each relevant compartment, receive programme-specific briefings and comply with additional handling rules that exceed ordinary classified-information procedures. Information is stored and processed only within approved SAP facilities and authorised information systems designed for that compartment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>

Classification guidance also reflects compartment boundaries. DoD security classification guidance establishes programme identifiers, nicknames and compartment structures so that documents are marked consistently without revealing unnecessary information about the wider architecture. Enterprise guidance standardises protection of administrative details—such as programme identifiers and the existence of subordinate tiers—while umbrella-specific guidance defines the hierarchy inside individual SAP families.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/888387389/DoDM-5205-07-Special-Access-Program-Security-Manual-Jan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[Scribd]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DoDM 5205 07 Special Access Program Security Manual Jan</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoDM 5205.07 - &quot;Special Access Program Security Manual&quot; (Jan 17 2025) &#124; PDF &#124; United States Department Of Defense &#124; Computer Securi...</span></span></span>

An important feature of this system is that administrative language often conceals the precise position of an individual within the hierarchy. Air Force guidance notes that personnel being briefed into compartments, sub-compartments or projects are generally told only that they are entering a "SAP", helping avoid unnecessary disclosure of the architecture itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/saf_aa/publication/afi16-701/afi16-701.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">afi16 701</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-PublishingAIR FORCE INSTRUCTION 16-701December 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 7, 2025</span></span></span>

## Where compartmenting can create coordination problems

Although compartmenting strengthens security, it also introduces operational costs.

Large defence programmes frequently depend on multiple engineering disciplines working together. If compartment boundaries become too rigid, teams may lack information needed to solve integration problems, increasing delays and duplication of effort. Engineers may independently develop similar solutions because they are unaware that another compartment has already addressed the same issue.

Security administration also becomes more complex. Every additional compartment requires separate access approvals, record keeping, document controls, security guidance and oversight. Excessive subdivision can therefore increase bureaucracy without proportionally improving security.

DoD policy acknowledges this balance by requiring critical programme information to be grouped logically and protected at the lowest practical level rather than creating unnecessary numbers of compartments. Air Force guidance similarly directs programme managers to review existing structures before establishing new compartments or projects in order to reduce redundant organisational layers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>

## Why compartmenting remains central to black projects

The significance of compartmenting is not simply that information is hidden—it is that knowledge is distributed selectively across a large organisation.

Modern black projects often involve government laboratories, military organisations, intelligence agencies and multiple defence [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) working simultaneously. Without compartmenting, the number of people exposed to the programme's complete technical picture would grow rapidly, increasing opportunities for espionage, leaks or accidental disclosure.

By separating programmes into carefully managed compartments and sub-compartments, SAPs allow highly sensitive research and operational capabilities to be developed collaboratively while ensuring that no individual automatically gains visibility across the entire enterprise. The result is a security model that seeks to preserve both operational effectiveness and strict control over the nation's most sensitive defence capabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS Enterprise Services DivisionDoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration, and Oversight of DoD Special Access Programs,&quot; Sept...</span></span></span>
