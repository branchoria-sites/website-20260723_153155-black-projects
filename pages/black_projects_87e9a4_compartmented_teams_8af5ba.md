## Introduction

[Black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) are rarely built by a single all-knowing organisation. Governments usually depend on private contractors for specialist design, manufacturing, software, testing and materials, yet restrict each company—and often each engineer—to the information required for a defined task. A security clearance establishes eligibility, but it does not grant blanket access: personnel must also have a valid [need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}) and, for a Special Access Program, specific approval from the programme authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">32 CFR Part 117 -- National Industrial Security Program...The contractor must determine that access to classified information is ess...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_compartmented_teams_8af5ba-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Teams" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This compartmented model allows a small group to solve extraordinarily difficult engineering problems without revealing the complete machine to everyone involved. It can protect a decisive feature such as stealth geometry, sensor performance or an intelligence mission. The cost is organisational friction. Interfaces become harder to explain, subcontractors may optimise components without seeing the whole system, and defects often emerge only when hardware and software are finally integrated. The central challenge is therefore not merely keeping secrets; it is giving each team enough context to produce something that works with everything it is not permitted to see.

## Need-to-know work

A useful distinction separates **clearance**, **access** and **understanding**. A contractor employee may be eligible to handle information at a particular classification level, but access is permitted only when the information is essential to contract performance. US industrial-security rules require contractors to minimise the number of employees for whom eligibility is requested and expressly reject maintaining a reserve pool of cleared personnel without an operational requirement. Access to Special Access Program information is then decided separately by the relevant government authority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117/section-117.10" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">i) The contractor must determine that access to...</span></span></span>

In practice, this produces teams whose knowledge resembles overlapping circles rather than a conventional organisation chart. A structural engineer might know the aircraft’s loads, temperatures and attachment points but not its intended target. A software supplier might receive message formats and timing limits without seeing the complete sensor architecture. Manufacturing staff may work from controlled drawings whose unusual tolerances make little sense without the hidden performance requirement. A small integration group at the centre holds enough cross-compartment knowledge to judge whether the pieces form a viable system.

Compartmentation is not the same as keeping every worker ignorant. Complex engineering depends on understanding assumptions, failure modes and interfaces. Successful programme managers therefore attempt to conceal the mission-revealing whole while distributing carefully prepared fragments of context: dimensional envelopes, thermal loads, electrical characteristics, test conditions, software interfaces and acceptance criteria. The information boundary should follow the task boundary. When it does not, secrecy can prevent engineers from recognising that a requirement is contradictory or that a local design choice creates a system-wide problem.

The Lockheed teams behind the U-2 and A-12 OXCART offer a prominent historical example. The Central Intelligence Agency contracted directly with Lockheed for the U-2 and later awarded the OXCART aircraft contract to the company in 1959. The A-12 required advances across titanium construction, propulsion, fuel, navigation, flight controls, electronic countermeasures, radar-signature reduction and life-support equipment. These were not independent curiosities: they had to operate together in an aircraft capable of sustained flight above Mach 3 at extreme altitude.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Archangel-CIAs-Supersonic-A-12-Reconnaissance-Aircraft.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Archangel CIAs Supersonic A 12 Reconnaissance Aircraft</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ARCHANGEL:by D ROBARGE · Cited by 25 — In 1954, CIA retained the Lockheed Corporation to build the U-2 reconnaissance aircraft. OXCART demonstrates why compact, highly trusted engineering groups can be effective. Lockheed’s advanced-development organisation could make rapid trade-offs across disciplines without sending every decision through a large corporate hierarchy. Yet even such a concentrated team depended on outside organisations for engines, sensors, materials and specialised equipment. The secret programme was therefore less a sealed workshop than a controlled network, with Lockheed and CIA personnel managing what each participant needed to know and how their contributions would meet at the aircraft level.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001459069.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">HISTORY OF THE OXCART PROGRAMof A-12. The model has a further human consequence: only a minority of employees can explain the significance of their own work. That reduces the damage caused by one disclosure, but it can also weaken ordinary professional communication. Engineers cannot always seek advice across the wider company, compare their work openly with similar projects or describe the true problem to an outside expert. The programme effectively trades a broad technical community for a smaller circle with deeper [trust]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}) and narrower channels.

## Contractor boundaries

Classified development often extends through several contractual layers. A prime contractor may design and integrate the complete platform while subcontractors supply engines, processors, coatings, optics, actuators or software. Under US rules, classified information may be passed to a subcontractor only when the recipient organisation has the appropriate facility eligibility, its personnel have the necessary access, and the disclosure is required for performance. The prime contractor must also provide the relevant classification guidance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">32 CFR Part 117 -- National Industrial Security Program...The contractor must determine that access to classified information is ess...</span></span></span>

This gives the prime contractor two simultaneous roles. It is an engineering authority responsible for the system and a security gatekeeper responsible for limiting what leaves its compartment. Those duties can conflict. A supplier usually performs better when it understands the operating environment and the reasons behind demanding specifications. Security officers, however, are expected to reduce unnecessary disclosure. The resulting compromise is often an interface-control document or sanitised requirement that states what a component must do without explaining the capability that makes the requirement sensitive.

Some special-access contracts are treated as **carve-outs**. In these arrangements, normal industrial-security oversight is removed from the standard defence security organisation and retained by the government component responsible for the programme. Historically, the justification was that ordinary inspection or administrative channels could expose the programme’s existence or protected details. GAO described [carve-out contracts]({{ 'carve-outs-95feed/' | relative_url }}) as special-access contracts for which the usual investigative service had been relieved of inspection responsibility, leaving the sponsoring defence component to administer security. Current Department of Defense policy likewise provides for a separate cognisant security office to oversee cleared contractors supporting these programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/ggd" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

A carve-out narrows the visible network, but it also concentrates responsibility. The programme office must maintain industrial-security expertise, inspect contractor facilities, oversee access lists, investigate incidents and ensure that subcontracting does not create uncontrolled pathways. Earlier GAO reviews questioned whether these specially administered arrangements consistently received adequate inspection and documentation, illustrating a recurring problem: a mechanism intended to hide a programme from unnecessary bureaucracy can also hide weak administration from ordinary scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-162.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nsiad</span></span></span>

Corporate ownership adds another boundary. A company can possess valuable technical skills yet face restrictions because of foreign ownership, control or influence. Depending on the mitigation arrangement, foreign-owned US contractors may be excluded from Top Secret, Special Access Program or similarly sensitive work unless the government makes a specific national-interest determination. This can separate cleared operating units from parts of their own corporate leadership and constrain the movement of people, information and technology inside an otherwise unified company.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/a247113.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

Physical and digital separation reinforce these contractual divisions. Classified material may be stored only in approved spaces and systems, with contractors responsible for protecting information in their custody. Dedicated programme areas, controlled networks, restricted repositories and individually managed access records prevent ordinary corporate tools from becoming routes into the compartment. These measures reduce exposure, but they also make modern collaborative engineering—shared models, distributed software development, automated supply-chain data and rapid design iteration—more cumbersome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117/section-117.15" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ecfr.gov.</span></span></span>

The practical result is that the visible contractor on a black project may represent only the integration centre of a much wider industrial effort. Public histories tend to credit one famous laboratory or programme manager, while the capability actually rests on a chain of specialist firms. Compartmentation makes that chain difficult to reconstruct even after partial declassification, because contracts, company names, technical contributions and security arrangements may be released at different times.

## Integration challenges

Compartmentation works best when a system can be divided into modules with stable, measurable interfaces. It becomes risky when one subsystem’s behaviour strongly affects another. Aircraft signatures, heat management, power generation, flight controls, apertures, software timing and structural loads are tightly coupled. A change that improves one feature may degrade several others. The engineers permitted to see these interactions therefore become disproportionately important.

The essential organisational unit is the **integration team**: a relatively small group authorised to cross selected compartment boundaries. Its job is to maintain the system architecture, control interfaces, resolve requirement conflicts and decide when a supplier needs additional information. In a well-run programme, this group does not simply collect finished components. It tests assumptions early through simulations, hardware rigs, software laboratories, prototypes and progressively more representative system demonstrations.

The difficulty is that secrecy encourages problems to travel upwards. A subcontractor may be unable to diagnose unexpected behaviour because it cannot observe the adjacent subsystem. It reports a symptom to the prime contractor, which may reproduce the issue in a more highly classified laboratory and return a revised specification without disclosing the cause. This relay protects information but lengthens feedback loops. Small misunderstandings can survive until late testing because the people closest to a component do not possess the evidence needed to challenge their instructions.

The [B-2]({{ 'b-2/' | relative_url }}) bomber illustrates how painful late integration can become in a highly classified programme. GAO reported manufacturing defects, inefficient labour and the transfer of work from subcontractor plants to the final-assembly site. One identified cause was the decision to begin manufacturing before the design was complete. This meant that interface and producibility problems that might have been resolved in design reviews instead appeared on the factory floor, where changes were more expensive and disruptive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span></span></span>

Software posed a related problem. The B-2’s integration software had to combine the functions of numerous subsystems so that the aircraft and crew could perform the intended mission. GAO later found that software deliveries arrived late, lacked planned capabilities and contained deficiencies that disrupted flight testing. The episode is a reminder that the final integrator must reconcile not just hardware dimensions but data, timing, control logic and operational behaviour. A set of individually acceptable components can still produce an unacceptable machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-95-164/html/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-95-164.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAOREPORTS NSIAD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAOREPORTS NSIAD</span></span></span>

Secrecy was not necessarily the sole cause of those B-2 difficulties; ambitious requirements, immature technology, concurrency and ordinary programme-management decisions also mattered. That qualification is important. Public evidence rarely allows outsiders to isolate the cost of compartmentation from the inherent difficulty of the technology. What can be said more confidently is that information barriers reduce the number of people able to detect cross-system errors and increase the burden on the few who can.

Several implementation practices help control this risk:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Expose interfaces, not missions.** Suppliers need accurate boundary conditions, loads, tolerances, data definitions and failure responses even when the operational purpose remains concealed.
* **Create authorised technical bridges.** Named engineers should be permitted to work across adjacent compartments rather than forcing all communication through security or contract staff.
* **Integrate early and repeatedly.** Laboratories, rigs and prototypes should reveal incompatibilities before full-scale assembly or flight testing.
* **Control configuration centrally.** Every compartment must work from compatible versions of drawings, software, requirements and interface definitions.
* **Escalate unexplained anomalies.** A supplier that cannot see the cause of a fault must have a protected route to engineers who can.
* **Revisit access as the design changes.** Need-to-know decisions made at programme launch may become technically obsolete when subsystems grow more interdependent.

</div>

These measures do not abolish compartmentation. They make it more precise. The aim is to avoid two extremes: giving so many people access that the special-access boundary becomes meaningless, or withholding so much context that teams build technically correct parts for the wrong system conditions.

## Secrecy as an engineering constraint

The common image of a black-project team is a small band of exceptional engineers freed from paperwork. Historical programmes such as the U-2, A-12 and [F-117]({{ 'f-117/' | relative_url }}) partly support that image: compact advanced-development organisations could move quickly, maintain close contact between designers and test personnel, and protect unusual ideas from premature exposure. The F-117’s full-scale development contract went to Lockheed’s [Skunk Works]({{ 'skunk-works/' | relative_url }}) in 1978 after the Have Blue demonstrator had generated the engineering evidence needed to proceed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.f117sfa.org/f117-site-history" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: f117sfa.org">[F-117 Stealth Fighter Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">f117sfa.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on f117sfa.org.</span></span></span>

But small teams are not effective merely because they are secret. Their advantage comes from authority, technical breadth, stable priorities and rapid access to decision-makers. Secrecy may preserve focus and limit leaks, yet it can equally create duplicated work, staffing bottlenecks and overdependence on individuals who alone understand a critical interface. A departure, clearance delay or access dispute can remove knowledge that was never widely distributed.

Nor does a special-access label guarantee stronger engineering discipline. It adds safeguards beyond ordinary classification, but the contractor still needs sound requirements, realistic schedules, qualified suppliers, configuration control and independent testing. The formal security system can determine who is allowed to know; it cannot determine whether the people inside the compartment are asking the right technical questions.

The most capable secret teams therefore treat information access as part of system design. They identify which facts would reveal the protected capability, which relationships engineers must understand, and which specialists need temporary or continuing access to resolve cross-cutting problems. Compartment boundaries are then managed like physical interfaces: deliberately defined, tested under pressure and changed when they obstruct the performance of the whole machine.

That is the central paradox of special-access engineering. A black project succeeds by preventing most participants from seeing everything, but its success ultimately depends on a trusted minority seeing enough—and having enough authority—to make every hidden piece work together.
