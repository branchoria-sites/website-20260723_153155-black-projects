## Introduction

The A-12 Avenger II programme became one of the clearest demonstrations that a fixed-price development contract cannot eliminate risk when the underlying technology is immature, the design is poorly understood and the customer has few practical alternatives. Rather than transferring financial risk from the US government to industry, the programme accumulated technical, schedule and commercial risks until the [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) declared that they could no longer absorb the losses. When the Navy terminated the contract for default in January 1991, a dispute that lasted more than two decades showed that contractual language alone could not resolve disagreements over responsibility for an increasingly unmanageable programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/mcdonnell-douglas-corp-general-dynamics-corp-v-united-states-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of the Solicitor General &#124; McDonnell Douglas Corp. &amp; General Dynamics Corp. v. Within the broader story of [hidden budgets]({{ 'hidden-budgets/' | relative_url }}) and cost-control risks in [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), the A-12 is important because it illustrates a paradox. Secrecy did not prevent formal oversight, but it reduced outside scrutiny while making optimistic assumptions about cost, schedule and technical feasibility harder to challenge before they became embedded in the contract.

## The contract and the promised transfer of risk

In January 1988 the US Navy awarded McDonnell Douglas and General Dynamics a fixed-price incentive contract to develop eight Full-Scale Engineering Development aircraft with a ceiling price of approximately $4.78 billion. Under this arrangement, the contractors accepted responsibility for overruns beyond negotiated targets up to the contractual ceiling. In theory, this structure encouraged efficient management by making industry rather than government bear the consequences of poor performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/mcdonnell-douglas-corp-general-dynamics-corp-v-united-states-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of the Solicitor General &#124; McDonnell Douglas Corp. &amp; General Dynamics Corp. v. The logic appeared attractive for several reasons:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* the Navy wanted predictable development costs after years of expensive defence programmes;
* a fixed-price incentive contract was expected to motivate tighter engineering discipline;
* competitive bidding suggested the winning team believed the aircraft could be delivered within the agreed budget.

</div>

In practice, however, the contract rested on assumptions that proved unrealistic. Unlike mature production programmes, the A-12 required major advances in carrier-compatible stealth design, large composite structures, manufacturing methods and systems integration. Many of these technologies had not yet been demonstrated together in an operational naval aircraft. As development progressed, uncertainty proved far greater than the pricing model assumed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/mcdonnell-douglas-corp-general-dynamics-corp-v-united-states-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of the Solicitor General &#124; McDonnell Douglas Corp. &amp; General Dynamics Corp. v. United States - Opposition &#124; U...</span></span></span>

The key lesson is that the contract transferred financial exposure on paper without removing the underlying engineering uncertainty. Once technical assumptions failed, the contractual allocation of risk became increasingly detached from reality.

## Design, weight and schedule problems overwhelmed the contract

The most serious problem was that the programme's technical difficulties multiplied faster than either side had anticipated.

The aircraft struggled to remain within its weight targets while also meeting demanding stealth, payload and carrier-operating requirements. Excess weight affected performance, structural design and manufacturing complexity, forcing repeated redesigns rather than isolated engineering corrections. At the same time, integrating advanced low-observable features into a carrier aircraft proved considerably more difficult than anticipated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/mcdonnell-douglas-corp-general-dynamics-corp-v-united-states-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of the Solicitor General &#124; McDonnell Douglas Corp. &amp; General Dynamics Corp. v. United States - Opposition &#124; U...</span></span></span>

These technical problems quickly translated into schedule failure. The first aircraft was contractually due in June 1990, but the contractors missed the delivery date and informed the Navy that completing the programme would require costs substantially above the contractual ceiling. They also acknowledged that certain performance specifications could not be achieved on the existing schedule.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/osg/brief/mcdonnell-douglas-corp-general-dynamics-corp-v-united-states-opposition" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[Department of Justice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of the Solicitor General &#124; McDonnell Douglas Corp. &amp; General Dynamics Corp. v. United States - Opposition &#124; U...</span></span></span>

This created a vicious cycle:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* engineering delays increased labour costs;
* redesign generated additional testing requirements;
* schedule slips reduced confidence in cost estimates;
* growing overruns exceeded the amount the contractors were contractually willing or able to absorb.

</div>

Instead of containing risk, the fixed-price structure magnified commercial pressure on the contractors precisely when technical uncertainty was increasing.

## Why strategic dependence returned risk to government

A common assumption is that fixed-price contracts permanently shift risk to industry. The A-12 demonstrated an important limitation: governments remain exposed when a programme becomes strategically important.

The Navy needed a replacement for the ageing A-6 Intruder and had already invested heavily in the A-12. By the time serious problems emerged, billions of dollars had been spent and alternative solutions were limited. This reduced the practical credibility of simply allowing the programme to fail without wider consequences for naval aviation planning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/t-nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Information on the A-12 Default Termination &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Information on the A-12 Default Termination &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

Although the Navy ultimately terminated the contract for default in January 1991, doing so did not eliminate government exposure. Instead, it shifted the dispute into litigation over responsibility for the collapse.

Following termination:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* approximately $3 billion had already been spent on the programme;
* the government sought recovery of roughly $1.35 billion in progress payments for unfinished work;
* the contractors argued that the termination should have been treated as one for the government's convenience rather than for default;
* litigation continued until a negotiated settlement was finally reached in 2014.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/t-nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Information on the A-12 Default Termination &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Information on the A-12 Default Termination &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

</div>

The government's strategic dependence therefore transformed technical failure into financial and legal risk that could not simply be transferred through contract clauses.

## Secrecy and optimistic assumptions reinforced one another

The A-12 was a highly classified programme, and that classification complicated external evaluation of its progress.

Security restrictions did not eliminate oversight. The programme remained subject to internal reviews, Pentagon supervision and congressional oversight by cleared officials. However, secrecy inevitably narrowed the circle of people able to examine technical assumptions, compare competing engineering approaches or publicly question cost estimates during development.

This mattered because fixed-price development depends heavily on realistic early estimates. If initial assumptions about weight growth, manufacturing complexity or schedule prove too optimistic, later contractual incentives cannot recover the lost realism. ## What the A-12 changed about acquisition thinking

The programme became a cautionary example in defence acquisition rather than proof that fixed-price contracts are inherently flawed.

Its principal lessons were more specific:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* fixed-price development works best when technical uncertainty has already been substantially reduced;
* immature technologies are difficult to price accurately before detailed design is complete;
* contractors cannot absorb unlimited losses without threatening programme viability;
* governments retain substantial strategic, financial and legal exposure even when contracts appear to transfer risk.

</div>

The lengthy legal dispute reinforced that allocating risk in a contract is different from eliminating it. Courts ultimately addressed questions of contractual responsibility, while the broader acquisition community drew a different conclusion: ambitious defence programmes require realistic assessments of technical maturity before financial risk can genuinely be shifted to industry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/government-and-contractors-seek-end-long-running-12-litigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: justice.gov">[justice.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">justice.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of JusticeOffice of Public Affairs &#124; Government and Contractors Seek to End Long-Running “A-12” Litigation &#124; United States Dep...</span></span></span>
