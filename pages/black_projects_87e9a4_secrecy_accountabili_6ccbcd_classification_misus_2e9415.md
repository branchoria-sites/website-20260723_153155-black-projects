## Introduction

Governments may legitimately classify information to protect national security, but United States classification rules draw a clear legal boundary: secrecy cannot be used to shield officials, agencies or contractors from embarrassment. That principle is especially important for [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) and other highly classified programmes, where the public cannot independently verify official claims. Without such a rule, classification could become a tool for avoiding political [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) rather than protecting military capabilities.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_secrecy_accountabili_6ccbcd_classification_misus_2e9415-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Improper Secrecy illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_secrecy_accountabili_6ccbcd_classification_misus_2e9415-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_secrecy_accountabili_6ccbcd_classification_misus_2e9415-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The distinction is straightforward in principle but difficult in practice. Information may remain classified because its disclosure would reveal sensitive technology, [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}) or operational methods. By contrast, information cannot lawfully be classified merely because it would expose poor decisions, administrative mistakes, wasteful spending, procurement failures or institutional embarrassment. The challenge is that outsiders often cannot determine whether secrecy is protecting genuine national security interests or simply protecting reputations.

## What the law prohibits

The central US rule appears in Executive Order 13526, which governs most national security classification. Section 1.7 expressly states that information may not be classified, kept classified or withheld from declassification in order to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* conceal violations of law;
* conceal inefficiency or administrative error;
* prevent embarrassment to a person, organisation or agency;
* restrain competition; or
* delay release of information that does not genuinely require protection for national security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/node/287521" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: presidency.ucsb.edu">[The American Presidency Project]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">presidency.ucsb.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The American Presidency ProjectExecutive Order 13526—Classified National Security Information &#124; The American Presidency ProjectDecember 2...</span></span></span>

</div>

This prohibition reflects an important constitutional principle. Classification exists to protect the nation, not to protect the reputation of the government. A classified aircraft design, intelligence source or cryptographic capability may warrant continued protection because disclosure would aid an adversary. A report showing procurement mismanagement, schedule overruns or flawed decision-making does not become classifiable simply because it would create political difficulties.

For black projects, this distinction matters because unusually restrictive security controls can easily blur the line between operational secrecy and institutional self-protection. The legal framework attempts to ensure that programmes remain secret only to the extent required for security, not to insulate their managers from criticism.

## Why misuse is difficult to detect

Although the prohibition is explicit, proving that classification was motivated by embarrassment rather than security is inherently difficult.

The first obstacle is informational asymmetry. Those challenging a classification decision usually cannot examine the underlying evidence because the material is itself classified. Courts, journalists and members of the public therefore cannot readily determine whether a legitimate security justification exists.

Second, many documents contain both sensitive and embarrassing information. A report may discuss classified sensor performance alongside evidence of management failures. Agencies can legitimately protect the operational details while releasing or segregating the non-sensitive portions. In practice, however, separating those elements is not always straightforward, creating opportunities for overly broad secrecy.

Third, classification decisions are often predictive. Officials are asked whether disclosure could reasonably be expected to damage national security in the future, not whether damage has already occurred. Because such judgements involve uncertainty, agencies usually receive considerable deference unless independent reviewers find the justification unsupported.

These structural features explain why the prohibition on embarrassment is more difficult to enforce than it is to state.

## Black projects and the temptation toward excessive secrecy

Special Access Programs (SAPs), commonly associated with black projects, impose security restrictions beyond ordinary classification. Their purpose is to protect exceptionally sensitive military or intelligence capabilities, not to exempt programmes from accountability.

Historically, however, Congress and the Government Accountability Office (GAO) have expressed concern that extremely restrictive access can complicate oversight. During the late 1980s and early 1990s, GAO reviews found weaknesses in SAP management, inconsistent reporting and the need for stronger oversight mechanisms. These reports did not argue that SAPs were inherently improper; rather, they concluded that stronger institutional review was necessary precisely because secrecy limits normal scrutiny.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">nsiad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Programs: DOD Criteria and Procedures for Creating Them Need Improvement &#124; U.S. GAOMay 24, 1988...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 24, 1988</span></span></span>

This illustrates the broader democratic concern. The more exclusive a programme becomes, the greater the risk that classification decisions may be accepted without meaningful challenge. Even when officials act in good faith, reduced external scrutiny can allow inefficient practices, excessive costs or administrative errors to persist longer than they otherwise would.

## How independent review enforces the rule

Because the public cannot directly verify most classified information, enforcement depends on institutions with authorised access rather than complete transparency.

Several mechanisms are designed to provide that independent review:

* **Congressional oversight.** Selected congressional committees and cleared members receive classified briefings and funding information for sensitive programmes, enabling scrutiny without [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}).
* **Inspectors General.** Agency Inspectors General can investigate allegations of waste, fraud, abuse or improper classification while maintaining necessary security protections.
* **The Information Security Oversight Office (ISOO).** ISOO oversees implementation of the national security classification system across the executive branch and promotes compliance with classification standards established by executive order.
* **Government Accountability Office reviews.** Although GAO access has historically been contested in some highly classified programmes, its audits have repeatedly highlighted improvements needed in oversight, reporting and management of Special Access Programs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

These mechanisms do not eliminate misuse, but they reduce the likelihood that classification decisions remain entirely within the control of the same officials whose actions may be under scrutiny.

## Why the rule matters for democratic accountability

The prohibition against classifying information to avoid embarrassment serves two complementary purposes.

First, it preserves the legitimacy of classification itself. Public confidence in genuine national security secrecy depends on the belief that classification protects military and intelligence interests rather than political convenience. If classification became a general mechanism for concealing mistakes, confidence in necessary secrecy would steadily erode.

Second, it distinguishes secrecy from impunity. Democratic systems accept that some information must remain hidden from adversaries, but they reject the idea that secrecy should prevent authorised oversight. Sensitive programmes can remain operationally secret while still being evaluated for legality, effectiveness, fiscal responsibility and compliance with policy.

For black projects, this distinction is particularly significant. Advanced capabilities may require extraordinary protection, but the legal authority for that protection ends where its purpose changes from safeguarding national security to shielding officials, agencies or [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) from legitimate scrutiny.
