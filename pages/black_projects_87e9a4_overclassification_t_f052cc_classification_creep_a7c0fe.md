## Introduction

Classification creep is the process by which a single legitimate secrecy decision gradually expands far beyond its original justification. Within [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}), this usually does not happen because officials repeatedly make new decisions to classify the same information. Instead, it spreads through **derivative classification**: people preparing new documents, databases, contracts or briefings copy existing classification guidance and markings into fresh material. Over time, one cautious judgement can shape an entire network of records, even after the original security rationale has weakened or become outdated. This mechanism contributes directly to over-classification, making programmes harder to oversee, more difficult to declassify and less transparent without necessarily improving security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

## How derivative classification reproduces earlier decisions

Most people working with classified information do not possess authority to make original classification decisions. Instead, they perform **derivative classification**, meaning they create new documents by incorporating, summarising or paraphrasing information that has already been classified, or by following an approved [classification guide]({{ 'guide-rules/' | relative_url }}). Their task is not to reconsider whether secrecy remains justified, but to carry forward existing markings accurately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

This process is essential for consistency across large organisations. A black project involving government agencies, [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) and laboratories may generate thousands of reports, presentations, software records and procurement documents. Requiring every author to reassess national security risks independently would produce inconsistent results and increase the chance of accidental disclosure.

The same mechanism, however, also enables classification creep. Once an original decision is embedded in a security classification guide, later authors generally inherit it automatically. Each derivative document then becomes another source from which future documents are created, extending the original judgement through successive generations of records. The spread resembles a chain reaction:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* an original authority classifies sensitive information;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/faqs/original-classification-authority-derivative-classification" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Is the statement, &quot;original classification authority may extend the duration of classification up to 25 years from the date of th...</span></span></span>
* the decision is incorporated into a classification guide or marked document;
* new reports inherit those markings;
* later reports cite those reports rather than revisiting the original rationale;
* databases, briefings and contract documentation repeat the same restrictions.

</div>

Because derivative classifiers are expected to preserve earlier decisions rather than revise them, the system naturally favours continuity over reassessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

## Why one sensitive paragraph can classify an entire document

Classification creep also spreads through document structure itself. Under US classification rules, the **overall classification** of a document is determined by its highest classified portion. A report containing one Secret paragraph and twenty pages of unclassified administrative information is still marked overall as Secret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

This rule protects against accidentally separating sensitive material from its context, but it creates practical consequences:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* entire files become harder to circulate;
* electronic repositories restrict access to everything inside the document;
* future authors often copy the whole document into new products rather than extracting only the sensitive section;
* administrative information becomes tied permanently to classified records.

</div>

The result is not necessarily that every sentence becomes classified in its own right. Rather, non-sensitive material becomes trapped inside classified containers, reducing its accessibility even when its contents would not independently justify protection.

Over years of revisions, annexes, appendices and briefing slides, these inherited restrictions accumulate. The classified portion may remain unchanged while surrounding material expands dramatically, increasing the amount of information requiring special handling.

## Contracts, briefings and databases multiply the effect

Black projects rarely exist as a single document. They depend upon interconnected systems of technical drawings, budget paperwork, supplier instructions, testing records, security procedures and programme management databases.

Classification guidance therefore propagates through organisational infrastructure as well as written reports.

For example, if a contract references a classified subsystem, later procurement documents may inherit the same markings. Training presentations prepared from those contracts repeat the restrictions. Metadata entered into classified databases is then exported into reports for programme managers, inspectors and auditors. Even routine scheduling information may remain within classified systems because it accompanies genuinely sensitive technical material.

Each step may be individually reasonable. Collectively, they can produce extensive classified ecosystems in which many records remain restricted primarily because earlier documents carried the same markings rather than because each record received an independent assessment.

Compartmented programmes intensify this effect. Because relatively few people possess access to the entire project, there are fewer opportunities for someone to notice that inherited restrictions have become broader than necessary.

## Why copied secrecy becomes difficult to reverse

Classification systems contain formal procedures for declassification and periodic review, but inherited restrictions often prove much easier to create than to remove.

Several factors contribute to this imbalance.

First, reviewing thousands of derivative records individually requires substantial resources. Even if the original technical capability has become obsolete, agencies must determine whether later documents reveal additional information whose combination could still cause damage.

Secondly, derivative documents often cite multiple classified sources. Regulations require classifiers to carry forward the longest applicable declassification instructions when several sources are combined. As information from different origins becomes merged, the resulting records frequently inherit the most restrictive timetable among them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

Finally, institutional incentives generally discourage removing classifications prematurely. The consequences of under-classifying genuinely sensitive information are immediate and potentially severe, whereas unnecessary secrecy usually carries fewer personal risks for individual officials. This asymmetry encourages cautious decisions that accumulate over time.

## How precise marking limits unnecessary secrecy

Modern classification policy attempts to reduce classification creep through increasingly precise marking requirements rather than simply lowering classification levels.

One important safeguard is **portion marking**. Instead of labelling an entire document at a single level, individual paragraphs, tables and graphics are marked separately as Unclassified, Confidential, Secret or Top Secret. Regulations explicitly state that each portion should reflect its own classification rather than automatically inherit the highest level elsewhere in the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

Proper portion marking produces several benefits:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* reviewers can identify genuinely sensitive passages quickly;
* unclassified sections can often be released independently;
* later authors can extract only the protected information needed for new documents;
* derivative classifiers are less likely to elevate unrelated material unnecessarily.

</div>

The implementing directives also require derivative classifiers to identify the source from which their classification decisions were derived. This creates an audit trail linking later documents back to original guidance, making later review more feasible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo implementing directive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security Information; Final Rule &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2010</span></span></span>

These safeguards cannot eliminate classification creep entirely, particularly in highly compartmented black projects, but they reduce the tendency for one broad judgement to spread automatically through every subsequent document.

## Why classification creep matters for trust

The practical consequence of classification creep is not merely that more documents receive security markings. It changes how outsiders perceive secrecy itself.

When historical releases reveal that procurement records, meeting agendas or administrative correspondence remained classified largely because they inherited markings from genuinely sensitive technical material, observers may conclude that classification was excessive across the entire programme. That perception can weaken confidence in legitimate secrecy surrounding capabilities that still require protection.

For black projects, the challenge is therefore not only deciding what deserves protection initially but ensuring that derivative classification remains precise as programmes evolve. Careful portion marking, clear classification guides and regular review help prevent one defensible secrecy decision from expanding into an unnecessarily broad network of restricted information that ultimately undermines both transparency and public [trust]({{ 'trust/' | relative_url }}).
