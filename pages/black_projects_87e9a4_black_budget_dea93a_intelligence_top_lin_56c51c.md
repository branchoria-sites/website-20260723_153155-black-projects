## Introduction

Public releases of the United States [intelligence budget]({{ 'budget-games/' | relative_url }}) answer one important question while leaving many others unresolved. They reveal the overall scale of national intelligence spending through annual top-line figures for the National Intelligence Program (NIP) and the Military Intelligence Program (MIP), allowing observers to track broad funding trends over time. They do not, however, disclose which classified programmes receive funding, which contractors perform the work, how successful individual efforts have been, or which activities are expanding or shrinking. That distinction is central to understanding [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}): the published totals provide evidence that can be analysed, but they are intentionally designed to conceal operational and programme-level detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

## Why NIP and MIP totals matter

The intelligence budget is divided into two broad funding categories. The National Intelligence Program finances the activities of the U.S. Intelligence Community that serve national-level decision-makers, while the Military Intelligence Program funds intelligence activities supporting military operations. Since the late 2000s, the government has released annual aggregate figures for these programmes, creating a public record of overall intelligence expenditure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

## What the published figures still omit

The usefulness of the top-line figures ends where programme-level secrecy begins.

The public totals do not reveal how money is distributed among the eighteen elements of the Intelligence Community, nor do they identify individual classified programmes. An increase of several billion dollars may reflect satellite development, cyber capabilities, artificial intelligence, signals intelligence, human intelligence, secure communications, personnel costs, or numerous unrelated activities occurring simultaneously. The published number alone cannot distinguish among these possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/IF/IF10524/11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on congress.gov.</span></span></span>

Just as importantly, the released totals conceal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* individual programme names;
* spending by agency beyond limited unclassified appropriations;
* contractor allocations;
* technical objectives;
* acquisition schedules;
* programme performance;
* cancellations and cost overruns;
* classified research priorities.

</div>

The ODNI explicitly states that, beyond disclosure of the aggregate NIP figure, no additional currently classified budget information will be released except where specific appropriations are already unclassified. That statement illustrates that the published number is intended as a transparency measure without becoming an intelligence disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4078-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceDNI Releases FY 2026 Budget Request Figure for the National Intelligence Program &#124; Office of the Directo...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters when discussing black projects. A publicly announced increase in intelligence spending does not automatically indicate expansion of any particular classified aircraft, satellite constellation, cyber capability or special access programme. Numerous unrelated classified and unclassified activities compete within the same aggregate budget.

## Why a larger budget does not identify individual black projects

One common misunderstanding is that a rising intelligence budget provides evidence that specific secret programmes are growing.

In reality, the aggregate figures are too coarse to support such conclusions.

Several entirely different developments could produce the same increase in the published top line:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* higher personnel costs across multiple agencies;
* inflation affecting existing contracts;
* replacement of ageing satellite infrastructure;
* investment in secure information technology;
* expanded cyber operations;
* increased analytic staffing;
* new classified collection systems.

</div>

Without programme-level appropriations, outside observers cannot reliably attribute changes to any individual capability. Intelligence oversight committees inside Congress receive substantially more detailed classified budget information than the public does precisely because aggregate figures cannot support programme oversight on their own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/IF/IF10524/11" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on congress.gov.</span></span></span>

This is why open-source researchers generally combine budget data with procurement records, [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) documents, contracting information, Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office reports, inspector general findings and occasional official disclosures when attempting to understand classified spending patterns.

## How readers should interpret spending changes

Changes in published intelligence totals should be interpreted as indicators of strategic direction rather than direct evidence about specific secret activities.

Several practical principles help avoid overinterpretation.

**Treat the totals as measures of scale.** They demonstrate how much the United States is willing to devote overall to intelligence capabilities but not how that money is allocated internally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

**Separate requests from appropriations.** Budget requests represent executive branch priorities, while appropriated amounts reflect congressional decisions and may differ substantially. Both figures are publicly released for the NIP, allowing comparison between proposed and enacted funding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/what-we-do/ic-budget" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

**Recognise that not all intelligence spending appears in these totals.** Congressional Research Service analyses note that some intelligence-related activities remain outside both the NIP and MIP because departments maintain intelligence capabilities supporting their own missions without those activities being counted within the formal intelligence budget. The published totals therefore describe the principal intelligence programmes rather than every intelligence-related expenditure across the federal government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span></span></span>

**Avoid assuming direct links to individual technologies.** Headlines announcing larger intelligence budgets cannot, by themselves, confirm funding for stealth aircraft, advanced surveillance systems, artificial intelligence programmes or other frequently discussed black projects.

## The value and limits of transparency

The publication of intelligence budget top lines represents a compromise between democratic accountability and operational secrecy.

On one hand, annual disclosure allows citizens, researchers and legislators outside classified committees to monitor long-term spending trends and debate the overall scale of intelligence investment using official data rather than speculation. It also makes historical comparisons possible and provides an objective baseline for discussions about the size of the intelligence enterprise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R44381" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Community Spending Trends &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress</span></span></span>

On the other hand, the disclosures are deliberately structured to reveal almost nothing about the composition or effectiveness of classified programmes. Knowing that tens of billions of dollars have been appropriated says little about which projects exist, which have succeeded, which have failed, or where future priorities lie. The public sees the financial outline of the intelligence system, while the detailed map beneath that outline remains classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/press-releases/press-releases-2025/4078-pr" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National IntelligenceDNI Releases FY 2026 Budget Request Figure for the National Intelligence Program &#124; Office of the Directo...</span></span></span>
