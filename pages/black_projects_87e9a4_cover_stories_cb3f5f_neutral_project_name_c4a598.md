## Introduction

Secret programmes cannot operate without paperwork. Staff must be hired, contracts issued, budgets approved and facilities managed, even when the underlying mission is highly classified. For that reason, many [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) receive deliberately ordinary names that can circulate through administrative systems without revealing what the programme actually does. Rather than describing the technology or objective, these neutral identifiers allow thousands of routine actions to proceed while limiting how much information any individual document exposes.

This approach is not primarily about creating an elaborate fictional [cover story]({{ 'cover-story/' | relative_url }}). Instead, it is a practical security mechanism that separates a programme's public administrative identity from its classified purpose. The name becomes one layer in a broader system of compartmentalisation, ensuring that most people who encounter it in day-to-day work learn only that a project exists—not what it actually does.

## What neutral identifiers hide

A neutral project name works by removing descriptive meaning. Instead of labels such as "stealth bomber development" or "satellite reconnaissance system", sensitive programmes receive names that reveal little or nothing about their purpose.

Modern Special Access Program (SAP) policy illustrates this clearly. US Air Force guidance requires every SAP to have an **unclassified nickname** for administration and congressional reporting, while a separate classified code word may be assigned for more sensitive uses. This separation achieves several goals simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It prevents administrative documents from disclosing operational capabilities.
* It allows individuals outside the operational compartment to process paperwork.
* It reduces the number of classified documents that require highly restrictive handling.
* It makes isolated records less meaningful if viewed without wider context.

</div>

The effectiveness comes from context rather than secrecy of the word itself. A neutral label may be entirely unclassified, yet remain useful because only a limited number of authorised people know what it represents.

## How names travel through routine paperwork

Even the most sensitive programme generates ordinary administrative activity. Human resources departments issue employment records. Procurement officers approve invoices. Security offices maintain access lists. Financial staff process payments. Most of these functions do not require detailed knowledge of the underlying technology.

Neutral names therefore become the common language of bureaucracy.

A purchase order might reference only a project nickname. A visitor request may mention only the administrative identifier. Travel authorisations, property inventories, [maintenance]({{ 'maintenance/' | relative_url }}) schedules and budget submissions can all use the same harmless label without revealing whether the project concerns reconnaissance aircraft, cyber capabilities or advanced sensors.

This creates an important separation between **administrative knowledge** and **operational knowledge**.

Someone processing expenses may legitimately know that "Project Atlas" exists without knowing whether it involves aircraft testing, software development or laboratory research. Another employee may understand part of the technical work without having access to the complete programme. Compartmentalisation means no single routine document tells the whole story.

The system therefore protects information not by eliminating records, but by ensuring that individual records reveal very little in isolation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/saf_aa/publication/afi16-701/afi16-701.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">afi16 701</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">E-PublishingAIR FORCE INSTRUCTION 16-701December 7, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 7, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why boring names are often more effective than dramatic ones

Popular [culture]({{ 'culture/' | relative_url }}) often portrays secret projects as having mysterious or intimidating code names. In practice, administrative nicknames are frequently intentionally dull.

A descriptive title attracts attention because it encourages questions. By contrast, a generic administrative label blends into the thousands of ordinary project references that exist within large government departments and defence [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}).

Security planners generally prefer names that:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* avoid describing technology or geography;
* have no obvious connection to the mission;
* can appear naturally in routine correspondence;
* are unlikely to attract curiosity if encountered outside their intended audience.

</div>

US military naming guidance similarly distinguishes between unclassified **nicknames**, used for administration and public communication where appropriate, and classified **code words**, which protect genuinely sensitive information. The two serve different purposes rather than representing different levels of theatrical secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/navy_nicknames_and_codeword_ma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Navy Nicknames and Codeword Management</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American ScientistsNavy Nicknames and Codeword Management - Federation of American Scientists...</span></span></span>

## The Manhattan Project: a famous example of administrative naming

The best-known historical example demonstrates how an apparently ordinary name can conceal extraordinary work.

Before becoming known as the Manhattan Project, the programme briefly used the title "Laboratory for the Development of Substitute Materials". General Leslie Groves considered that wording too revealing because it implied unusual scientific research. The programme was instead organised administratively as the **Manhattan Engineer District**, following the Army Corps of Engineers' convention of naming districts after their headquarters location in Manhattan. The resulting title concealed the programme's true purpose while appearing to describe an ordinary engineering organisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/new-york-new-york.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: home.nps.gov">[nps.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">home.nps.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Park Service New York, New York (U.S. National Park Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Park ServiceNew York, New York (U.S. National Park Service)January 14, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 14, 2026</span></span></span>

The choice illustrates several characteristics of successful neutral naming:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* it had a plausible administrative explanation;
* it fitted existing organisational practice;
* it revealed nothing about nuclear weapons;
* it remained usable across contracts, correspondence and financial records.

</div>

Importantly, the name alone did not secure the project. Security depended equally on compartmentalisation, controlled access and strict [need-to-know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}) rules. The neutral title simply ensured that routine paperwork did not advertise the programme's true objective.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://home.nps.gov/mapr/faqs.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: home.nps.gov">[National Park Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">home.nps.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Park Service Frequently Asked Questions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Park ServiceFrequently Asked Questions - Manhattan Project National Historical Park (U.S. National Park Service)...</span></span></span>

## When a harmless label becomes suspicious

A neutral identifier does not remain effective indefinitely. As projects mature, outsiders may begin connecting scattered clues.

Several developments can transform an ordinary label into an object of interest:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* repeated appearances across unrelated procurement records;
* unusually large funding associated with an otherwise unknown project;
* concentration of specialist contractors;
* recurring references in technical documents;
* links to restricted facilities or unusual testing activity.

</div>

Investigative journalists, historians and researchers often reconstruct formerly secret programmes by assembling these fragments rather than by discovering a single revealing document. A name that initially appeared meaningless can eventually become a searchable thread connecting contracts, environmental filings, budget documents and [declassified records]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}).

For this reason, organisations sometimes retire identifiers, rename programmes or create new compartments as projects evolve. The objective is to prevent long-term administrative accumulation from creating an increasingly complete public picture.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_cover_stories_cb3f5f_neutral_project_name_c4a598-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Neutral Names illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_cover_stories_cb3f5f_neutral_project_name_c4a598-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_cover_stories_cb3f5f_neutral_project_name_c4a598-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

## Neutral names conceal identity, not existence

One common misunderstanding is that neutral names are intended to make programmes invisible. In reality, they usually acknowledge that complete invisibility is impossible.

Large defence programmes inevitably leave administrative traces. People are hired, facilities constructed and money spent. Neutral naming accepts this reality by ensuring that these traces reveal as little as possible individually.

The mechanism succeeds because it separates three distinct questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Does a programme exist?** Administrative records may answer yes.
* **Who is authorised to work on it?** Access controls determine this separately.
* **What does it actually do?** That information remains confined to those formally read into the relevant compartment.

</div>

Viewed in isolation, a neutral project name is simply an organisational label. Only when combined with classified context—or later historical evidence—does it acquire its true meaning. That separation between ordinary administration and sensitive mission is precisely why deliberately boring names have remained a durable feature of black projects and other highly compartmented government programmes.
