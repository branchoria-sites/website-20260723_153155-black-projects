## Introduction

Public intelligence and defence authorisation laws can appear surprisingly sparse because many of their most consequential spending decisions do not appear in the published text. Instead, Congress often places programme-level funding decisions, directions to agencies and detailed spending schedules into classified annexes that accompany legislation. These annexes have legal force for the executive branch while remaining unavailable to the general public. The result is a system in which citizens can see that money has been authorised, but not which specific classified programmes received it, how much individual projects cost or which activities Congress directed agencies to expand or reduce. This mechanism is one of the principal ways that [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) remain financially concealed without removing them entirely from legislative oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/118th-congress/house-report/162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">H. ## How classified schedules work

The key mechanism is the use of a classified schedule of authorisations attached to an otherwise public intelligence authorisation bill. Congress publishes the broad legislation, but the accompanying classified annex contains programme-by-programme funding levels, explanatory language and directives that are distributed only to Members of Congress and staff with the appropriate clearances.

Modern Intelligence Authorisation Act committee reports explicitly state that the classified Schedule of Authorizations is incorporated into the Act itself and therefore carries the legal status of public law. Agencies are instructed to comply with the classified directions just as they would with any published statutory requirement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/118th-congress/house-report/162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">H. Rept.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">INTELLIGENCE AUTHORIZATION ACT FOR FISCAL YEAR 2024 &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of CongressAugust 18, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 18, 2023</span></span></span>

The classified annex typically includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Funding amounts for individual classified programmes.
* Adjustments to the President's requested budget.
* Programme-specific reporting requirements.
* Restrictions on spending.
* Explanatory guidance for intelligence agencies.
* Reprogramming instructions governing future budget changes.

</div>

Because these details are classified, the enacted public law may authorise billions of pounds or dollars in aggregate spending while revealing almost nothing about how those totals are divided among individual secret programmes.

## What the public bill leaves out

The public Intelligence Authorization Act usually identifies broad authorisations for intelligence activities but omits the detailed allocation of resources.

For example, committee reports accompanying recent Intelligence Authorization Acts note that only limited unclassified appropriations can be discussed publicly because the underlying intelligence activities remain classified. The reports explain that the detailed recommendations reside in the classified annex rather than in the published legislation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/committee-report/119th-congress/senate-report/51/1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">S. Rept.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">INTELLIGENCE AUTHORIZATION ACT FOR FISCAL YEAR 2026 &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of Congress...</span></span></span>

As a result, the public generally cannot determine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which classified satellite programme received increased funding.
* Whether a covert collection capability was expanded or reduced.
* Which Special Access Program experienced cost growth.
* Whether Congress rejected or modified an executive request for a specific classified system.
* Which agencies received detailed operational guidance.

</div>

The published law therefore communicates that Congress approved intelligence spending without exposing the operational map behind that spending.

## Why the annexes have legal force

One common misconception is that classified annexes are merely advisory. In practice, intelligence committee reports repeatedly state that the classified Schedule of Authorizations is incorporated into the statute itself and possesses the status of law. Agencies are directed to follow both the public legislation and the classified committee guidance contained in the annex.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/118th-congress/house-report/162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">H. Rept.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">INTELLIGENCE AUTHORIZATION ACT FOR FISCAL YEAR 2024 &#124; Congress.gov &#124; Library of CongressAugust 18, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 18, 2023</span></span></span>

Separate statutory provisions also require the executive branch to submit classified [intelligence budget]({{ 'budget-games/' | relative_url }}) justification materials to the congressional intelligence and appropriations committees shortly after the President submits the annual budget. Those materials provide Congress with the detailed explanations needed to evaluate programmes whose specifics cannot be discussed publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/50/3105a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Legal Information Institute50 U.S. Code § 3105a - Classified intelligence budget justification materials &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Leg...</span></span></span>

This creates two parallel versions of the budget process:

Public processClassified processAggregate funding requestsProgramme-level budget justificationPublished committee reportsClassified committee annexesPublic appropriations totalsClassified funding schedulesGeneral policy languageDetailed operational direction

The public can therefore observe the existence of intelligence spending without seeing the granular decisions that determine where the money ultimately goes.

## Why hidden line items matter for black projects

For black projects, secrecy depends on more than simply classifying technical documents. Financial visibility can itself reveal sensitive information.

If every classified aircraft, satellite or intelligence capability appeared as a named line item in public appropriations legislation, analysts could infer:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the existence of previously unknown programmes;
* relative programme priorities;
* sudden increases associated with new technologies;
* production schedules;
* programme cancellations; and
* shifts in strategic emphasis.

</div>

Even without technical specifications, budget patterns can reveal strategic intentions. Keeping programme-level appropriations inside classified schedules therefore limits the ability of foreign intelligence services, defence analysts and commercial competitors to reconstruct sensitive capabilities from financial records alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12943" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 14, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 14, 2025</span></span></span>

## Oversight without public disclosure

Classified annexes are designed to preserve congressional oversight rather than eliminate it. Members of the intelligence committees, appropriations committees and authorised staff review detailed funding proposals, question agency officials and make changes before authorisation. The executive branch must then execute spending in accordance with those classified instructions.

Committee reports also specify who may review the annexes under controlled conditions, reinforcing that secrecy is directed at [public disclosure]({{ 'public-disclosure/' | relative_url }}) rather than at Congress itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/118th-congress/house-report/162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">H. Critics nevertheless argue that this arrangement limits broader democratic [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) because outside researchers, journalists and ordinary taxpayers cannot independently evaluate many programme-level spending decisions. Supporters counter that revealing those details could expose intelligence priorities, collection methods or technological capabilities.

## The practical effect on understanding black budgets

The existence of classified annexes explains why public intelligence budgets often appear internally incomplete. Published totals are genuine, but they function as the visible cover page for a much larger classified accounting system.

Consequently, open-source analysts can estimate overall intelligence spending and sometimes identify major procurement trends, yet they cannot reconstruct the complete distribution of funds among classified programmes from public appropriations law alone. The hidden line items remain embedded within classified schedules that possess legal authority but are available only to officials with the appropriate access and [need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congress.gov/congressional-report/118th-congress/house-report/162" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congress.gov">[Congress.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congress.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">H.
