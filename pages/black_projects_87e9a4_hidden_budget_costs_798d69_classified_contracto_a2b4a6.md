## Introduction

One of the least visible [cost risks]({{ 'cost-risk/' | relative_url }}) in [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) is not a secret technology but a secret workforce. Once a contractor has assembled a team with the necessary security clearances, specialist facilities and years of compartmented programme knowledge, replacing that company becomes far more difficult than replacing an ordinary supplier. Even if another firm appears technically capable, it may lack cleared personnel, accredited facilities or access to the classified design history needed to assume responsibility without major delays or additional cost.

This creates a form of contractor lock-in. Governments may retain legal authority to recompete a contract, but the practical cost of transferring knowledge and rebuilding a cleared workforce can make continued reliance on the incumbent appear to be the least risky option. The result is weaker competitive pressure, reduced bargaining power and a greater chance that long-running classified programmes become expensive dependencies rather than contestable procurements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Contracting: Opportunities Exist to Increase Competition and Assess Reasons When Only One Offer Is Received &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 26...</span></span></span>

* Personnel with appropriate security clearances, sometimes including access to highly compartmented programmes.
* Secure facilities accredited to handle classified information.
* Specialised information technology and document-control systems that satisfy industrial security requirements.
* Proven experience working under classified acquisition rules.

</div>

Each requirement raises the cost of entering the competition before a company can even demonstrate its technical qualifications. Security clearance processing has historically taken months or longer, and delays have repeatedly been identified by the Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office (GAO) as increasing programme costs and making it harder for industry to recruit and retain qualified personnel.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-06-748t" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">06 748t</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Personnel Clearances: New Concerns Slow Processing of Clearances for Industry Personnel &#124; U.S. GAOMay 17, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2006</span></span></span>

The defence industrial base is also relatively concentrated in several highly specialised sectors. GAO has repeatedly identified limited supplier capacity, workforce shortages and dependence on small numbers of specialised firms as structural industrial-base risks. When only a handful of companies possess both the technical capability and the required cleared workforce, genuine price competition becomes difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Industrial Base: Integrating Existing Supplier Data and Addressing Workforce Challenges Could Improve Risk Analysis &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

The effect is magnified in programmes protected by Special Access Program (SAP) controls, where access is limited even among already-cleared personnel. A company cannot simply recruit experienced engineers from elsewhere if those engineers have never been read into the specific compartment containing the programme's technical information.

## The cost of transferring classified knowledge

Replacing a contractor involves much more than moving contract documents between companies.

Long-running classified programmes accumulate large amounts of tacit knowledge that may not exist in formal documentation, including:

* Design trade-offs that were never publicly recorded.
* Classified testing history.
* Manufacturing techniques developed inside secure facilities.
* Relationships between subsystem [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) operating under different security [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}).
* Operational lessons learned from classified users.

Much of this expertise resides in individuals rather than documents. Although governments normally own or control many programme deliverables, the practical understanding needed to continue development often remains concentrated within the incumbent workforce.

Knowledge transfer is further complicated because every engineer joining the successor programme must receive the necessary clearances and compartmented access before reviewing sensitive material. Even where clearances already exist, additional programme-specific access approvals may be required, creating delays that would not occur on conventional projects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-06-748t" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">06 748t</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Personnel Clearances: New Concerns Slow Processing of Clearances for Industry Personnel &#124; U.S. GAOMay 17, 2006...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 17, 2006</span></span></span>

This produces a paradox. Competition is intended to lower long-term costs, yet changing [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) may require overlapping teams, duplicate secure facilities and months of transition activity before productive work resumes. Programme managers therefore face a difficult trade-off between preserving competition and avoiding disruption to highly sensitive capabilities.

## Why incumbents gain lasting bargaining power

Over time, successful performance can itself become a competitive advantage.

An incumbent contractor often possesses:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* The largest population of programme-cleared staff.
* Mature secure manufacturing infrastructure.
* Existing relationships with classified government customers.
* Detailed understanding of programme-specific certification requirements.
* Historical technical data that allows quicker engineering decisions.

</div>

None of these advantages necessarily result from anti-competitive behaviour. Instead, they emerge naturally as classified work continues over many years.

The difficulty is that every additional contract extension further strengthens the incumbent's position. As more classified knowledge accumulates inside one organisation, alternative suppliers become progressively less credible, making future competitions less effective than they appear on paper.

GAO has identified similar dynamics in broader federal procurement. Reviews of non-competitive contracts found that agencies frequently struggled to compete follow-on work because they lacked sufficient access to proprietary technical data and had become heavily dependent on contractor expertise developed over many years. Officials interviewed by GAO described some programmes as effectively being "stuck" with particular contractors because switching suppliers had become impractical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Contracting: Opportunities Exist to Increase Competition and Assess Reasons When Only One Offer Is Received &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 26...</span></span></span>

Although those findings were not limited to classified programmes, secrecy can intensify the problem by restricting who can review technical information and participate in transition planning.

## Contractor lock-in is not only about intellectual property

A common assumption is that lock-in arises mainly because companies own proprietary technology. Intellectual property certainly matters, but classified programmes often face additional barriers even when governments possess broad contractual rights to technical data.

Other sources of dependency include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Cleared personnel scarcity.** Experienced engineers with high-level clearances represent a limited labour pool.
* **Accredited facilities.** Secure laboratories, production areas and information systems require time and investment to establish.
* **Compartmented experience.** Engineers familiar with one classified programme cannot automatically work on another.
* **Security compliance history.** Government customers may place substantial value on contractors with proven records of handling highly sensitive information.

</div>

Industrial security itself also creates continuing oversight costs. Recent GAO work notes that the Defence Counterintelligence and Security Agency conducts thousands of industrial security reviews annually while monitoring hundreds of security violations and vulnerabilities across cleared contractor facilities. Maintaining compliant secure infrastructure is therefore a continuing investment rather than a one-time qualification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107861/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[GAO Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO FilesGAO-26-107861, INDUSTRIAL SECURITY: Improved Risk Management and Stakeholder Engagement Needed to Help DOD Address Mission GapsA...</span></span></span>

## Ways government can preserve bargaining power

Governments cannot eliminate every source of contractor dependency in highly classified programmes, but acquisition policy can reduce the risk that a single supplier becomes indispensable.

Important approaches include:

* **Retaining sufficient technical data rights.** Access to design information makes future competition more realistic and reduces dependence on contractor-held knowledge. GAO has repeatedly identified inadequate access to technical data as a major obstacle to competition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Contracting: Opportunities Exist to Increase Competition and Assess Reasons When Only One Offer Is Received &#124; U.S. GAOJuly 26...</span></span></span>

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Maintaining an informed government technical workforce.** Civil servants and military programme offices that understand the system independently are better positioned to oversee transitions and challenge contractor claims.
* **Planning transitions early.** Knowledge-transfer requirements should be considered before contracts near completion rather than after a replacement has been selected.
* **Supporting multiple qualified suppliers.** Where practical, governments can encourage second-source capability for critical components or maintain competition during early development rather than waiting until production.
* **Monitoring industrial-base resilience.** Understanding supplier concentration and workforce shortages helps acquisition officials recognise where future dependence may emerge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defense Industrial Base: Integrating Existing Supplier Data and Addressing Workforce Challenges Could Improve Risk Analysis &#124; U.S. GAO...</span></span></span>

</div>

These measures do not guarantee successful competition, particularly where only a few firms possess the necessary security capabilities. They do, however, reduce the likelihood that secrecy itself evolves into a structural source of bargaining weakness.

## Why this matters for hidden programme costs

Contractor lock-in illustrates how secrecy can influence programme economics without directly increasing engineering costs. A classified programme may remain technically successful while becoming progressively harder to compete because specialised knowledge, cleared personnel and secure infrastructure accumulate inside one organisation.

That dependency affects negotiations long before any formal sole-source contract is awarded. If government officials believe replacing the incumbent would delay delivery, increase security risk or require rebuilding years of classified expertise, the threat of competition becomes less credible. The hidden budget consequence is not necessarily higher prices in every case, but weaker market discipline over time—a subtle cost-control risk that is especially relevant to long-lived black projects.
