## Introduction

In highly compartmented defence and intelligence programmes, different [contractors]({{ 'contractors/' | relative_url }}) and engineering [teams]({{ 'teams/' | relative_url }}) are often given only the information necessary to design their assigned component. Instead of seeing the complete system, they receive tightly controlled interface specifications describing how their subsystem must connect to others. This approach protects sensitive capabilities and missions, but it introduces a recurring engineering risk: components that individually satisfy every documented requirement may still fail when they are first assembled into the complete system.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_compartmented_teams_8af5ba_sanitized_interface_b7b1e4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Integration Failures illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_compartmented_teams_8af5ba_sanitized_interface_b7b1e4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_compartmented_teams_8af5ba_sanitized_interface_b7b1e4-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The central problem is not that interface documents are inherently inadequate. Well-designed Interface Control Documents (ICDs) and Interface Requirements Documents (IRDs) are fundamental systems engineering tools. Rather, difficulty arises when security-driven sanitisation removes assumptions that engineers need to understand why an interface exists, what operating conditions truly matter, or how apparently unrelated subsystems influence one another. Final integration therefore becomes the first occasion on which hidden dependencies are exposed, often revealing problems that no individual contractor could reasonably have predicted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/reference/system-engineering-handbook-appendix/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">System Engineering Handbook: Appendix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">System Engineering Handbook: Appendix - NASAJuly 26, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 26, 2023</span></span></span>

These documents intentionally define the boundary between systems without describing the classified purpose of the system itself. The omitted information can be equally important. Sanitised documentation may not disclose:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Why particular tolerances are unusually strict.
* Which operating modes are most mission-critical.
* Hidden performance margins required elsewhere in the system.
* Future growth plans or classified upgrades.
* Sensitive algorithms, signatures or operational concepts.

As a result, engineers understand the interface but not necessarily the engineering rationale behind it.

This distinction matters because interfaces rarely capture every assumption. An electrical connector specification may define voltage and timing perfectly while omitting the operational sequence that makes those timings meaningful. A structural interface may specify allowable loads without revealing the rare manoeuvre that drives the actual design limit. Such omissions are deliberate from a security perspective but increase the likelihood of incompatible design assumptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/reference/system-engineering-handbook-appendix/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">System Engineering Handbook: Appendix</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">System Engineering Handbook: Appendix - NASAJuly 26, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 26, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why local optimisation creates system-wide problems

[Compartmentation]({{ 'compartmentation/' | relative_url }}) encourages teams to optimise within their own contractual boundaries. From an individual contractor's perspective, success means meeting the documented specification at acceptable cost, weight, reliability and schedule.

The complete programme, however, depends upon interactions extending beyond those documented boundaries.

Several recurring mechanisms explain why locally correct designs can collectively produce system failures.

**Hidden assumptions become incompatible.** Two teams may interpret unspecified operating conditions differently. Since neither possesses the overall system model, both designs appear valid until integration.

**Performance margins disappear.** Individual components may each consume slightly more electrical power, cooling capacity, bandwidth or processing time than originally estimated. If every supplier independently uses its available margin, the combined system exceeds available resources despite every subsystem passing acceptance testing.

**Interface specifications freeze while designs evolve.** Classified programmes frequently experience design refinement inside secure [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}). Unless every affected interface document changes simultaneously, different teams may unknowingly work against different assumptions.

**Mission priorities remain invisible.** Engineers naturally optimise measurable parameters they can observe. Without understanding the broader mission, they may improve one characteristic while unintentionally degrading another that was intentionally prioritised elsewhere.

Systems engineering literature consistently identifies interfaces—not isolated components—as one of the highest sources of programme risk because organisational boundaries frequently coincide with technical boundaries. NASA's lessons learned from the Space Shuttle programme explicitly note that many failures occur at organisational and hardware interfaces and argue for early, continuous interface management rather than treating integration as a late-stage verification exercise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20100015497.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerProject Interface Requirements Process Including Shuttle Lessons Learned - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

## Why final integration becomes the first true system test

For compartmented projects, subsystem qualification does not necessarily demonstrate system success.

Each contractor may have verified its hardware against the sanitised interface specification while never exercising the complete operational chain. Integration therefore represents the first opportunity to observe emergent behaviour—the behaviour produced only when independent subsystems interact.

Typical integration discoveries include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Timing interactions that only appear when every processor, sensor and communications link operates simultaneously.
* Electromagnetic interference between separately compliant electronic systems.
* Thermal interactions in which individually acceptable heat loads collectively exceed cooling capacity.
* Software behaviours triggered by combinations of messages that no isolated development environment reproduced.
* Mechanical tolerances that stack together across multiple assemblies.

</div>

None of these failures necessarily indicates poor engineering by individual suppliers. Instead, they reflect information that only exists when the entire architecture is viewed together.

Modern systems engineering therefore treats integration as more than assembly. It is the process of validating assumptions that could never be fully tested within isolated development environments. Interface documentation reduces uncertainty but cannot eliminate dependencies that extend beyond the documented boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nist.gov/publications/systems-engineering-foundations-software-systems-integration" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nist.gov">[NIST]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Systems Engineering Foundations of Software Systems Integration &#124; NIST</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Systems Engineering Foundations of Software Systems Integration &#124; NIST...</span></span></span>

## Historical patterns from aerospace and defence programmes

Publicly available information on classified programmes is necessarily limited, making it difficult to attribute individual integration failures directly to compartmentation. However, open engineering literature and historical aerospace experience illustrate consistent patterns.

The Space Shuttle programme generated extensive lessons learned emphasising that interface management required continuous coordination across organisations rather than reliance on static documentation alone. NASA subsequently strengthened formal interface processes, recommending dedicated interface engineering, early negotiation of interface requirements and continual revision of Interface Control Documents throughout development.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrs.nasa.gov/archive/nasa/casi.ntrs.nasa.gov/20100015497.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrs.nasa.gov">[NASA Technical Reports Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrs.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Technical Reports ServerProject Interface Requirements Process Including Shuttle Lessons Learned - NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS)...</span></span></span>

Large international scientific projects provide a comparable engineering model without the security restrictions. Facilities such as the Gemini Observatory and the Thirty Meter Telescope rely on independently developed subsystems connected through formal interface documentation. Their published systems engineering work highlights that interface control documents function as contractual boundaries, but also require ongoing verification because interfaces evolve throughout development rather than remaining fixed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://webarchive.gemini.edu/systems/interfaces.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: webarchive.gemini.edu">[gemini.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">webarchive.gemini.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Systems Engineering</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Interface Control...</span></span></span>

The broader lesson applies equally to compartmented defence projects: documentation alone cannot substitute for system-level understanding.

## How integration teams reduce the risk

Special-access programmes generally recognise these risks and establish small, highly trusted integration groups that possess broader visibility than individual contractors.

Rather than exposing the entire programme to every supplier, these teams attempt to bridge compartment boundaries by:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Maintaining authoritative interface control documents under strict change management.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nasa.gov/reference/6-3-interface-management/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[nasa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">6.3 Interface Management</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">6.3 Interface Management</span></span></span>
* Running system-level simulations using representative subsystem models.
* Conducting progressive integration instead of waiting until every subsystem is complete.
* Verifying assumptions through joint interface testing before full system assembly.
* Managing configuration changes centrally so interface revisions propagate consistently.

</div>

The objective is not to eliminate compartmentation but to prevent security controls from becoming engineering blind spots.

Increasingly, systems engineering also supplements document-based interface management with digital system models that allow interface consistency to be checked automatically as designs evolve. Research into model-based systems engineering and documentation-as-code reflects recognition that static documents alone struggle to capture rapidly changing interfaces in complex programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11668" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the tension cannot be completely removed

The underlying trade-off is structural rather than procedural. Compartmentation protects sensitive capabilities by limiting knowledge, while successful systems engineering depends upon understanding interactions across subsystem boundaries.

Sanitised interfaces represent a compromise between those competing objectives. They enable independent development without exposing classified system details, but they inevitably simplify reality. The more information withheld to preserve security, the greater the chance that important engineering assumptions remain implicit rather than documented.

For that reason, final integration retains exceptional importance in special-access programmes. It is often the first point at which the complete system—not merely its carefully separated parts—can be evaluated under realistic operating conditions, revealing interactions that no individual contractor had sufficient visibility to anticipate.
