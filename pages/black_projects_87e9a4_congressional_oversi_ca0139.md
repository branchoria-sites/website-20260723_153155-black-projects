## Introduction

Congressional oversight of hidden defence programmes is built around a compromise: Congress is meant to know enough to authorise, fund and challenge secret work, while the public record [reveals]({{ 'reveals/' | relative_url }}) little or nothing about the programme itself. In the United States, the main formal mechanism is the Special Access Program, or SAP, a highly controlled category used for some of the most sensitive defence and intelligence activities. These programmes are not supposed to operate outside law; they are supposed to operate inside restricted reporting channels.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_congressional_oversi_ca0139-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Oversight" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The central rule is simple but often misunderstood: secrecy from the public does not automatically mean secrecy from Congress. Title 10 of the US Code requires the Secretary of Defense to report annually to congressional defence committees on Department of Defense SAPs, including requested funding, programme descriptions, milestones, past costs and future cost estimates. It also requires notice and justification for new SAPs, advance reporting before public declassification announcements, and a 30-day waiting period before most SAPs can begin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

## The Committees That See What the Public Cannot

Oversight of hidden defence programmes is concentrated in a small set of committees rather than spread across all members of Congress. Under Title 10, the “defence committees” are the Armed Services Committees and Appropriations Committees in both chambers, including the defence appropriations subcommittees. These are the bodies expected to handle classified budget material, hold closed briefings, scrutinise line items, and decide whether secret programmes deserve continued funding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

That design has a practical logic. A stealth aircraft programme, classified sensor system or compartmented intelligence capability may involve details that would be valuable to an adversary: performance limits, vulnerabilities, contractors, locations, mission assumptions or collection methods. The oversight system therefore narrows access to legislators and staff with the relevant committee role, clearance arrangements and secure facilities.

But narrowed access also creates a democratic tension. Most members of Congress may vote on defence authorisation or appropriations bills without seeing the full classified annexes behind them. A Congressional Research Service report on intelligence spending notes that classified parts of defence authorisation bills can cover special access or “black” programmes, including intelligence activities and procurement of new weapons systems such as stealth aircraft; members may obtain information about those classified parts from the Armed Services Committees, but the detail is not placed in ordinary public legislation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/94-261.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Every CRS Report Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS Report Intelligence Spending: Public Disclosure Issues</span></span></span>

This is why congressional oversight of [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) is less like a public hearing and more like a controlled audit chain. The public sees the broad legal structure, occasional aggregate budgets, selected declassified histories and rare controversies. The committees see more, but even they may receive information in compartmented form.

## What the Law Requires Defence Officials to Report

The key statutory checklist in 10 U.S.C. §119 is more detailed than the phrase “[black budget]({{ 'black-budget/' | relative_url }})” suggests. [Annual SAP reports]({{ 'annual-reports/' | relative_url }}) must include the total amount requested for Department of Defense SAPs in the President’s budget and, for each SAP in that budget, a brief description, major milestones, actual costs for previous years, estimated total costs, and projected costs for the current budget year and four succeeding years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

New SAPs trigger a separate reporting duty. By 1 February each year, the Secretary of Defense must provide notice of each new SAP designation and justify why the programme needs that protection. That report must include the current estimate of total programme cost and identify existing programmes or technologies with similar missions or technology. This matters because one oversight risk in hidden programmes is duplication: two compartments may be working on similar capabilities without enough cross-checking outside the compartment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

The law also addresses changes in secrecy status. If the Department of Defense plans to change the classification of a SAP or declassify information about it for public release, it must give the defence committees a report describing the proposed change, the reasons and any planned public announcement. In ordinary circumstances that report must arrive at least 14 days before the change or announcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

A newer parallel provision, 10 U.S.C. §119a, applies to programmes managed under “alternative compensatory control measures”, another form of special handling for sensitive defence work. It mirrors the SAP oversight structure: annual reports, cost estimates, milestone information, new-programme notices, declassification reporting, case-by-case waiver authority and a 30-day notice-and-wait rule before initiation in most cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

## Defence Committees Are Not the Only Gatekeepers

Congressional oversight depends partly on what happens inside the Pentagon before material ever reaches Capitol Hill. The Department of Defense has its own SAP governance structure, including the DoD Special Access Program Central Office, or SAPCO, and internal oversight authorities. A current DoD instruction assigns senior officials responsibility for maintaining SAPCO offices and overseeing SAPs within their areas. It also gives the Director for Defense Intelligence a role in preparing and submitting DoD intelligence SAP reports to congressional intelligence committees when required by law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Environmental Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

That internal structure is not a substitute for Congress, but it shapes what Congress receives. DoD SAP offices manage access lists, programme security, congressional liaison and the mechanics of preparing reports. A Department of Defense security manual states that DoD SAPCO functions as the single point of congressional liaison for SAPs and verifies that congressional offices approved to process and store DoD SAP information meet security requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[Defense Environmental Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

Older but still useful policy descriptions show the same model: the Special Access Program Oversight Committee was described as reviewing SAPs annually, looking at cost, schedule and performance, and validating whether continued compartmentation was needed or whether a programme should be restructured, downgraded or terminated. It also had a stated role in ensuring required information was provided to Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/othergov/sapoc.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Access Program Oversight Committee (SAPOC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Program Oversight Committee (SAPOC</span></span></span>

The point is not that internal Pentagon review guarantees [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}). It is that congressional oversight of black projects is usually mediated through a security bureaucracy. A member or staffer does not simply ask a contractor for all files. Access is channelled through DoD rules, SAPCO procedures, secure rooms and programme access approvals.

## Waived Programmes Show the Sharpest Oversight Tradeoff

The most sensitive category is the “waived” SAP. Under §119, the Secretary of Defense may waive certain reporting details case by case if including them in a report would harm national security. But the information and the justification for the waiver must still be provided jointly to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

DoD policy has treated waived SAPs as an even tighter channel. A 2010 Department of Defense directive stated that members assigned to designated defence and intelligence committees are authorised access to SAPs within their oversight role, except for waived programmes. Unless the Secretary or Deputy Secretary approves broader access, only the chair, ranking minority member and staff directors of the relevant committees are authorised to access waived SAPs. It also states that waived SAP material submitted to Congress remains in DoD custody unless senior approval is given otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dami.army.pentagon.mil/site/sci/docs/DoD%205205_07.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dami.army.pentagon.mil">[dami.army.pentagon.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dami.army.pentagon.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DoD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD</span></span></span>

That is the oversight dilemma in its clearest form. The rule does not say “no congressional knowledge”. It says knowledge is compressed to a very small number of people. Supporters see this as necessary for extremely sensitive capabilities. Critics worry that it can reduce oversight to a handful of briefed leaders who may lack the time, technical support or [staff access]({{ 'staff-access/' | relative_url }}) needed to challenge a programme’s assumptions.

## The B-2 Shows How Oversight Can Move From Classified to Public

The [B-2]({{ 'b-2/' | relative_url }}) bomber is a useful example because it shows that congressional scrutiny can begin in secret and later become public. The Government Accountability Office reported in 1990 that, before that year, all aspects of the B-2 programme were highly classified. The report was prepared at the request of Representative Les Aspin, then chair of the House Armed Services Committee, and GAO noted that it had already issued five classified reports on the B-2 since 1986 before classification changes permitted an unclassified report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NSIAD-90-120 Strategic Bombers: B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-90-120 Strategic Bombers: B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span></span></span>

That sequence matters. Public oversight did not begin only when the aircraft was visible. A classified oversight track existed first: committee request, GAO review, classified reporting, later partial release. Once more information could be disclosed, the public learned about cost, schedule and test issues without receiving every performance detail. The 1990 GAO report said the B-2’s cost and schedule had changed significantly since 1986, that the final delivery had been extended, and that the acquisition strategy depended on high annual funding and ordering many aircraft before testing proved critical performance requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NSIAD-90-120 Strategic Bombers: B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-90-120 Strategic Bombers: B-2 Program Status and Current Issues</span></span></span>

The B-2 case also shows why secrecy is not only about hiding exotic technology. It can hide ordinary programme-management risks: cost growth, optimistic schedules, immature testing and contractor performance. Oversight committees therefore need more than technical briefings. They need budget history, milestone tracking, comparable programmes, independent auditors and enough cleared staff to test the claims being made inside the compartment.

## UAP Records Highlight a Newer Transparency Pressure

Recent congressional attention to unidentified anomalous phenomena, or UAP, is not the same as oversight of every black defence programme. Still, it illustrates a modern pressure point: Congress has become more willing to legislate record searches and reporting channels when it suspects that compartmented information may be poorly visible outside specialised offices.

The 2024 National Defense Authorization Act required the National Archives and Records Administration to establish a UAP Records Collection and required federal agencies to review, identify and organise UAP-related records in their custody for disclosure and transmission to the Archives. The National Archives guidance says the collection covers government, government-provided or government-funded records relating to UAP, technologies of unknown origin and equivalent subjects, while excluding temporarily non-attributed objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Separately, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence stated that the 2024 consolidated annual UAP report was submitted to Congress in classified form, with an unclassified report made public.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/index.php/newsroom/reports-publications/reports-publications-2024/4020-uap-2024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">4020 uap 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">4020 uap 2024</span></span></span> This split mirrors the broader black-project oversight model: Congress receives a classified layer, the public receives a filtered layer, and the argument continues over whether the filtering is appropriately narrow or too protective.

The UAP example should not be inflated into proof of hidden extraordinary technology. The stronger point for this topic is procedural. When Congress worries that hidden programmes or records may sit outside normal visibility, it can create record-collection duties, reporting deadlines and classified-plus-unclassified reporting requirements. That is a governance intervention, not a public reveal-all.

## What Oversight Can and Cannot Do

Congressional oversight of hidden defence programmes can do several important things. It can force annual reporting, require cost estimates, compare new projects with existing capabilities, delay programme initiation until committees are notified, condition funding, request GAO reviews, hold closed hearings and shape later declassification. These tools make black projects accountable to elected institutions even when most voters cannot see the details.

But oversight has limits that are built into the design. Information may be compartmented so tightly that only a few legislators and staff can assess it. Some material may remain in DoD custody. GAO access can require concurrence from defence or intelligence committee leaders and DoD SAPCO, plus the appropriate clearance. Personal congressional staff are not granted SAP access under DoD rules, and even professional staff access may be limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dami.army.pentagon.mil/site/sci/docs/DoD%205205_07.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dami.army.pentagon.mil">[dami.army.pentagon.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dami.army.pentagon.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DoD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD</span></span></span>

The system therefore depends heavily on the seriousness of committee leadership, the quality of cleared professional staff, the willingness of agencies to provide candid briefings, and the ability of auditors to examine classified records. A lawful reporting channel is not the same as robust scrutiny. It is the minimum architecture that makes scrutiny possible.

For readers trying to understand black projects, the key takeaway is this: hidden defence programmes are not meant to be invisible to Congress, but Congress sees them through narrow, classified and sometimes highly restrictive channels. The unresolved governance question is whether those channels are strong enough to catch waste, duplication, legal drift or strategic overreach before secrecy turns from protection into insulation.
