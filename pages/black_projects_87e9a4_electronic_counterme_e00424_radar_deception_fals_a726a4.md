## Introduction

Within the electronic countermeasures associated with advanced military aircraft, deception jamming represents a different philosophy from simple noise jamming. Instead of trying to drown out a radar with overwhelming radio energy, it attempts to manipulate the radar's own tracking logic so that it measures the wrong position, speed or direction. The objective is not necessarily to make an aircraft disappear, but to persuade the radar that the aircraft is somewhere else, moving differently, or accompanied by convincing false echoes. This selective approach can be particularly valuable for low-observable aircraft because it complements stealth rather than replacing it. Publicly available research and electronic warfare literature consistently describe deception techniques as exploiting the way tracking radars process returns instead of merely overpowering their receivers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1979/november/u-s-navy-electronic-warfare-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute The U. ## How false range and velocity cues are created

A tracking radar does not simply display every reflected pulse. It estimates where a target should appear next and centres electronic tracking windows—known as tracking gates—around the expected return. These gates continuously update as the target appears to move.

Deception jamming exploits this prediction process. A jammer first receives the radar pulse, then retransmits an altered version that closely resembles the genuine reflection. Because the retransmitted signal is coherent with the original radar waveform, it appears authentic to the victim radar. Modern systems commonly achieve this using Digital Radio Frequency Memory (DRFM), which digitises the incoming pulse, stores it briefly, modifies it if required, and retransmits an accurate copy with carefully controlled timing or frequency changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_radio_frequency_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digital radio frequency memory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital radio frequency memory</span></span></span>

### Range deception

The best-known example is **Range Gate Pull-Off (RGPO)**.(#endnote-4 "Endnote 4")<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_gate_pull-off" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Range gate pull-off</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Range gate pull-off</span></span></span>

Initially, the false echo is transmitted at almost exactly the same delay as the genuine reflection, making both returns appear to originate from the same location. Once the radar has accepted the stronger artificial return, the jammer gradually increases the transmission delay by tiny increments. Since radar calculates distance from signal travel time, the delayed echo appears progressively farther away.

Rather than suddenly jumping to a false position—which would be rejected by many tracking algorithms—the deception moves smoothly enough that the radar's automatic range tracker follows the counterfeit return. Eventually the radar's tracking gate separates from the aircraft's real reflection, causing it to track an increasingly inaccurate range estimate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_gate_pull-off" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Range gate pull-off</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Range gate pull-off</span></span></span>

### Velocity deception

Pulse-Doppler radars measure target speed through Doppler frequency shift rather than timing alone.

Against these systems, **Velocity Gate Pull-Off (VGPO)** gradually alters the apparent Doppler frequency of the retransmitted signal instead of its delay. The radar's velocity tracker interprets this as a target whose speed is changing steadily. As with RGPO, the manipulation is gradual enough that the tracking filter follows the counterfeit measurement until the genuine aircraft falls outside the radar's accepted velocity window.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_gate_pull-off" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Range gate pull-off</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Range gate pull-off</span></span></span>

### Creating multiple ghost targets

Rather than producing a single misleading echo, a sufficiently capable coherent jammer can generate numerous delayed and Dop Doppler-shifted copies of the incoming pulse. Each modified copy may appear to the radar as an independent aircraft at a different range or velocity.

To the radar operator or automated tracker, these synthetic returns can resemble a formation of genuine contacts. The challenge becomes identifying which, if any, corresponds to the real aircraft. Modern research into anti-deception radar increasingly focuses on distinguishing these false tracks from authentic targets because high-fidelity replay techniques can generate highly convincing phantom returns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_radio_frequency_memory" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Digital radio frequency memory</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Digital radio frequency memory</span></span></span>

## How deception disrupts tracking and weapon handover

The value of deception lies less in confusing a search radar than in interfering with the precise tracking needed for weapon employment.

Air-defence systems generally pass through several stages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* initial detection;
* target tracking;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395371424_Random_Finite_Set-Based_Mitigation_of_Radar_Range_Deception_Jamming_for_Single-Target_Tracking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[researchgate.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* fire-control solution generation;
* missile guidance or weapon handover.

</div>

The later stages demand far greater positional accuracy than simple surveillance. Small errors in measured range, velocity or angle can degrade engagement quality or force the radar to restart the tracking process.

If a tracking radar follows a counterfeit return instead of the genuine aircraft, the resulting fire-control solution is based on incorrect information. Even if the aircraft remains detectable, the system may be tracking the wrong point in space. Breaking this continuous track can interrupt missile guidance or require the defender to reacquire the target before continuing the engagement. Publicly available literature consistently describes RGPO and VGPO as techniques intended to break or divert radar lock rather than merely reducing detection probability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_gate_pull-off" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Range gate pull-off</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Range gate pull-off</span></span></span>

In the context of advanced low-observable aircraft, deception therefore complements stealth. Stealth reduces detection range and tracking quality, while selective deception attempts to exploit whatever tracking opportunity the radar still obtains.

## Why selective deception can outperform brute-force noise

Noise jamming works by raising the background energy received by the radar until genuine echoes become difficult to distinguish. This often requires substantial transmitting power, especially against modern radars operating at relatively short ranges.

Deception instead attempts to insert believable information into the radar's measurement process. Because the retransmitted signal is designed to resemble a legitimate radar echo, it can often achieve its effect with considerably less radiated energy than would be required simply to overwhelm the receiver. The radar is effectively persuaded to make an incorrect measurement rather than prevented from making any measurement at all.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1979/november/u-s-navy-electronic-warfare-part-2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: usni.org">[U.S. Naval Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">usni.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Naval Institute The U. S. Navy: Electronic Warfare (Part 2) &#124; Proceedings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Naval InstituteThe U. S. Navy: Electronic Warfare (Part 2) &#124; Proceedings - November 1979 Vol. 105/11/921...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 1979</span></span></span>

For aircraft whose survivability already depends on limiting their electromagnetic signature, this distinction is important. Broadcasting high-power noise continuously can advertise the aircraft's presence and approximate direction. A selective deception technique may require shorter transmissions directed only against a specific radar at a specific moment, making it more compatible with emissions control and low-observable operations.

## Why modern radars make deception increasingly difficult

Deception jamming has never been a guaranteed solution. Its effectiveness depends heavily on understanding the victim radar's behaviour and accurately reproducing its transmitted waveform.

Modern radar designers have introduced numerous countermeasures intended to reduce vulnerability, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* rapid frequency agility between successive pulses;
* changing pulse repetition intervals;
* adaptive waveforms;
* monopulse angle tracking;
* multiple coordinated sensors;
* sophisticated signal processing that searches for inconsistencies between genuine and replayed returns.

</div>

These improvements force deceptive jammers to react faster and reproduce increasingly complex signals. The resulting competition has become an ongoing cycle in which radar developers seek methods for recognising replay attacks while electronic warfare engineers develop more faithful and adaptive signal generation techniques. Recent research reflects this continuing contest, focusing on algorithms capable of detecting, classifying and mitigating sophisticated false-target attacks generated by coherent replay systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00285" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arxiv.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Advances in Anti-Deception Jamming Strategies for Radar Systems: A Survey</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Advances in Anti-Deception Jamming Strategies for Radar Systems: A Survey</span></span></span>

## What this means for secret aircraft programmes

Although the specific electronic warfare suites fitted to classified aircraft remain largely undisclosed, publicly released defence documentation and open technical literature indicate that survivability increasingly depends on integrating passive sensing, low observability and selective electronic deception rather than relying on any single technique.

Exactly how individual black programmes implement deception remains classified. However, the underlying mechanism is well understood in the open literature: instead of merely hiding from radar, sophisticated deception seeks to reshape the radar's perception by feeding it carefully engineered false measurements.
