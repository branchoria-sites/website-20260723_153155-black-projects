## Introduction

Secret intelligence programmes are designed to protect sensitive capabilities, not to operate without scrutiny. Routine inspections are one of the few mechanisms that allow senior leaders to verify that classified activities remain lawful, effective and aligned with policy while keeping operational details secret. When those inspections stop—or when findings are not systematically followed up—the result is not simply less paperwork. Decision-makers lose an independent view of what is happening inside highly classified organisations, unresolved problems can persist for years, and confidence in internal compliance begins to rest on self-reporting rather than verification.

A significant example emerged in 2024 when the U.S. Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office (GAO) reviewed the Department of Defense's intelligence oversight programme. The review concluded that the department had ceased conducting regular inspections of individual intelligence organisations, had shifted towards broader thematic assessments, and lacked a reliable process for tracking whether inspection recommendations had actually been implemented. GAO warned that these changes introduced oversight risks requiring mitigation rather than assuming that alternative methods provided equivalent assurance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106190" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Intelligence: Actions Needed to Strengthen Program Oversight and Manage Risks &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why routine inspections matter

Routine intelligence inspections differ from criminal investigations or financial audits. They are periodic examinations intended to test whether classified activities continue to comply with law, executive orders, internal directives and operational standards.

Within highly classified programmes, inspections typically examine questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* whether intelligence collection remains within authorised legal boundaries;
* whether sensitive information is properly protected;
* whether previous compliance problems have been corrected;
* whether oversight procedures function consistently across organisations;
* whether leadership receives accurate reporting about operational risks.

</div>

Because many intelligence activities cannot be evaluated through public scrutiny or conventional external auditing, inspections provide an independent internal check. They allow inspectors to compare written policies with actual practice through document reviews, interviews and site visits rather than relying solely on management assurances.

This distinction becomes particularly important for programmes protected by special access restrictions or compartmented classification. The fewer people who can observe an activity, the more valuable independent inspection becomes as a safeguard against unnoticed drift in procedures or legal compliance.

## What changes when inspections stop?

Stopping routine inspections does not necessarily mean oversight disappears entirely. Organisations may still conduct self-assessments, respond to complaints or undergo reviews focused on specific high-risk issues. However, these approaches answer different questions and create different blind spots.

The GAO's 2024 review found that the Department of Defense intelligence oversight office had moved away from inspecting individual organisations and instead concentrated on department-wide topic assessments. While those thematic reviews could identify systemic issues, GAO concluded that ending regular organisational inspections created risks that required explicit mitigation because certain problems might no longer be detected through the new approach alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106190" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Intelligence: Actions Needed to Strengthen Program Oversight and Manage Risks &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

Several practical consequences follow when inspection cycles lapse.

**Loss of independent verification.** Managers increasingly rely on components' own descriptions of compliance instead of direct observation. Even well-intentioned organisations can overlook weaknesses in their own procedures.

**Reduced visibility for senior leadership.** Executives receive fewer objective assessments comparing conditions across multiple organisations, making it harder to distinguish isolated problems from recurring patterns.

**Delayed identification of emerging risks.** Regular inspections often detect procedural weaknesses before they become serious legal or operational failures. Longer intervals between inspections allow deficiencies to become embedded in routine practice.

**Less consistent oversight.** Organisations receiving fewer inspections may gradually develop different interpretations of common intelligence rules, creating uneven compliance across the wider intelligence enterprise.

## The cost of untracked recommendations

Inspections create value only if their findings lead to measurable improvements. A report identifying weaknesses is not, by itself, evidence that those weaknesses have been corrected.

The 2024 GAO review highlighted this implementation problem directly. Between 2017 and 2022, the Department of Defense intelligence oversight office issued hundreds of recommendations following oversight activities. However, GAO found that the office did not consistently monitor whether all recommendations had been implemented, leaving leadership without reliable information about whether identified problems had actually been resolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106190" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Intelligence: Actions Needed to Strengthen Program Oversight and Manage Risks &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

This creates several governance problems.

First, unresolved recommendations can quietly accumulate across multiple review cycles. Without systematic tracking, inspectors may repeatedly identify similar deficiencies without knowing whether earlier corrective actions failed or were never completed.

Second, leaders lose an accurate picture of organisational performance. A programme may appear compliant because recommendations have been issued, while in reality many remain outstanding.

Third, institutional learning weakens. Recommendation tracking allows oversight organisations to identify recurring failures across different units, helping distinguish isolated mistakes from structural weaknesses requiring broader policy changes.

For intelligence organisations operating largely outside public visibility, this internal feedback loop is particularly important because external stakeholders cannot independently observe whether reforms have taken effect.

## A concrete lesson from the 2024 federal review

The GAO review illustrates that inspection systems themselves require oversight.

Rather than arguing that thematic assessments were inherently ineffective, GAO concluded that the Department of Defense needed to recognise and manage the risks created by discontinuing regular inspections. It therefore recommended three principal improvements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* strengthen monitoring of inspection recommendations;
* mitigate the risks associated with no longer conducting routine organisational inspections;
* improve the quality and credibility of intelligence oversight assessments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106190" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Intelligence: Actions Needed to Strengthen Program Oversight and Manage Risks &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

</div>

The recommendations reflected a practical implementation concern rather than an argument against secrecy. Intelligence activities could remain fully classified while still being subject to systematic inspection and documented follow-up.

The department agreed with the recommendations concerning recommendation tracking and mitigation of inspection risks. According to GAO's implementation updates, the Department of Defense subsequently developed a reporting tool to monitor oversight findings and recommendations, illustrating how oversight systems themselves can be strengthened without reducing operational secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106190" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 24 106190</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Intelligence: Actions Needed to Strengthen Program Oversight and Manage Risks &#124; U.S. GAOFebruary 13, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 13, 2024</span></span></span>

## Restoring reliable oversight reporting

Inspection programmes become more dependable when they focus not only on producing reports but also on demonstrating that oversight continues to function over time.

Effective implementation generally includes several reinforcing practices:

* maintaining predictable inspection schedules rather than relying solely on reactive reviews;
* tracking every recommendation until evidence confirms completion or justified closure;
* reporting implementation status to senior leadership using standard metrics;
* documenting why inspection models change and identifying any resulting [oversight gaps]({{ 'oversight-gaps/' | relative_url }});
* periodically evaluating whether revised oversight methods provide equivalent assurance to the systems they replaced.

These measures preserve a central principle of democratic accountability for classified activities: secrecy may restrict public access to operational information, but it should not prevent authorised officials from independently determining whether intelligence organisations remain lawful, properly managed and responsive to identified problems.

Within the broader governance of [black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }}) and other highly classified programmes, routine inspections therefore serve as an internal substitute for public transparency. When they lapse—or when their recommendations disappear into administrative backlogs—the immediate consequence is not necessarily misconduct. Rather, it is growing uncertainty. Senior leaders become less able to distinguish verified compliance from assumed compliance, increasing the risk that significant legal, procedural or management failures remain hidden until exposed by a crisis, whistleblower or external investigation.
