## Introduction

Special Access Programs, usually called SAPs, are the formal machinery behind many US “[black projects]({{ 'black-projects/' | relative_url }})”. They do not create a new classification level above Top Secret. Instead, they add a tighter access system around particular classified information: fewer people, named access lists, special briefings, extra handling rules, dedicated facilities, and a governance trail that is itself partly hidden. Executive Order 13526 defines a SAP as a programme for a specific class of classified information that imposes safeguarding and access requirements beyond those normally required at the same classification level. It also says SAPs should be limited to programmes where the ordinary access population is “reasonably small” and proportionate to the need for enhanced protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">executive order classified national security information</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/black_projects_87e9a4_special_access_progr_550fe1-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for SAPs" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters. A black project is the public phrase; a SAP is the control regime that decides who may know the project exists, who may see which details, how Congress is notified, and where public knowledge is supposed to stop.

## What SAP Controls Add

The basic US classification system already restricts information marked Confidential, Secret or Top Secret. A SAP adds a second gate. Someone may hold a Top Secret clearance and still be barred from a SAP because they have not been approved for that programme, have not signed the required paperwork, or lack a specific [need to know]({{ 'need-to-know/' | relative_url }}). The Department of Defense’s current SAP instruction says it establishes policy, responsibilities and procedures for the “management, administration, and oversight” of all DoD SAPs under Executive Order 13526 and DoD SAP policy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

In practice, SAP controls are designed to make secrecy active rather than passive. They do not merely stamp a document with a classification level; they create a controlled environment around the work. The main added controls include:

* **Compartmented access:** SAP information is divided into programme-specific access channels. DoD policy recognises SAP architecture with [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}), sub-compartments and projects, so a person read into one part does not automatically see everything else.
* **Programme access [approval]({{ 'approval/' | relative_url }}):** access normally requires a programme access request, approval by the relevant SAP authority, and an indoctrination briefing before the person is “read in”.
* **Special indoctrination:** the 2025 DoD SAP Security Manual says individuals must sign a SAP Indoctrination Agreement before indoctrination and that the briefing should teach what the programme’s critical programme information is and how to safeguard it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>
* **Recurring training:** all personnel with SAP access must receive initial training before or during their first SAP briefing and annual training thereafter; training must be updated when relevant security classification guidance, protected information, acknowledgement status, or threats to the programme change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>
* **Dedicated facilities and systems:** SAP information may be handled only inside approved spaces and on authorised systems, often referred to in DoD policy as SAP facilities and SAP information systems.
* **Centralised paperwork and templates:** the Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency maintains SAP-specific process, procedure, document, form and template repositories for government organisations and contractors working under the DoD SAP Security Manual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dcsa.mil/Industrial-Security/Special-Access-Programs-Templates/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dcsa.mil">[dcsa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dcsa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Access Programs Templates</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Programs Templates</span></span></span>

This is why SAPs are central to black projects. The value of a sensitive aircraft, intelligence sensor, cyber capability or weapons concept may depend not only on the technology but on adversaries not knowing what to look for. SAP controls are the administrative way to keep the circle small enough that the programme can function while reducing the number of people, offices, contractors and systems that could expose it.

## Who Gets Access

A SAP is not accessed by rank, job title or clearance level alone. The usual formula is clearance eligibility plus need to know plus programme-specific approval. DoD SAP guidance uses “need to know” as a defined access concept, and the SAP manual ties access to approved programme processes rather than general possession of a clearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The process can sound bureaucratic, but the governance logic is simple: every person added to a SAP becomes another possible route for compromise. Access therefore tends to be handled through named roles, approved billets, indoctrination agreements and programme-specific security officers. Contractors may also be included, but usually through formal contract security channels and programme-specific direction rather than ordinary corporate access.

The public often treats “Top Secret”, “SCI” and “SAP” as if they were interchangeable badges. They are not. Sensitive Compartmented Information, or SCI, is a control system most associated with [intelligence sources]({{ 'intelligence-sources/' | relative_url }}) and methods; SAPs are programme-centred special access controls used widely in defence and other national-security settings. The Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency explains the broader principle plainly: there are three clearance levels, but some classified information has extra protection measures such as SCI and SAP, and special approval is required to access it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dcsa.mil/Industrial-Security/Entity-Vetting-Facility-Clearances-FOCI/Facility-Clearances/FAQs-Facility-Security-Officers/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dcsa.mil">[dcsa.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dcsa.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dcsa.mil.</span></span></span>

## Acknowledged, Unacknowledged and Waived SAPs

SAP secrecy is not one fixed setting. The Department of Defense distinguishes between SAPs whose existence can be acknowledged and those whose very existence is restricted. The DoD instruction defines an **acknowledged SAP** as one whose existence is made known, while specific details such as technologies, materials and techniques remain classified. It defines a **waived SAP** as an unacknowledged SAP for which the Secretary of Defense has waived applicable reporting after determining that normal reporting would adversely affect national security; [waived SAPs]({{ 'waived-saps/' | relative_url }}) have more restrictive reporting and access controls than other unacknowledged SAPs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

That distinction is one of the most important ways secrecy is controlled. An acknowledged SAP may let the government say, in effect, “this programme exists, but the details are classified”. An unacknowledged SAP may prevent officials from confirming the programme at all to people outside the approved access circle. A waived SAP narrows even some of the ordinary congressional reporting pathways, although DoD policy says waived SAPs remain subject to congressional oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

This structure also explains why black projects create so much public uncertainty. When a programme is acknowledged, outsiders may be able to discuss its broad existence without seeing its classified details. When it is unacknowledged, the absence of confirmation is not evidence that nothing exists; it may simply be part of the control system. At the same time, the SAP label itself is not proof of exotic technology or illegality. SAPs can protect acquisition, intelligence, and operations-and-support activity, including sensitive but comparatively routine work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/dod_saps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dod saps</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod saps</span></span></span>

## How Oversight Tries to Keep Secrecy from Becoming a Blank Cheque

SAPs are built to restrict knowledge, but they are not meant to operate without any internal or legislative oversight. Executive Order 13526 requires each agency head to maintain an accounting system for SAPs and subjects SAPs to oversight by the Information Security Oversight Office, although access may be limited to the ISOO Director and, in extraordinarily sensitive cases, only that Director.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">executive order classified national security information</span></span></span> The National Archives describes ISOO’s broader role as gathering data on agency classification programmes, analysing it and reporting annually to the President.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives ISOO Reports &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives ISOO Reports &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

Inside the Department of Defense, SAP governance is layered. The current DoD instruction describes a SAP Oversight Committee, Senior Review Group and SAP Senior Working Group. The SAP Oversight Committee is supposed to conduct an annual review of DoD SAPs to assess whether SAP security controls are still needed, including whether a SAP can be disestablished when extra protection is no longer warranted and whether waived status should continue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

Congressional oversight is also written into statute. Title 10, section 119 requires the Secretary of Defense to submit an annual report on SAPs to the defence committees, including the total amount requested for DoD SAPs and programme-level information such as descriptions, milestones, past costs and estimated future costs. It also requires notice and justification for each new SAP.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

The same law shows how secrecy and oversight are balanced uneasily rather than neatly. If a SAP’s classification is to change or information about it is to be [declassified]({{ 'declassified/' | relative_url }}) and made public, the Secretary of Defense must report the proposed change, reasons and planned public announcement to the defence committees in advance, except in exceptional circumstances. But the law also allows the Secretary to waive inclusion of certain information in reports if disclosure in that channel would adversely affect national security, with the information and justification instead provided to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span>

## What Congress Can See, and What It May Not

The public image of a black project is often a programme hidden from everyone. The formal system is more complicated. The statutory scheme assumes that Congress should be notified and that designated committees should receive sensitive information, but DoD procedures tightly control which members and staff may be briefed.

The DoD SAP instruction says members of Congress assigned to the defence and intelligence committees are authorised access to DoD SAPs within their committee’s oversight jurisdiction, except for waived programmes under narrower rules. For waived SAPs, only the chair, ranking member, staff directors and one designated security manager of the defence and intelligence committees are authorised access within the relevant jurisdiction, unless additional access is approved by the Secretary or Deputy Secretary of Defense.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

This arrangement creates a real democratic tension. On one hand, wide disclosure to hundreds of legislators and staff would defeat the purpose of a SAP. On the other, very narrow access can make it harder for Congress as an institution to challenge costs, duplication, legal risk, technical failure or policy drift. The 1997 Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy treated special access controls as part of the wider secrecy problem: they can be necessary for sensitive information, but they also intensify the need for disciplined oversight and limits.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on publicintelligence.net.</span></span></span>

The most practical way to understand this is not “no oversight” versus “full transparency”. SAPs occupy a middle ground: a small number of authorised officials may know enough to approve, fund, audit or challenge a programme, while the public and most officials remain outside the compartment.

## Where Public Knowledge Stops

Public knowledge usually stops at one of three barriers: the existence of the programme, the details of the capability, or the budget line that would reveal too much. An acknowledged SAP may cross the first barrier by admitting that a programme exists. It usually keeps the second barrier intact by withholding design features, performance, contractors, locations, vulnerabilities, operating assumptions or intelligence sources. An unacknowledged SAP keeps even the existence barrier closed for unauthorised audiences.

Budget visibility is especially limited. Title 10 requires classified reporting to the defence committees, including cost and milestone information for SAPs, but that does not mean the same information appears in public budget documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional oversight</span></span></span> Historical Congressional Research Service material on SAPs and the defence budget framed this as part of understanding the “[black budget]({{ 'black-budget/' | relative_url }})”: SAPs can sit inside lawful appropriations while the details needed for public scrutiny remain classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IB87201.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[EveryCRSReport]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on everycrsreport.com.</span></span></span>

That boundary is why SAPs often surface publicly only in fragments. A programme may be inferred from construction, aviation sightings, contract language, procurement anomalies, declassified histories, memoirs, congressional hints or later official acknowledgement. Those fragments can be valuable, but they are not the same as programme access. The SAP system is designed so that partial public signals do not automatically unlock the protected technical, operational or financial picture.

## Why SAPs Matter for Black Projects


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

SAPs matter because they turn secrecy from a label into a governance intervention. They decide not only that information is classified, but how many people may know it, how they are briefed, what they may discuss, where they may work, how Congress is informed, and when extra controls should end. In the world of black projects, that can be the difference between a hidden but accountable programme and a hidden programme whose costs, risks and assumptions are visible only to a very small circle.

</div>

The strongest case for SAPs is operational. Some military and intelligence capabilities can be neutralised if adversaries learn the design, target, sensor, signature, algorithm, access method or deployment pattern too early. The strongest concern is institutional. The same controls that protect a genuine breakthrough can also conceal cost growth, technical failure, duplication, weak legal analysis or over-classification. DoD’s own annual-review requirement recognises this risk by asking whether SAP protection remains necessary, whether sharing is possible, and whether disestablishment should occur when added protection is no longer warranted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS Enterprise Services Division]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The most accurate view of SAPs is therefore neither conspiratorial nor complacent. They are real, formal, rule-bound control systems for highly sensitive classified work. They can protect capabilities that would otherwise be compromised, but their legitimacy depends on the quality of the approval, access, accounting, congressional reporting and review mechanisms that surround them.
